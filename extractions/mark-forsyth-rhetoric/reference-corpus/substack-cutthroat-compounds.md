# Cutthroat Compounds

Source: https://markforsythauthor.substack.com/p/cutthroat-compounds
Author: Mark Forsyth
Published: 2026-06-30
Retrieved: 2026-08-06 (verbatim, unedited body text)

---

English is nice and simple, some of the time. But most of the time it is delightfully dotty. 

If you drink beer, you are a beer-drinker. If you eat meat, you are meat-eater. If you fancy dogs, you are a dog-fancier. It’s nice and simple: noun-verb + er. If you mong coster, you are a costermonger*. 

But once upon a time you could do it the other way around. If, for example, you were picking pockets in 1592 you could be called not a pocket-picker, but a pickpocket, with the verb going before the noun. 

The earliest record of the word from the Oxford English Dictionary is:

The picke-pockets and cut-purses are nothing so daungerous to meete withal, as these Coosening Cunny-catchers

Saying pickpocket rather than pocket-picker, or cut-purse instead of purse-cutter, is called a exocentric verb-noun compound, or, more simply, a cutthroat compound. Because somebody who cuts throats is not a throat-cutter, but a cutthroat.

Scenes like this are becoming all too common in London. I took this photograph last Thursday near St Paul’s.Cutthroat compounds were very popular once upon a time, especially around the 1590s, which was their day of hey. And the language is full of them. There are tell-tales and turncoats, scarecrows and scapegraces, dreadnoughts and daredevils, catch-farts and lickspittles, which is somebody who licks his boss’s spittle.

They are almost always describing people, and specifically people that you don’t like. A chap who spoils your sport is no fun, and is a spoilsport. Somebody with a terribly bad temper who spend his time spitting fire, is a spitfire. 

They can describe things: rotgut whisky is a cutthroat, as is tanglefoot. And very occasionally they can be positive. When you break your fast with bacon and eggs, that is not a fast-breaker, but a beautiful breakfast. 

However, usually the cutthroats are reserved for the know-nothing, pinchpennny, killjoys of this world. 

The odd thing about cutthroat compounds is that there are no new ones. Of the examples above, the latest one is tangle-foot from 1859. Almost nothing from the twentieth century has caught on. Scofflaw was invented during American Prohibition to describe people who laughed all the way to the speak-easy (not a cutthroat), but after that they peter out and vanish away. 

Kickass doesn’t really count, as it’s almost always an adjective, rather than being a person who kicks asses. The habit survives a little in the names of some children’s characters, like Dudley Do-Right. George R.R. Martin filled his books with sell-swords (although he didn’t invent the term); but other than that they’re gone. 

I have been trying, in my idle moments, which are legion, to invent some modern ones that work. Obviously you can come up with smokevape or gazephone, but they don’t seem… natural. Try as I might, I can’t imagine them catching on.

The best two I have been able to manage are:

Flex-muscle - for one of those chaps who is always maxing their looks. 

And:

Tweet-twaddle - which I should hope is self-explanatory. 

If you can think of anything better, feel free to be a leave-comment.
