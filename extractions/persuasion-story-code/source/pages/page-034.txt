At this point, you have the raw materials for your story in your notes.
Put your Discovery Story together like this:
1. What were you doing and what was life like before you made
your discovery—and what was your one big frustration? Write
factually, but include images and your feelings of frustration so
your reader or listener will understand where you were at.
2. What was the discovery you made?
3. What changed for the better as a result?
Besides being a ready-made answer to the inevitable question, "Huh.
How did you come up with that idea?"
, a Discovery Story adds weight
and meaning to something important you found. It also adds value,
which is handy if your discovery is the same thing as what you're selling.

FAILURE-SUCCESS STORY
This is a very classic kind of origin story. It works particularly well when
a) it is true, and
b) your market is made up of people who have sincerely struggled to
learn a skill or attain a goal for a long time, but have repeatedly failed—
although they haven't given up entirely.
Making money and getting rich certainly fall in that category of skills
and goals. Entrepreneur, author and copywriter Joe Karbo released a
series of full-page newspaper ads in the early 1980s that began with this
story. Even today, you've got to admit, it's pretty compelling:
You think you've got problems?
Well, I remember when a bank turned me down for a $200 loan.
