The stories that journalists write begin with facts. Learning to find and
include facts in a story is a major skill they learn, and a significant factor
in making journalistic stories true, as well as believable.
When it comes to persuasion stories, I've noticed four major problems
that keep them from being believable: Inconsistency, vagueness,
exaggeration and not being conversational.
Let's take a look at these mistakes in a persuasion story.
FOUR WAYS TO RUIN A PERSUASION STORY'S BELIEVABILITY
In the last chapter, we looked at an expert testimonial from a session
guitarist. The testimonial was for a bonus included in that hypothetical
home-study course, Guitarist on Steroids. Now, let's look at a Future
Benefit Story about that bonus. First, the story will be told in a believable
way. After that, we'll seriously weaken the story in four different ways—
each one severely damaging the story's believability.
Here's the story presented believably:
One more thing. Besides vastly improving your chops and making
yourself a top pick when anyone with a band is looking for a great
guitar player...
... you will also learn our secret method for being able to play any
song within half an hour after you hear it! You've probably heard
of musicians who can do this. And you assumed it was something
you were born with, or you would never have it.
Well, we've figured out a way to create the ear-mind-muscle
connection that has worked for everyone who's taken the five
simple steps we recommend. Suddenly, people assume they are
music geniuses. But it's their secret that they are simply students
of Guitarist on Steroids!
OK. If you're a guitar player, you know this is desirable. Learning new
songs can be a real drag. Knowing you'll be able to do this in 30 minutes
might be enough by itself to close the sale on this hypothetical course.
