When you think about it, there's hardly a better way than the one he
used, to spell out the markers of higher status-without actually using the
words "higher status." And surely that story within the ad is one of the
main reasons it has been so successful.
Notice how the story doesn't proclaim, "If you pronounce words like an
uneducated lower-class person does, you'll never get a good-paying job
—but now, with this course, you will." It doesn't say, "Immigrants with
poor language skills will never get their children into good colleges-but
that's all about to change, once you take this course."
Those indeed may have been the hopes of those reading the ad.
Sackheim was clever in the way he finessed these awkward issues in his
story. Issues about social status can be sore spots for a lot of people, and
this story threads the needle admirably to make its point without
creating hostility and distrust in the reader.
Tips For Improved Status Stories
Hinting works here. In Sackheim's case, people already know that being
marked "as an educated person" opens doors for them that previously
would have been bolted closed. They don't need to have it spelled out for
them. So the story promises improved status in a way that does not
create resistance, and that the reader can accept.
If you have a product or service that can improve your prospect's status,
it's often best to promise this indirectly rather than too blatantly and
bluntly. Again, notice Sackheim didn't say, "You'll be seen as a member
of the upper classes of society" or anything quite that direct. It's more
effective and believable when you allude to the improved status, rather
than hit the prospect over the head with it!
