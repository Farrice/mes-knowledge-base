## Genius Patterns

### Pattern 1: Copy = Behavior Engineering (not writing)
**Unconscious behavior**: Sam never frames copy as "words." It's "understanding what people want and how to get them to do what you want them to do… then figure out which words are needed." He even ran AIDA to land a date with his wife.
**Executable**: Before writing a word, define the single behavior you want the reader to take. Reverse-engineer the words from the action, not the action from the words.
**Deploy when**: Any persuasive piece. **Metric**: Can you name the one action this copy is engineered to trigger?

### Pattern 2: AIDA as the universal spine
**Unconscious behavior**: Attention → Interest → Desire → Action structures everything — ads, flirting, content. It's his default skeleton.
**Executable**: Draft every piece as A-I-D-A beats. Attention = the punch. Interest = the relevant problem named. Desire = the mechanism/transformation. Action = the ask (often buried late).
**Deploy when**: Long-form ads, VSLs, emails, story posts.

### Pattern 3: Mind the Gap (curiosity gap / open loop)
**Unconscious behavior**: A headline opens a tension the reader *must* resolve. "They laughed when I sat down at the piano — but then I started to play." Humans can't tolerate an unresolved cliffhanger.
**Executable**: Open with a statement that creates an information gap, then withhold the resolution until they've read on. Short-form: **stack a new open loop every ~8 seconds** (mapped to retention drop-off points).
**Deploy when**: Every hook, headline, reel script. **Metric**: Does the first line create a question the reader needs answered?

### Pattern 4: The Slippery Slope (Sugarman) + Rule of Consistency
**Unconscious behavior**: "The purpose of the first sentence is to get you to read the second. The second, to read the third." Built on the consistency principle — a small yes makes the next yes likelier (the yard-sign study: +40% with a prior micro-commitment).
**Executable**: Engineer momentum sentence-to-sentence. Plant an early micro-yes (a head-nod line) so the reader is already agreeing before the ask. Goal is NOT 100% buying — it's getting the *right* reader to consume the whole thing.
**Deploy when**: Long-form sales copy, email, VSL.

### Pattern 5: Punch in the Face (first sentence)
**Unconscious behavior**: "The first sentence needs to punch you." Often breaks the fourth wall: *"You're reading this not because you want to, but because I want you to."*
**Executable**: Make sentence one a fist — confrontation, a bold claim, a fourth-wall break, or a visceral image. Never warm up.
**Deploy when**: Every opener. **Metric**: Would a stranger stop scrolling on the first line alone?

### Pattern 6: "Long converts — there's no such thing as too long, only too boring"
**Unconscious behavior**: Contrarian conviction that long-form and "ugly" almost always outconvert short/pretty — the real failure is *boring*, not *length*. (copythat.com: 2,000 words, white background, ~5% conversion. 27-min VSLs printing money.)
**Executable**: Never cut for length; cut for boredom. If something's not working, the diagnosis is "not interesting enough," never "too long."
**Deploy when**: Anyone says "no one will read this, it's too long." **Metric**: Is every paragraph interesting, or just present?

### Pattern 7: Niches Make Riches (targeting via headline)
**Unconscious behavior**: Call out the specific person to win the click. "50 reasons you know you're from Denver" → 30% of Denverites click. Homesick candles (smell of your home state). Hampton: "If you make over $3M/year, I know exactly how you feel."
**Executable**: Name the exact reader in the headline. Specificity of *who* beats breadth of *how many*.
**Deploy when**: Headlines, ad targeting, subject lines.

### Pattern 8: Story is the Conversion Engine — customer is HERO, brand is GUIDE
**Unconscious behavior**: "Story, story, story, story." Every sellable thing wraps in a story with beginning/middle/end and tension. The brand positions as the *guide*; the customer is the *hero*. WSJ "Tale of Two Boys" ran 28 years → $2B.
**Executable**: Build the ad as hero's journey — reader is lost → encounters the guide (your product) → overcomes → returns transformed. Bury the product as the guide, not the hero.
**Deploy when**: Any ad/email/landing page selling a transformation. **Stacks with**: Donald Miller (StoryBrand), Luke Iha (VSL).

### Pattern 9: Rhythm — write like music
**Unconscious behavior**: "Short. Some sentences a little longer. And some sentences keep going because they're setting up what's next." Short-medium-long-short. One point per sentence. Use a period where you'd use a comma. Start sentences with "and"/"but." 7th-grade reading level (Hemingway app; NYT=7th, USA Today=4th). Buffett's avg sentence = 17 words (down from 25 in his youth).
**Executable**: Vary sentence length deliberately for cadence. Break compound sentences into staccato. Run copy through hemingwayapp.com; target 7th grade.
**Deploy when**: Every draft's edit pass. **Metric**: Read aloud — does it have a beat, or does it drone?

### Pattern 10: Cut a Third / Write Drunk, Edit Sober / Kill Your Darlings
**Unconscious behavior**: Write the crap to get it out, then cut a third — and a third again. "Write with your eraser." Ogilvy: "I'm a shitty writer. I'm a great editor." Constraint forces clarity (Hemingway: "The man was sad. He wanted to go fish.").
**Executable**: First draft fast and ugly. Then delete ~33% — clauses, hedges, alternate phrasings — until only the point survives.
**Deploy when**: Every second draft. **Metric**: Did you cut at least a third without losing meaning?

### Pattern 11: The Tangibility Engine (make the abstract physical)
**Unconscious behavior**: Converts every claim into a physical object the reader can see. "Imagine storing your food in the toilet bowl" (Tupperware E. coli). A brick of fat = movie popcorn. The deconstructed Coke bottle = 40% sugar. "Would you microwave your kid's milk in melting plastic? That's polyester pajamas in the dryer."
**Executable**: Take the dry stat/benefit → ask "what physical object makes this visceral?" → write the image, not the number. Comparison to a disgusting/vivid known object beats abstraction.
**Deploy when**: Any data point, benefit, or risk. **Metric**: Can the reader *see* it? **Stacks with**: Harry Dry (zoom-in).

### Pattern 12: Bury the Product (Trojan Horse) + Absolve Guilt
**Unconscious behavior**: Build desire through story FIRST; reveal the product late. The "failed solution" move absolves the reader: "It's not your fault — you just didn't have the right [mechanism]." Bad ads "throw the product at you immediately."
**Executable**: Delay the offer. Open with problem + story + desire. Insert the "not your fault, you were missing X" beat. Reveal product as the missing mechanism, then stack social proof.
**Deploy when**: Direct-response ads, especially health/finance/transformation.

### Pattern 13: Steal & Twist 3% (the signature ethos)
**Unconscious behavior**: Merciless copying of classics, twisted ~3% to make them his. The Hustle's "Trends" ripped off WSJ's "Tale of Two Boys" → made a lot of money. "I was pretty mercilessly copying it and it crushed it."
**Executable**: Pull a proven classic (ad, structure, phrase). Change the subject/voice ~3% to fit your brand. Deploy. Originality is remix. **Bridges to his taste skill** (`/taste-copy`, copy work).
**Deploy when**: Stuck on a blank page, or want a proven skeleton fast.

### Pattern 14: Objection Handling — the 8 Mile move
**Unconscious behavior**: Name your own flaws before the reader can (Eminem freestyle: "Yeah I'm white trash, my mom…" → opponent has nothing left). Or handle indirectly via anecdote: "I wore them in the shower" (never says "waterproof"). FAQs as objection containers.
**Executable**: List the reader's top 3 objections. Voice them first ("I know what you're thinking…"). Defuse the biggest one with a concrete personal anecdote rather than a claim.
**Deploy when**: Mid-to-late ad copy, sales pages, FAQs.

### Pattern 15: Short-Form Retention Engineering + Frame-Breaking
**Unconscious behavior**: Reads the retention graph; wherever people drop off, inserts a curiosity gap. Tracks 3-second stop rate and skip rate. The onion-dad video: raw, no script, 4M views, 80% 3-sec stop rate, 85% retention at 3s, ~50% watched to end — then atomized into a Hampton ad. Frame-breaking = defies expectations ($10M onion business = one guy, two phones, a calculator, a pack-and-play daycare).
**Executable**: Script reels with a punch hook, open loops every ~8s, and a frame-breaking premise (the surprising truth). After publishing, read the retention graph and re-cut at drop-off points. Repurpose winners into ads.
**Deploy when**: Short-form video, Parallax atomization, LinkedIn video. **Stacks with**: Lara Acosta (hooks), Runia (story).

## Hidden Knowledge

- **The first sentence's ONLY job is the second sentence.** Architecture is recursive, not topical. Stop optimizing sentences for meaning; optimize them for "keep reading."
- **The product must be buried.** Desire precedes the offer. Bad copy leads with the product; great copy makes you *want* before it tells you *what*.
- **"Long is bad" is a category error.** The real variable is *interesting*. People misdiagnose boring copy as "too long" and cut the wrong thing.
- **Headlines = 80% of the spend** (Ogilvy: 5× more read the headline than the body) — yet most people write the headline last. Invert it.
- **One ad can be worth a company.** The Hustle's "My friends think I'm smart. I'm not. (I just read The Hustle.)" ad with the Ben Franklin bill was "singularly responsible for the exit." Copy compounds at enterprise-value scale.
- **⚠️ Sam improvises with FABRICATED stats to demonstrate STRUCTURE.** He repeatedly says "everything I just said was fake — I don't know if it's true." The teachable thing is the *architecture* (AIDA + absolve-guilt + proof). **GUARDRAIL: in real deployment, every stat must be verified (Chain Step 5.5). Structure from Sam; facts from research.** This is non-negotiable in Farrice's system.
- **Cross-pollination is an unfair advantage.** Swiping from Thrasher / Japanese Popeye / JFK Jr. magazines works *because* "tech people don't read these." Source ideas where your competitors aren't looking. Read fiction to write better ads.

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Exemplar 1: The Tale of Two Boys (Wall Street Journal)
- **Context**: The longest-running ad in WSJ history; Sam's gold standard for story-selling.
- **The Example**: "On a beautiful spring afternoon 25 years ago, two young men graduated from the same college. They were very much alike… Both were better-than-average students, both personable, both filled with ambitious dreams. Recently these men returned to their 25th reunion. They were still very much alike… but they were different." One subscribed to the WSJ; one didn't. Ran **28 years**, drove **$2B+**.
- **Why it's excellent**: Pure hero's journey — two identical heroes, one finds the guide (WSJ), returns transformed. Tension via the parallel. The product is the guide, never the hero. Sam *stole and twisted 3%* of it for Trends and it crushed.

### Exemplar 2: The Live AG1/AGZ Sleep-Supplement Rewrite
- **Context**: Host shows a weak ad — "Nightly Rest is within arm's reach. AGZ combines magnesium, l-theanine, and saffron…" Sam rewrites it on the spot.
- **The Example** (AIDA + absolve-guilt + Trojan horse): *"There's a scientific reason why, after the age of 35, 75% of men wake up between 2 and 3am."* (Attention) → *"You know the feeling — you wake up unsure if you need to pee… and for five years you've just assumed it's age."* (Interest) → *"The truth is, you feel this way because you lack vitamin D — notice it's worse in winter? It's not your fault."* (Desire + absolve guilt) → *"The good news: it's easy to fix… we spent 10 years and $100M sourcing the best. Trusted by 100,000 dads just like you."* (Action + social proof).
- **Why it's excellent**: Demonstrates the full spine in 30 seconds — punch hook, niche call-out, yes-ladder, failed-solution absolution, buried product, social proof. **Note Sam's own caveat: "everything I just said was fake."** The *structure* is the lesson; the facts must be verified before deployment.

### Exemplar 3: The Caraway / Tupperware Tangibility Rewrite
- **Context**: Weak ad — "Designed with clean materials… food storage helps you store more and stress less. Your storage can do better."
- **The Example**: *"Imagine storing your food in the toilet bowl. Because that's exactly what you're doing. There's more E. coli in a one-year-old plastic Tupperware container than in your toilet bowl."* → then the glass-is-better turn.
- **Why it's excellent**: The Tangibility Engine in its purest form — an abstract hygiene claim becomes a visceral, unforgettable physical image. Punches in the first sentence. You can *see* it.

### Anti-Exemplar: The Product-First Ad
- **What mediocre looks like**: "Nightly Rest is within arm's reach. A daily restorative drink for sleep." / "Your storage can do better." Two sentences, benefits shoved in your face, product named immediately, zero story, zero desire built, no reason to believe.
- **Why it fails**: Violates Bury-the-Product (Pattern 12) and Story (Pattern 8). Skips desire entirely. No open loop, no yes-ladder, no tangibility. "They haven't built any reason to actually believe."

## Signature Moves

- **Open with a punch / break the fourth wall** — first sentence grabs the collar; often addresses the reader directly. → **Deploy when**: Every hook and headline.
- **Bury the product (Trojan horse)** — build desire through story; reveal the offer late. → **Deploy when**: Any DR ad selling a transformation.
- **Make it physical** — convert any claim/stat into a visible object (toilet bowl, brick of fat, deconstructed Coke). → **Deploy when**: Any data point or benefit lands flat.
- **Steal & twist 3%** — pull a proven classic, change it slightly, deploy. → **Deploy when**: Blank page, or you want a proven skeleton.
- **Cut a third** — write ugly, then delete 33% to find the point. → **Deploy when**: Every second draft.
- **Name the objection before the reader** (8 Mile) — voice their doubt first, defuse with anecdote. → **Deploy when**: Mid/late ad copy, sales pages.
- **Plant an early yes** — a head-nod line in the first beats to start the consistency ladder. → **Deploy when**: Long-form, VSL openings.

## Expert-Specific Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Score 4 (Acceptable) | Score 7 (Good) | Score 10 (Savant) |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Hook punch** | Opens with context/warm-up | First line creates mild interest | First line stops a stranger cold — punch, fourth-wall break, or visceral image |
| **Curiosity gap / open loops** | One vague question implied | A clear gap opened and resolved | Gap opened in line 1; in short-form, a fresh loop every ~8s mapped to drop-off |
| **Desire-before-offer (Trojan horse)** | Product named in first third | Some desire built before the ask | Product fully buried; reader *wants* it before they know what it is |
| **Tangibility** | Claims stated as benefits | One concrete example used | Every key claim rendered as a physical, visceral, unforgettable image |
| **Rhythm** | Uniform sentence length | Some variation, mostly readable | Short-medium-long-short cadence; 7th-grade level; reads like music aloud |
| **Story structure** | A fact list, no narrative | A loose anecdote present | Full hero's-journey with tension; customer=hero, brand=guide |
| **Objection handling** | Objections ignored | One addressed directly | Top objections voiced before the reader, defused via anecdote/FAQ |
| **Editing discipline** | First draft shipped | Some tightening | Cut a third+; every word earns its place (Buffett-grade concision) |
| **Targeting specificity** | "Anyone who…" breadth | A segment named | The exact reader called out by identity/income/situation (niche = riches) |
