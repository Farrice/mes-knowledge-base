# Your Buyer Quit Reading Labels In 2015 — Contrarian Sales Argument (Email, v2)

## Argument Brief

**Objective (SMART):** In this email I want to convince supplement and performance-brand founders that answering the buyer's category-level disbelief on the first screen is better for accomplishing profitable growth than buying more traffic against the page they already have. To do this I will hang the argument on one external checkable artifact (the 2015 New York AG supplement investigation), corroborate with mined buyer language, name the second-order damage, and appeal to sunk-cost frustration and founder pride in the product.

**Contention:** A supplement brand's flat month is almost never a traffic problem — the page argues features at a buyer who convicted the entire category years ago, so every new dollar of traffic just routes more people through the same disbelief.

**Precondition 1 — perceived choice:** Yes. The reader owns the page, the copy, and the media budget. Nothing structural stops him from rewriting the first screen this week. No identity sub-argument required.

**Precondition 2 — meaningful stakes:** Real and immediate. Spend goes out weekly against a page answering an unasked question; the deeper stake is spending years and the last of the runway reformulating a product that was never the broken part.

**Format route:** Email — Even-If open, Attention/Acknowledgement/Aggravate compressed, proof deferred to the call, ONE CTA.

**False belief to forgive:** "If it isn't converting, the formula's wrong / I'm bad at this." Reframed as inherited: the features argument came with the category.

**Mode:** Change belief (diagnosis swap), not reaffirm.

**Borrowed-authority object:** The February 2015 New York Attorney General DNA-testing investigation into store-brand herbal supplements at Walmart, Walgreens, Target and GNC — cease-and-desist letters to all four chains. External, independently checkable, and *not* self-sourced. Mined buyer language rides behind it as corroboration.

**Subject line:** Your buyer quit reading labels in 2015

## The Piece (email body — 495 words)

**[Attention]** Somewhere between the third tub that did nothing and the second bottle that did less, your customer stopped being a shopper and became a juror. Quietly. No review, no refund request. She just decided about the whole aisle — and then kept buying anyway, cheaper each time, with a little less hope in it.

**[Acknowledgement]** Even if you tested that page nine ways last quarter, the flat month still reads like a traffic problem. It always does. Fresher creative, a new angle, a cheaper click. That's the honest first read, and every dashboard you own confirms it.

**[Aggravate]** But she isn't weighing you against a competitor. She's weighing you against the tub that did nothing. *"How do I know this isn't just another overpriced powder."* That's the sentence your first screen has to survive. Instead it's naming grams.

**[Authority]** Look up February 2015. Or don't — you probably remember the headlines even if you've forgotten why. New York's Attorney General had DNA tests run on store-brand herbal supplements from Walmart, Walgreens, Target and GNC, and most of what they tested came back without the herb on the label. Rice. Houseplant. Wild carrot. Cease-and-desist letters went out to all four chains. The industry pushed back hard on the method, and I think they had a fair point about what extraction does to DNA, but the story had already run everywhere and done the damage. That was the week "supplements are all the same" stopped being a suspicion and became something everybody just knows. She didn't stop trusting you. *"I stopped trusting labels a long time ago"* — years before you printed one.

**[Angst]** Here's the problem behind the problem. Traffic doesn't dilute disbelief, it routes more people through it, faster, at a worse CPM. So the number stays flat, and a good founder eventually reaches the only conclusion left: the formula's wrong. Reformulate. Repackage. Another two years and the last of the runway spent fixing the one thing that was never broken — and you'd come out the far end older, out of money, and still right about the product. That isn't carelessness. You didn't choose the features argument. It came with the category.

**[Ambiguity]** The fix isn't louder. It's earlier. Pull your last fifty reviews and your competitor's one-stars, and find the sentences that are accusations instead of requests. *"I've wasted so much money on pills that did nothing"* is an accusation. Answer the loudest one above the fold, before a single ingredient gets named. Because it isn't a shelf of competitors you're up against. It's a record of everyone who let her down before you got there.

**[Appeal]** Four teardowns a month, free, because I run them live. Thirty minutes, your page on my screen, and I name the disbelief your first screen isn't answering. Can't name it inside fifteen, I hand you the rest of the time back.

Imagine what changes when she believes you before she reads the panel.

Reply with your URL and I'll send a time.

## Staged FAQ (validate → answer → reframe)

**"My conversion rate is fine. This is a scaling question, not a copy question."**
Validate: at your current spend it probably is fine — the page was built for people already searching for you. · Answer: the tell isn't the rate, it's what the rate does when you widen the audience; cold buyers arrive already convicted, warm ones don't. · Reframe: a page that answers disbelief converts warm traffic just as well and stops taxing every new channel you open.

**"That AG story was a decade ago and the methodology was junk."**
Validate: the DNA-barcoding criticism was legitimate, and I said so in the email. · Answer: the argument doesn't rest on whether those products were actually adulterated — it rests on what the coverage did to buyer belief, which happened regardless of who was right. · Reframe: you're not defending an industry, you're inheriting its reputation, and the page is where you either address that or don't.

**"We've already tested headlines. A lot of them."**
Validate: most brands in this category have, and some of the winners were real. · Answer: headline tests move which feature leads; they don't change what the page is arguing. Nine variants of a features argument is still one argument. · Reframe: change the argument once and every future test runs on better ground.

**"Reviews are noise — angry people and freebie hunters."**
Validate: plenty of them are, and sorting by recency hands you exactly that. · Answer: sort by most-helpful votes instead — those are the reviews other buyers voted useful, which makes them language buyers have already agreed with. · Reframe: you're not collecting complaints, you're collecting pre-validated objections in the buyer's own words.

**"You're a marketer telling me marketing is the problem. Convenient."**
Validate: it is convenient, and you should discount it accordingly. · Answer: which is why the teardown is free and diagnostic — I name the disbelief, you decide whether it's real; you know your product better than I ever will. · Reframe: if the page turns out to be fine, you've ruled out the cheapest possible fix in thirty minutes before touching the formula.

**"Our formula genuinely is different. We're not another overpriced powder."**
Validate: I believe you, and that's exactly why this stings. · Answer: she doesn't disbelieve your dose, she disbelieves the category, and she got there before your ad ever loaded. · Reframe: differentiation only pays after credibility — answer the accusation first and your formulation finally gets read.
