## 2. METHODOLOGY — decoded in full

### 2.1 The "Do The Math" Worth-Justification Method (p.305–306)
Purpose: justify premium/equity pricing to a skeptical service-seller by quantifying the buyer's upside instead of pricing the deliverable.
Mechanics:
1. Take a real (or realistic, past-client-derived) baseline: e.g., client currently does $1M/year.
2. Project the tangible outcome your service could produce: doubling to $2M/year = "$1M in growth."
3. Reframe your ask: you're not selling "a website" — you're selling a share of $1M in growth.
4. Apply a modest gross-share benchmark (Vosler uses 10% of *just the growth*, not total revenue) → $100,000 "worth" to you.
5. If positioned as an equity/profit-share deal from the outset, this $100K figure becomes your literal entitled payout (give or take gross vs. profit basis).
6. Close with a rhetorical framing question to the reader: "Would you be comfortable charging $20,000+ if you knew it had a $1M upside for the customer?" — used to make a specific fee (in this case $20K) feel trivial next to the computed upside.
Conditions/caveats stated explicitly: results aren't guaranteed; the math depends on "the right kind of strategy" and being good at what you do; use PAST CLIENT RESULTS to make the projection realistic, not invented numbers.
Three downstream benefits Vosler lists from this pricing shift: (1) client becomes an engaged "partner" who moves mountains to help hit the goal because they understand the stakes; (2) you can charge far more than competitors because you're no longer competing on "solutions," you're the only one selling "growth"; (3) because you're paid more per client, you need fewer clients and get your time back.

### 2.2 "Coal" Calling — Zen Client Identification & Closing (p.307–309)
Core reframe: "Sell the right thing to the right person and you won't have to sell at all." Vosler explicitly disclaims being a "pitchman" — his close rate comes from targeting, not persuasion skill.
Method steps (decoded):
1. **Don't wait for inbound.** Proactively identify the exact kind of client who could benefit from your service — and benefit *enough* to justify premium/equity fees. This is the qualifying filter, not a demographic profile.
2. **Apply the "math makes sense" test** before pursuing any lead: if your service (marketing, sales, accounting, etc.) can't move the needle enough for the growth-math from 2.1 to work, it's not a good-fit lead "right out the gate." Example given: easier to justify $20K to help a $1M company reach $1.2M than to double a $5K/month company — the absolute-dollar delta must be big enough.
3. **Position, don't pitch.** The "right thing" (your offer, reframed as growth/outcome, per Part 3 methodology) must be clearly understood by you first, then positioned to the specific target — this positioning work happens *before* the sales conversation, not during it.
4. Six diagnostic questions Vosler says you must answer to execute this (used as a checklist for readers building their own targeting system): Where do I find potential clients who need my help to grow? Should I do inbound or systematically hunt specific client types? How do I optimize my offer/positioning to sell "growth"? How do I position without sounding salesy? Can I structure equity deals within my existing business model? 
5. Recap (4-point): (I) redefine your worth via positioning: (II) sell only to clients where you've pre-identified you can help them grow, and pursue ONLY those; (III) don't just sell a service — position as a partner and deliver; (IV) selling = building trust + honesty about what the lead actually needs, not just selling what they want.
When to use: freelancers/agency owners/coaches stuck in "feast or famine" — juggling sales and delivery with no time to breathe. When NOT to use (implied): if your offer genuinely can't move the needle on a prospect's growth math, don't pursue them regardless of how eager they seem.

### 2.3 Reverse Product Development Strategy (p.326–330, expanded in Part One–Five)
Central question posed: "Can the principles that create great copy be applied to developing & creating great products? If so, how?"
Diagnosis of the common failure mode: entrepreneurs start with a solution/idea ("I think people would love it if they could do X faster") and build with little market research, assuming "the idea's so great it'll sell itself" — a Jurassic-Park "could vs. should" trap (explicit Jeff Goldblum reference).
The corrective principle: **write the marketing messaging BEFORE building the product.** By mapping out, writing, and rewriting the marketing copy for a hypothetical product first, you surface: (a) where you need more features/benefits, (b) who the product is actually for, (c) more effective stories and deeper empathy for the end user. The four-icon visual sequence on p.327 literalizes this as a loop: identify a problem → map out a solution → develop your message → optimize your solution → test your messaging (a five-stage cycle, message-development sits before/alongside solution optimization, not after).
Explicit caveat/floor: "great marketing applied to a crappy product is just adding saffron to a turd" — the method assumes product quality is a hard floor, not something copy can rescue.
Scope note: Vosler's personal experience is in "information products" (books, courses, workshops, consulting) so that's the sharpened focus of this section, but he states the principles transfer to physical products, SaaS, and B2B.
Stated non-tangible benefits of adding an info-product arm to ANY business: enhanced credibility, new entry points for sales funnels, and (least tangible but most important) confidence in your own abilities/understanding.

### 2.4 The Dung Beetle Parable — "Strategy Beats Advantages" (p.331–333)
This is Vosler's central strategic-thinking framework for the info-product section, taught entirely through a biological analogy (used later as a teaching template — see Voice & Teaching DNA).
Mechanics of the parable:
- The horned dung beetle is (pound for pound) the strongest creature on Earth (can lift 1,141x its body weight — equivalent of a human lifting six double-decker buses).
- Two male morphs exist in this species: "Chad" (big, horned, fights rivals for females) and "Winston" (small, hornless, uses a covert tunneling strategy to mate with Chad's females while Chad is busy fighting).
- Trade-off mechanism: the bigger the horn, the smaller the testicles — Chad invests resources in weaponry/fighting, Winston invests in "substantially larger testicles," increasing his fertilization odds per encounter even without direct competition access.
- Outcome: Winston frequently out-reproduces Chad despite zero raw strength advantage, because his strategy exploits Chad's blind spot rather than competing on Chad's terms.
Explicit lesson translated to business: "The Right Strategy Can Beat The Biggest Advantages." If you're not a "Chad" (i.e., don't have the capital/audience/experience advantage), don't try to out-Chad Chad — "embrace your inner Winston" and find an asymmetric approach.
Named resource axes for launching an info-product, explicitly framed as substitutable: financial capital, distribution/audience access, OR time & experience. The operating principle: "match the right strategy with the resources we have."
Named cautionary counter-examples (big-advantage, poor-strategy failures): Facebook Phone, Amazon Fire Phone, Blackberry, Google+, Google Glass, Microsoft Zune.
Closing frame device: a Calvin Coolidge persistence quote, re-tagged by Vosler as "persistence is an effective (say it with me now) strategy" — deliberately recasting a famous quote to fit the chapter's thesis word.

### 2.5 The Info-Product Opportunity Framework — 3 Questions (p.334–341)
Structural device: the whole Section Four/Part Two–Four is organized as answers to 3 explicit questions, stated up front (p.330) and revisited as section headers:
- **Q1 (Insight Is Power, p.334–336):** What opportunities exist for those breaking into info-products? Answer: information is abundant but unstructured; demand now is for people who can *distill* knowledge into structure/action, not for raw information itself. Sub-argument: the "$1,000,000 = 100,000 sales @ $10, or 10,000 @ $100, or 1,000 @ $1,000, or 100 @ $10,000" style infographic math is technically true but misleading — it hides just how hard customer acquisition at volume actually is. Vosler redirects readers to think in **monthly income targets**, not lump-sum fantasy numbers, and offers a reframe: "80 sales per week... can translate to $20,000+ per month" — more attainable-feeling than "10,000 sales."
- **Q2 (The Who, p.337–339):** Who should/shouldn't build an info-product? Framed via a producer-vs-consumer dichotomy (Cal Newport "Deep Work" quote, Winston Churchill "we make a living by what we get, we make a life by what we give"). Five explicit qualifying characteristics (see Named Artifacts below). Explicit ethical disclaimer: NOT everyone should do this — most "have the ability" but won't put in the effort or will let life get in the way. Two FAQ: (1) Do you need in-depth expertise first? No — the strategy is designed to build your expertise *as you execute it*, not as a prerequisite. (2) What if you don't enjoy studying/organizing info? Normal (99.999% of people) — "enjoyment is DERIVED from DOING, not the other way around... you don't love it first then do it."
- **Q3 (The End Game, p.340+):** What does it take to launch successfully in 2020+, and why do most entrepreneurs get it wrong? Answered via the Value Ascension Strategy (2.6 below).

### 2.6 THE VALUE ASCENSION STRATEGY — full named framework (p.340–346)
This is the flagship named methodology of this page range. Full decode:

**Foundational structure — Two-Product Model** (p.340):
- **Info Product #1 — "Step by Step Tactical Guide."** Solves ONE specific narrow problem. Delivered as book/mini-course. Price range: $24–$49.
- **Info Product #2 — "Time Framed Strategic Guide."** Solves multiple related problems AND presents a path to a bigger transformation ("something 'new' for the buyer"). Delivered as a step-by-step video course. Price range: $199–$499.
- The pairing is explicitly an ascension/upsell pair: Product #1 is the low-friction entry, Product #2 is the backend upsell to buyers of #1.

**Key Information block** (p.341, framed like a mini project brief):
- OBJECTIVE: launch a sustainable info product that solves specific buyer problems effectively.
- GOAL: reach $20K/month in sales within 30 days.
- STRATEGY: the 4-step Compass/Map/Vessel/Putting-It-Together sequence (below).
- MEASURE: hit 80 sales/month minimum of the core (Product #1) with a 20% conversion (take rate) on the upsell (Product #2).

**The $20K/mo math table** (exact figures reproduced faithfully as a decoded table — this is a literal reusable spreadsheet model):
| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Visitors (weekly) | 1,000 |
| P1 price | $24 |
| P1 conversion | 8.00% |
| P1 units sold | 80 |
| P1 revenue | $1,920 |
| P1 sales feeding P2 | 80 |
| P2 price | $199 |
| P2 conversion (of P1 buyers) | 20.00% |
| P2 units sold | 16 |
| P2 revenue | $3,184 |
| **Revenue/week** | **$5,104** |
| **Monthly revenue @ 1,000 visits/week** | **$20,416** |
| % of total sales from P1 | 62.38% |
| % of total sales from P2 | 37.62% |

Executable takeaway: to replicate this, you need ~1,000 weekly visitors to a funnel, an 8% front-end conversion rate on a sub-$25 tactical product, and a 20% take-rate on a ~$200 strategic upsell to those same buyers. Reality-check caveat explicitly stated: the time/experience/effort needed to hit these conversion numbers is not assumed — it's something you build *alongside* the product (echoing the Q2 FAQ answer above). Scalability point: info-products scale cheaply because unit costs stay low as customer service scales, unlike labor-heavy service models.

**The 4 Sequential Steps of the Value Ascension Strategy** (a nautical-metaphor framework, pp.343–346):

- **Step 1 — "Your Compass": Picking A Direction.** Definition: your compass = "your unflinching commitment to quality." Function: directs you through the inevitable storms of building something valuable; without it you're "easily blown off course, distracted by shiny objects." Mechanic: you refine the compass AS you build (not a one-time-set-and-forget), while explicitly avoiding the trap of perfectionism. This is Step 1 because everything downstream needs a fixed quality standard to measure against.

- **Step 2 — "Your Map": Charting Our Course.** Definition: a *working list* of assets to create, standards to measure quality against, and faster ways of doing things. Framed as "the TACTICAL approach to executing your strategy." Key property: the map is reusable and self-improving — "it can be used over and over again; getting better and more refined after each use." Map covers three domains that "tie together": (1) business model, (2) product structure/assets, (3) marketing plans. Two ways to build it: slower (experimentation) or faster (adapt an already-working strategy — implying readers should start by copying Vosler's structure, not inventing from scratch).

- **Step 3 — "Your Vessel": Creating Our Vessel.** Definition: the vessel = "the sum of all the unique pieces that we create on our journey" — more than the product or funnel alone. Explicit named components of the vessel (a checklist of business assets beyond the product itself): Credibility; Strategic Partners; Voice/Brand; Distribution; "and (as usual) a lot more." Explicit permission-to-start-small clause: the vessel "can be complex as a battle ship, or as simple as a rowboat" — the goal isn't a perfect ship on day one, it's a foundation you compound on over time.

- **Step 4 — "Putting It All Together" / Tactical Training.** Meta-note: everything in Part Five up to this point is "strategic" content; Step 4 acknowledges a second content type — **"tactical" information**, defined as "the specific tactics that you can follow to execute strategy." Vosler states tactical execution detail is beyond this book's scope and CTAs to an external free video training (link.sean.co/va-tactical). Functionally: readers should treat the book's Part Five as the strategic skeleton and expect to source execution tactics elsewhere (his paid/free funnel).

### 2.7 Appendix 1 — "Tried & True Copy Formulas" (full formula catalog decoded)

This is a **reference compilation**, not original Vosler IP — sourcing is explicit and load-bearing: intro note states these are compiled from multiple originators, credited where possible, with an open invitation (reference@sean.co) to correct missing attribution, and a companion URL (sean.co/copyref) mapping each "Source Name" to its original citation link. Table of contents (p.349) lists ~13 major category headers spanning: General Copywriting Formulas; Copywriting Formulas for Long-Form Sales Pages; Copywriting Formulas for VSLs; Headline Writing Formulas (general + lead-gen/ebook/blog-specific); Formulas for Writing Value Propositions; Fascination Formulas (bullet lists); Copywriting Formulas to Entice Action; More Formulas and Templates; 41 Classic Copywriting Headline Templates; Headline Generators; Even More Resources (subject lines, email templates, CTA templates, product description templates, social media copy, cold/sales email templates, other swipe files).

Fully decoded formulas actually read in this page range (each is a complete, executable skeleton):

- **AIDA** (century-old base model): **Attention** (Awareness) — what message pulls the reader in; **Interest** (Inspire) — challenge norms/explore counter-intuitive angles; **Desire** — build allure by appealing to emotion, what's the big win; **Action** — explicit ask. Example use-cases cited: enticing headlines, webinar copy.

- **AIDCA / IDCA variants** — AIDA + "Conviction" inserted before Action: Attention→Interest→Desire→**Conviction** (turn doubt into trust via testimonials/endorsements/statistics/research/demos/guarantees)→Action. IDCA = same minus the initial Attention stage (used once attention is already captured, e.g. landing page after an ad).
  - Also catalogs 5 additional named variants as a reference list (not separately decoded in depth, but skeletons given): **Basic AIDA** (Awareness→Interest→Desire→Action); **Lavidge Hierarchy of Effects** (Awareness→Knowledge→Liking→Preference→Conviction→Purchase); **McGuire's model** (Presentation→Attention→Comprehension→Yielding→Retention→Behavior); **Modified AIDA** (Awareness→Interest→Conviction→Desire→Action); **AIDAS** (Attention→Interest→Desire→Action→Satisfaction); **AISDALSLove** (Awareness→Interest→Search→Desire→Action→Like/dislike→Share→Love/Hate). Underlying psych model named: CAB — Cognition→Affect→Behavior.

- **Danny Iny's 6+1 Formula** (a spin on AIDA): 1) Consider the **context**; 2) Grab **attention**; 3) Move the reader swiftly to **desire** a solution; 4) Present the consequence of NOT acting — Iny calls this "**the gap**"; 5) Present the **solution**, revealing only as much as needed to get to the next step; 6) Call the prospect to **act**. The "+1" is the context-setting step 1 that precedes classic AIDA.

- **PAS — Dan Kennedy** (called "the most reliable sales formula ever invented" per Kennedy): **Problem** — present the felt problem; **Agitation** — poke at the problem until it's visceral; **Solution** — present your fix to the now-agitated problem. Named variant (Sean Mitchell): **PAD S** → Problem, Agitation, **Discredit** (discredit competing/alternative solutions), Solution.

- **4 Ps (Henry Hoke Sr.)**: **Picture** — create a vivid scene; **Promise** — state how your solution ends the painful (or fulfills the aspirational) scene; **Prove** — support the promise with evidence; **Push** — nudge to action. Alternate labeling given: Problem/Promise/Proof/Proposal.

- **ACCA**: **Raise Awareness**; increase **Comprehension** of the problem (i.e., teach it); inspire **Conviction** to act on it; rally to **Action**. Framed as useful "to rouse the hearts of a disconnected crowd."

- **AAPPA (or PAPA)**: Grab **Attention**; show the **Advantage(s)** of your solution; **Prove** what you just claimed; **Persuade**; move to **Action**.

- **PAPA** (distinct short variant): **Problem** → **Advantages** of solving it → **Proof** you can solve it → **Action**.

- **QUEST**: **Qualify** the prospect; **Understand** where they currently are (reflect this back to them); **Educate** them on a better way; **Stimulate** desire for that better way; **Transition** them from where they are to becoming your customer.

- **SLAP**: **Stop** the prospect; make them **Look** (read); make them **Act**; get the **Purchase**. Noted as suited to short pages / lower-cost offers. Vosler's own editorial aside: "act" and "purchase" arguably shouldn't be separate steps if the action IS the purchase — a rare moment of the author critiquing a formula he's cataloging rather than just relaying it.

- **AIDPPC (Robert Collier)**: **Attention → Interest → Description → Persuasion → Proof → Close.** Decoded distinction from AIDA: the first two stages match AIDA exactly, but "Desire" is expanded/unpacked into three sub-elements — (1) **Description**: paint the likely experience of the solution AND the problem/status-quo/cost-of-not-switching; (2) **Persuasion**: deploy persuasive levers like loss aversion, testimonials, future-pacing; (3) **Proof**: demos, testimonials, endorsements that the solution uniquely delivers. Explicit use-case: "if you've struggled with the 'desire' part of AIDA, try AIDPPC instead" — positioned as a more granular substitute for AIDA's weakest/vaguest stage.

- **5-Point Copywriting Formula (Jack Lacey)** — framed as ideal for lead-gen/landing pages, or to guide a full one-page site. Five questions the copy must answer, in order: 1) What will you do for me if I listen to your story? 2) How are you going to do this? 3) Who is responsible for the promises you make? 4) Who have you done this for? 5) What will it cost me?

- **AICPBSAWN** (long-form nine-element formula): **Attention** — biggest benefit/biggest problem you solve, i.e. the USP; **Interest** — reason to keep reading; **Credibility** — reason to believe you; **Proof** — evidence of your claim; **Benefits** — list them all; **Scarcity** — create urgency via limited availability; **Action** — tell them exactly what to do; **Warn** — state the cost/consequence of inaction; **Now** — motivate immediate action (not "later").

- **Bob Serling's Power Copywriting Formula** (11-item process checklist, not a mnemonic acronym — a build sequence): 1) Quality product (prerequisite, non-negotiable); 2) Customer profile; 3) Credibility; 4) Offer; 5) Conduct exhaustive research; 6) Rest — let ideas percolate before drafting; 7) Create a comprehensive features/facts/figures list; 8) List every benefit; 9) Create an irresistible offer; 10) Create a great guarantee (explicit note: don't be "ho-hum" about this critical risk-reducer); 11) Write an attention-grabbing headline. (List continues past p.4's cutoff into further steps on drawing attention with color, limiting graphics, no-holds-barred hook, eliminating early objections, crossheads, deepening the prospect's felt pain, establishing credibility, "insider benefit" lock-in, unquestionable proof, readable chunking, benefit listing/summarizing, feature listing, above-and-beyond packaging, pricing, CTA, order-bump/piggyback offer, risk minimization, benefit-summary close, P.S., distraction-link removal, letting copy rest before shipping, and a final revision pass — total 36 steps per the visible numbering, only steps 1–14 fully captured in this extraction window with steps 15–36 itemized as a numbered list of tactical additions.)

- **Craig Clemens Sales Letter Formula** (sequence, not lettered acronym): Headline → Subhead → Questions that show you understand and push buttons → Powerful analogy or story → "It's not your fault and there's hope" (reframe/blame-external beat) → Give away content (value-first proof) → Proof → "What is it I'm offering? What's in it?" (explicit offer reveal) → Bullet points/benefits → Who needs it? Who doesn't? (self-qualification) → What makes it different? → Wrap up → Price.

## 5. VOICE & TEACHING DNA

- Signature self-deprecating disclaimers before big claims: *"I HATE SALES. I mean I loath it."* / *"Is it my masterful marketing magic spell I cast on them? ... Nope.
