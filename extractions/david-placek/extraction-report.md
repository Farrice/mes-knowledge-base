## Genius Patterns

### 1. Ship of Gold Treasure Hunting

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Maps the entire territory before diving. Doesn't chase the first promising signal — surveys all shipwrecks, eliminates false leads, then commits resources to the highest-probability target.
- **Executable Behavior**: For any naming brief, build the full landscape map first — Greek roots, Latin roots, mythology, periodic table, aviation, unrelated domains. Generate 2,000+ candidates across multiple treasure-hunting zones before converging. Never stop at 50-100.
- **Deployment Context**: Any naming project, product launch, or creative sprint where the temptation is to grab the first decent option.
- **Success Metric**: You should have 10x–20x more raw candidates than you think you need before narrowing.

### 2. Surprisingly Familiar

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Identifies the precise crosshair where a word is easy to process (familiar root, real word, intuitive pronunciation) but creates a surprise reaction in its category context. The brain gets lazy processing — then gets jolted.
- **Executable Behavior**: For every candidate name, test: (1) Can someone pronounce it on first read? (2) Does it evoke *something* recognizable? (3) Is it *unexpected* in this specific category? All three must be yes. Swiffer on a mop — familiar "swift" sound, surprising in cleaning. Feather on a fiber supplement — familiar word, surprising in digestive health.
- **Deployment Context**: Final-round name evaluation, copywriting headlines, product naming.
- **Success Metric**: The name triggers the "I get it, but I wouldn't have thought of that" reaction.

### 3. The Comfort Trap Diagnosis

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Immediately identifies when a name has consensus but no energy. Maps names against a tension zone (high energy, polarizing, some team members hate it) vs. an invisible zone (safe, workable, unremarkable). Targets the tension zone.
- **Executable Behavior**: When evaluating names, plot them on the distinctiveness-vs-familiarity axis. If everyone agrees it's "fine," it's invisible. If half the room hates it, ask *why* they hate it — that's where the energy lives. Andy Grove's insight: "This is so polarizing — that means it has energy inside."
- **Deployment Context**: Any creative review session — names, headlines, positioning, brand concepts.
- **Success Metric**: At least one name on your shortlist should make someone uncomfortable.

### 4. Two-Person Teams, Three Divergent Briefs

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Eliminates brainstorming sessions (peer pressure kills originality). Runs three parallel two-person teams with deliberately different creative briefs — one with the real product, one with an invented product extension, one in a completely unrelated category.
- **Executable Behavior**: For any naming project, create three distinct creative frames:
  - **Team 1**: Full brief — knows everything about the actual product.
  - **Team 2**: Modified brief — adds a fictional feature (e.g., "it also has energy minerals").
  - **Team 3**: Category transplant — works as if the assignment is in a completely different domain (e.g., athletic performance instead of fiber supplement).
  Combine all three output streams. The synchronicity of seemingly irrelevant connections is the fundamental layer of creativity.
- **Deployment Context**: Any creative production beyond solo work — naming, copy, campaign concepts.
- **Success Metric**: Three distinct name families emerge from three distinct creative angles.

### 5. Approximate Thinking

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Uses a spectrum from *bizarre/absurd/illegal ideas* on one end to *safe/workable ideas* on the other, with "approximate thinking" in the middle. Gives teams permission to stop in the middle — ideas don't have to be fully baked, legally cleared, or polished. They just need to approximate a direction.
- **Executable Behavior**: Draw a line. Left end: bizarre. Right end: safe. Middle: approximate. Tell your team: "Stay in approximate. Don't perfect it. Don't kill it. Just get close." Then evaluate approximate ideas as a group.
- **Deployment Context**: Creative kickoffs, brainstorming replacements, team ideation sessions, overcoming perfectionism blocks.
- **Success Metric**: Creativity spikes because people stop self-censoring.

### 6. Sound Symbolism Engineering

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Evaluates names through linguistic science — specific phonemes carry psychological weight. He doesn't just "feel" which name is better; he has 30+ years of cross-language research on which sounds signal speed, reliability, innovation, pleasantness.
- **Executable Behavior**: Apply known sound symbolism principles:
  - **K, P, B**: Reliable, fast, strong (Pentium, BlackBerry)
  - **Z**: Attention-getting, cutting-edge (Azure, Breeze → Febreze)
  - **X**: Innovation, the unknown (SpaceX, Lexus)
  - **V**: Vibrancy, movement (Vercel, Viagra)
  - **CVCV pattern** (consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel): How children learn language — maximally easy to process (Sonos, Turo)
- **Deployment Context**: Name scoring, cross-language viability checks, shortlist refinement.
- **Success Metric**: Names pass both the sound symbolism check and the processing fluency test.

### 7. Proof-of-Concept Contextualization

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Never shows names on a spreadsheet. Always presents them *in context* — a Wall Street Journal headline, a celebrity endorsement ad, a product shelf mockup. The one rule: "Is this believable?"
- **Executable Behavior**: Before presenting any name to stakeholders, mock it up in at least two real-world contexts. Test the single-second believability: would someone leaning forward and seeing this for the first time think, "I believe a real company could be called this"?
- **Deployment Context**: Client presentations, internal creative reviews, name shortlist refinement.
- **Success Metric**: Stakeholders evaluate names as *brands* rather than *words*.

### 8. The Courage Culture

- **What He Does Unconsciously**: Redefines creative management from "leading" to "encouraging" (root word: courage). Uses specific verbal patterns — "I wouldn't have thought of that" (credits the person), "I wish we could make that work" (redirects without slamming), "That's interesting" (keeps the door open). Separates speculation from evaluation as distinct phases.
- **Executable Behavior**: 
  - When someone presents an idea: say "That's interesting" or "I wouldn't have thought of that" before anything else.
  - When an idea has problems: say "I wish we could make that so it wasn't [problem]" or "How do we modify that so it's [desired quality]?" — converts criticism into problem-solving.
  - Never evaluate during the generation phase. Ideas "live in the system" for a period before narrowing begins.
- **Deployment Context**: Managing any creative team — copywriters, designers, marketers, product teams.
- **Success Metric**: Team members stop self-censoring and generate higher-volume, more divergent ideas.

## Hidden Knowledge

Tacit expertise Placek demonstrates but doesn't explicitly teach. These are the crown jewel insights — what you'd only learn by working at Lexicon for years.

- **The Quantity Threshold**: Most founders generate 50-100 names and get stuck. Lexicon generates 2,000+. The quality curve doesn't kick in until you push past the comfortable stopping point. The first 100 names are the obvious ones everybody would find — the gold is in names 500-2,000.

- **The Body Language Read**: Placek reads *physical reactions* to concepts in conversation, not verbal responses. In the fiber exercise, he didn't listen to what Sean said — he watched Sean's posture lift upward when discussing lightness and said "You feel lighter." He reads the room's body, not their words.

- **The Category Escape**: The first hypothesis on any naming project is always "don't go where the category is." Before exploring any concepts, map the competitive landscape and declare it a no-go zone. Fiber brands all say "fiber" → therefore don't say fiber. Mops all say "mop" → therefore it's not a mop, it's a Swiffer.

- **The Strategic Question Sequence**: Four questions in exact order: (1) How do you define winning? (2) What do you *have* to win? (3) What do you *need* to win? (4) What do you *need to say*? Every client will answer question 1 differently — which is the diagnostic. The sequence moves from strategy → assets → gaps → message.

- **The Competitor Courage Frame**: When a name is polarizing internally, reframe it as competitive advantage: "Your competitors would *never* have the courage to put this name on a device." The BlackBerry close — the argument that broke through crossed arms wasn't about linguistics or data, it was about competitive positioning of courage.

- **The Re-Name Safety Net**: Placek has "never seen evidence" that renaming causes lost equity or momentum — provided the launch has enthusiasm and a clear "we were here, now we're going there" story. The biggest barrier to renaming is psychological (fear of losing equity), not empirical. Windsurf (née Kodium) is proof — the rename *was* the launch.

## Methodology

### The Placek Naming System (4 Phases)

**Phase 1 — Landscape Intelligence** (Days 1-3)
1. Map the competitive naming landscape — bucket every existing name in the category.
2. Declare the first hypothesis: "We're not going there."
3. Study the product with fresh eyes — what is this thing *actually*? (Swiffer is NOT a mop.)
4. Study the consumer — ladder up from features → benefits → ultimate benefit.
5. Run the Strategic Question Sequence: Define winning → What do you have → What do you need → What do you need to say.
6. Articulate the creative framework (a window to walk through, not a box to conform to).

**Phase 2 — Treasure Hunting** (Days 3-10)
1. Deploy three two-person teams with divergent briefs.
2. Each team hunts across multiple databases: Greek/Latin roots, mythology, periodic table (enriched), sounds/phonemes, domain-specific terminology, unrelated domains (aviation, sports medicine, etc.).
3. Free-associate for 20-minute sprints — pad + pen → AI queries (don't ask for names, ask about concepts) → pursue threads.
4. Generate 2,000+ candidates. Call them "trash" without judgment — necessary raw material.
5. Run two creative cycles (~2.5 weeks total, across multiple projects).

**Phase 3 — Convergence** (Days 10-15)
1. Evaluate candidates against the creative framework (objectives as window, not fence).
2. Apply the Surprisingly Familiar test: Original in category? Processing fluent? Unexpected?
3. Run sound symbolism analysis (speed, reliability, innovation, memorability, CVCV pattern).
4. Ship shortlist (10-15 names) to trademark/legal review.
5. Ship to global linguists for cross-language cultural viability.
6. Create proof-of-concept mockups for survivors.

**Phase 4 — Presentation & Decision** (Days 15-20)
1. Present names *in context* — headlines, endorsements, product mockups.
2. Apply the believability test: "Is this believable in less than one second?"
3. Navigate the comfort trap: if everyone agrees, that's a warning sign.
4. Use the polarization-as-energy frame for strong candidates.
5. Develop supporting copy ("The lightweight fiber you need") — consumers aren't stupid.
6. Finalize nomenclature, positioning line, short story.
