## Either / Or  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Creates the illusion of choice.
**Structure**: `Would you prefer {Option A} or {Option B}?`
> ****

##### Do you prefer {x} or {y}?

##### Examples
- Do you prefer credit card or wire transfer? 
- Would Tuesday at 10am work or is Thursday at 2pm better? 
- Would you prefer the monthly plan or the annual plan?
- Is the challenge primarily strategic, or operational?

##### Variations
Would you rather {option A} or {option B}?
---
Is the issue more {A} or {B}?
---
Would you start with {A} or {B}?

#### Why It Works

##### Replaces Yes/No With Choice
A yes/no question asks:
> Will you do it?
An either/or question asks:
> Which way would you like to do it?
The conversation shifts from deciding whether to participate to deciding how to participate.

##### Reduces Decision Fatigue
Many people struggle with unlimited options.
They do much better when presented with a smaller set of choices.
Instead of:
> What do you want to do?
We ask:
> Would you rather start with lead generation or conversion?
The conversation becomes easier because the search space becomes smaller.

##### Creates Clarity
The strongest use of this pattern is in it's diagnosis.
When someone is overwhelmed, confused, or rambling, narrowing the possibilities often creates immediate clarity.
For example:
> Is the problem that you don't know what to do, or that you're not doing what you already know?
That question can save twenty minutes of conversation.

##### Forces Comparison
Many people evaluate options in isolation.
This pattern forces side-by-side evaluation.
Instead of:
> Should I hire someone?
We get:
> Would you rather hire someone or continue doing it yourself?
Now the decision becomes clearer because both options are visible simultaneously.

##### Reveals Preferences
Sometimes people don't know what they want until they see alternatives.
When presented with two choices, preferences emerge naturally.

##### When It Backfires
This pattern becomes dangerous when the other person feels their choices are being artificially restricted.
For example:
> Would you like to move forward today or tomorrow?
If the prospect is thinking:
> I don't even know if I want to move forward yet.
They'll immediately notice the missing option. Instead of helping the conversation, the pattern creates resistance because it feels like you're trying to steer them somewhere they haven't agreed to go.
The moment someone feels manipulated, they stop evaluating the choices and start evaluating your intentions.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Narrows choices.
- Creates clarity.
- Simplifies decisions.
- Reveals preferences.
- Reduces overwhelm.
- Creates movement.
- Helps diagnose problems.
- Encourages comparison.
- Makes conversations more efficient.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | Did you reach out because you're trying to grow faster or because you're trying to reduce stress? |
| Discover | Is the challenge primarily strategic or operational? |
| Clarify | Is the issue lack of leads or lack of follow-up? |
| Expand | Would solving this create more money, more time, or both? |
| Commit | Would you rather do this on your own or would you like some help? |
| Resolve | Is the concern the investment itself or the return you expect from it? |

## Embedded Commands  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Hides a command from the conscious mind.
**Structure**: `You don't have to {desired action} right now…`
> ****

##### You don't have to {desired action} right now…

##### Examples
1. "You don't have to immediately accept this."
1. "I'm not saying you'll become successful overnight."
1. "Whether or not you decide to move forward..."
1. You don’t have to buy right now… 

#### Why It Works
Most commands trigger evaluation.
> Buy now.
> Decide now.
> Take action.
The conscious mind immediately recognizes a command and decides whether to comply.
Embedded commands hide the command inside a larger sentence.
Instead of:
> Decide now.
We get:
> You don't have to decide now.
The conscious mind processes the entire sentence.
The unconscious mind still receives:
> Decide now.
The command is present, but it arrives indirectly.

##### Repetition Creates Familiarity
One of the biggest mistakes people make is treating embedded commands as a one-time technique.
The real power comes from repetition.
If the same command appears repeatedly throughout a conversation, it starts becoming familiar.
For example:
> You don't have to decide right now.
> Whether or not you decide today...
> As you decide what's best for you...
> Before you decide...
> When you decide...
The listener keeps encountering:
> Decide.
> Decide.
> Decide.
> Decide.
The command becomes increasingly familiar and comfortable.

##### The Brain Focuses on the Action
One reason embedded commands are so effective is that attention naturally moves toward the action itself.
Consider:
> Don't think about a pink elephant.
Most people immediately think about a pink elephant.
The mind must first represent the idea before it can process the negation.
Similarly:
> You don't have to buy right now.
The mind still processes:
> Buy right now.
The action receives attention even though it appears inside a negation.

##### Preserves Autonomy
Direct commands often create resistance.
Embedded commands preserve choice.
Instead of:
> Buy now.
We get:
> You don't have to buy right now.
The listener retains freedom.
The suggestion feels less forceful.
People rarely resist freedom.

##### The Word "Now" Carries Weight
Few words do more work than:
> Now.
Now directs attention to the present moment.
Now implies immediacy.
Now creates action orientation.
Now interrupts delay.
Compare:
> You don't have to decide.
Versus:
> You don't have to decide now.
The second version subtly directs attention toward the act of deciding in the present moment.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Introduces commands without resistance.
- Creates familiarity through repetition.
- Directs attention toward desired actions.
- Reduces reactance.
- Makes suggestions feel natural.
- Encourages present-moment action.
- Allows ideas to be revisited repeatedly throughout a conversation.

#### Phonological Marking
A phonological marker occurs when the embedded command is delivered differently than the surrounding language.
For example:
> You don't have to DECIDE NOW...
The words may remain inside the sentence, but the delivery changes.
The speaker might:
- lower their voice
- raise their voice
- slow down
- pause before and after
- change rhythm
- change emphasis
- alter tonality
The command becomes subtly distinct from the surrounding language.
Not enough to consciously stand out. But just enough to be different.
The brain is constantly looking for differences.
If everything is delivered with the same tonality, nothing stands out.
The moment the delivery changes, attention is drawn to it.
A slight pause.
A slower pace.
A softer voice.
A change in emphasis.
The embedded command becomes highlighted without explicitly being highlighted.

##### The Unconscious Notices What the Conscious Mind Ignores
If done right, the listener won’t ever pick it out. 
They just simply experience it differently.
The conscious mind remembers the sentence.
The unconscious mind remembers the emphasis.

##### Hidden in Plain Sight
The strongest phonological markers do not sound theatrical.
They do not sound hypnotic.
They do not sound manipulative.
They sound natural.
The listener should not consciously notice anything unusual.
The phrase simply lands with slightly more weight than the words around it.
That's often enough.

## Frames  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Creates a context to make your communication most effective.
**Structure**: `{Situation} is {claim} because {context}.`
A frame is the context that is wrapped around the communication. 
To be as persuasive as possible, you try to put the best context  around what you are communicating. 
Different frames can be effective or disastrous depending upon when you use them. 
But most people don’t even attempt to control a frame at all - and then wonder why they often don’t get what they want when trying to influence a conversation. 
To understand frames, let’s take a casual encounter every child has had. 
Mom pours a glass of milk and gives it to the child and says: “Don’t spill it.”
She’s just framed the communication negatively. 
Instead, she could’ve said “be careful with the milk.”
Which is better?
In this specific circumstance, a positive phrase is better because you are describing the action you want to occur. 
Whereas telling a kid to not spill the milk makes the kids think of spilling the milk. 
Another example - if you tell a patient they have an 80% survival rate on a surgery, they are more likely to opt for it than if it has a 20% fatality rate… even though those two data points mean the same thing. 
It’s only the framing that changes. 
And there are a lot of frames. 
Revisiting the milk:

| Frame Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Positive Frame | Be careful with the milk. |
| Negative Frame | Don't spill the milk. |
| Outcome Frame | Let's safely drink the milk and put the glass in the sink when done. |
| Solution Frame | How do we make sure we are responsible with the milk?  |
| Identity Frame | I’ve noticed how careful you can be when drinking milk.  |
| Responsibility Frame | It's your job to take care of this glass of milk.  |
| Consequence Frame | If it spills, you'll have a mess to clean up. |
| Scarcity Frame | That's the last of the milk. |
| Comparison Frame | Being careful with the milk is easier than cleaning up spilled milk. |
| Challenge Frame | Think you can drink it all without spilling a drop?  |
| Curiosity Frame | What do you think is the easiest way to carefully drink the milk? |
Now, to generalize frames into structures you can use in all sorts of situations and conversations: 

| Frame | Description | Template |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Frame | Focuses attention on the pain, obstacles, and consequences that need to be solved. | What is the biggest problem with {situation}? |
| Outcome Frame | Focuses attention on the desired result rather than the current problem. | If this worked perfectly, what would {desired outcome} look like? |
| Decision Frame | Focuses attention on making a decision rather than endlessly discussing possibilities. | If you were going to decide, what would you need to know first? |
| Identity Frame | Focuses attention on who someone is, wants to be, or believes themselves to be. | Would you consider yourself an open-minded person?  |
| Meaning Frame | Assigns meaning to facts, behaviors, or circumstances. | The fact that {observable fact} means {meaning}. |
| Possibility Frame | Expands what someone believes is available, achievable, or true. | What might happen if {constraint} wasn't an issue? |
| Constraint Frame | Identifies what is preventing movement, progress, or commitment. | What is preventing {desired outcome} from happening right now? |
| Comparison Frame | Creates meaning by contrasting one thing against another. | Would you rather have {option A} or {option B}? |
| Risk Frame | Focuses attention on potential gains, losses, or consequences. | What is the risk of {action} versus the risk of {inaction}? |

| Frame | What It Does | Template / Example | Found In |
|---|---|---|---|

| Charitable Interpretation Frame | Interprets resistance, objections, or difficult behavior as evidence of positive character traits. Converts obstruction into virtue, making the listener feel seen while making the objection easier to work with. | "Because you're being this careful, that tells me you're someone who, once they commit, commits fully." | Positive Intent |

| Permission Frame | Seeks explicit consent before delivering a challenge or reframe. The act of asking permission creates a small agreement to remain open, turning the listener from a defender into a willing evaluator. | "Would it be okay if I pushed back on that for a second?" | Permission, Wrong, But |

| State Break Frame | Interrupts habitual thought patterns to create a brief window of openness. When the conversational script breaks unexpectedly, fixed positions soften and attention resets. | "Wait!? Are you an only child?” | Pattern Interrupt |

| Resource Access Frame | Activates existing internal resources (confidence, decisiveness, clarity) by accessing past memories where those states were present. Demonstrates that the resource already exists; it just isn't currently active. | "Can you remember a time when you committed fully to something and it changed what was possible?" | Reawakening, As a Child, Win/Lose Contrast |

Once someone confirms they are "someone who acts when something clearly makes sense," saying no becomes inconsistent with who they just said they are. | "Do you consider yourself someone who acts when the evidence is strong enough?" | Priming, Decision Primer |
| Epistemic Humility Frame | Admits uncertainty before delivering an interpretation. "I may be wrong, but..." signals that we aren't attached to being right but to be helpful. | "I may be wrong, but I don't think this is really about the money." | Wrong, But, Claim Softening |

## Presuppositions  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Assumes a desired outcome, decision or event as fact
**Structure**: `Ask a question that assumes {desired outcome, decision, action, or future state} has already occurred or will occur.`
> ****

##### Ask a question that assumes {desired outcome, decision, action, or future state} has already occurred or will occur.

##### Examples
- "When would you like to start?"
- "Which of these results are you most excited to get first?"
- "How much of the extra revenue will you reinvest back into growth?"
- "How quickly do you want to see the pipeline numbers shift?"
- "What will you do with the twelve hours a week you get back?"
- "After we fix the follow-up problem, which bottleneck do you want to tackle next?"
- "How are you planning to handle the increased capacity once the close rate improves?"

#### Why It Works

##### Bypasses the Yes/No Decision
Most questions open a debate. The listener decides whether to participate.
Presuppositions skip the debate entirely.
Instead of: "Would you like to start?" we ask: "When would you like to start?"
The decision to act is no longer on the table. Only the timing is.

##### The Mind Answers the Question It's Asked
When we hear a question, the brain automatically searches for an answer.
If we ask "When would you like to start?", the listener's brain starts looking for a time,  not a reason to say no, not a defense, just a time.
The question directs mental energy toward movement, not resistance.

##### Uses Certainty to Create Certainty
Presuppositions communicate confidence.
When we speak as if an outcome is already decided, the listener picks up on that certainty. Uncertainty is contagious. So is confidence.
If we sound unsure, they feel unsure. If we sound certain, the certainty transfers.

##### Creates Forward Momentum
Every presupposition moves the conversation forward. Instead of defending a position, both parties are already discussing what happens next.
The conversation feels natural and progressive rather than a negotiation.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Skips the debate over whether to act
- Directs attention to details rather than decisions
- Communicates confidence and certainty
- Creates conversational momentum
- Makes the desired outcome feel inevitable
- Reduces decision friction

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "Which part of your business are you most excited to talk about today?" |
| Expand | "How much more are you leaving on the table each month while this stays unsolved?" |
| Commit | "When you implement this, what would you want working in the first 30 days?" |
| Decide | "When would you like to get started?" |
| Resolve | "Once we address that concern, what do you see as the next step?" |

## Tie-down  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Gets listener to commit to the conclusion you want
**Structure**: `{Statement}, isn't it?`
> ****

##### {Statement that both parties know to be true}, {isn't it / wouldn't it / haven't you / doesn't it}?

##### Examples
- "Getting more predictable revenue would take a lot of pressure off, wouldn't it?"
- "You've been sitting with this problem longer than you'd like, haven't you?"
- "Finding a reliable way to fill the pipeline every month would change the game for you, wouldn't it?"
- "Solving this before Q4 would make the whole quarter feel different, wouldn't it?"
- "That kind of certainty is exactly what you've been missing, isn't it?"
Note: you can place it at the beginning or middle of a sentence as well:
- Isn’t it easy to follow along?
- It’s easy to follow along, isn’t it, especially when given clear instructions?

#### Why It Works

##### Creates a Pattern of Agreement
Each "yes" builds a small commitment. Small commitments stack.
The more someone says yes, the more natural it feels to keep saying yes. The pattern creates momentum in the direction you want to go.

##### Invites Confirmation, Not Debate
Most questions open a decision. A tie-down closes one.
Instead of asking whether something is true, we ask them to confirm what both of us already know to be true.
"Do you want more predictable revenue?" invites debate.
"Predictable revenue would make a big difference for you, wouldn't it?" invites agreement.

##### The Question Mark Carries Social Weight
When we add "isn't it?" or "wouldn't it?" to a statement, we signal that we expect agreement.
That expectation carries social pressure. Most people don't push back.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates a pattern of micro-agreements that build momentum
- Communicates confidence in the value being discussed
- Makes the listener feel heard and understood
- Creates internal pressure to remain consistent
- Prevents sudden reversal at the end of the conversation
- Frames negative outcomes as undesirable without forcing the point

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | "You've been dealing with this longer than you'd like, haven't you?" |
| Clarify | "So the real issue is follow-up, not lead volume, isn't it?" |
| Expand | "Fixing this now is less expensive than fixing it after another bad quarter, wouldn't you agree?" |
| Commit | "If we solved this, that would change everything for you, wouldn't it?" |
| Decide | "Everything we've talked about makes sense, doesn't it?" |
| Resolve | "That guarantee removes the biggest concern, doesn't it?" |

## Commitment Chain  [Commitment]
**Summary**: Creates agreement momentum by starting with smaller agreements first.
**Structure**: `Can you see {benefit}? → Would {benefit} be valuable? → If you were going to {benefit}, when would you start?`
> ****

##### Can you see {benefit}?
Would {benefit} be valuable? 
If you were going to {benefit}, when would you start?

##### Examples
Can you see how this could help generate more qualified leads?
Are you interested in generating more qualified leads?
If you were going to start generating more qualified leads, when would be the best time to start?
---
Can you see the value of having predictable lead flow?
Would predictable lead flow be useful?
If you were going to build predictable lead flow, when would be the best time to begin?
---
Can you see how clearer expectations would improve team performance?
Would improved performance be valuable?
If you were going to create clearer expectations, when would be the best time to start?

#### Why It Works

##### Agreement Ladder
The prospect walks up the staircase one step at a time instead of being asked to jump straight to a major decision.
Each agreement makes the next agreement easier.

##### Ownership of Benefits
The prospect must personally recognize the benefit before they are asked whether they want it.

##### Consistency Pressure
Once someone says:
> Yes, I can see that.
And:
> Yes, that would be valuable.
The third question creates internal pressure to remain consistent with those earlier answers.

##### Presupposition
The final question does not ask:
> Will you do it?
It asks:
> When would you do it?
The action is treated as the natural next step.
---

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates momentum inside the conversation.
- Gets the prospect selling themselves.
- Moves the conversation from possibility to implementation.
- Creates consistency pressure without confrontation.
- Makes the final step feel natural.
- Reveals genuine interest.
- Identifies whether the prospect sees value before discussing solutions.
- Creates micro-commitments that support larger commitments.

##### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Clarify | Can you see how the real issue might be follow-up rather than lead generation?  Would fixing follow-up be valuable if that turned out to be true?  If follow-up is the problem, when would you want to solve it? |
| Expand | Can you see how adding just one more salesperson without fixing the process could create even more chaos?  Would avoiding that chaos be important?  If you were going to fix the process first, when would be the best time to do it? |
| Commit | Can you see how having a written hiring process would help you avoid another bad hire?  Would avoiding another six-month hiring mistake be valuable?  If you were going to put that process in place, when would you begin? |
| Resolve | Can you see how the cost of doing nothing for another year is probably larger than the investment we're discussing?  Would avoiding that loss be worthwhile?  If you were going to stop absorbing that loss, when would be the best time to begin? |

## Decision Contract  [Commitment]
**Summary**: Creating the rules that get someone to say yes.
**Structure**: `If it's in your best interest, say yes. If not, say no. If unclear, let's discuss it. Fair enough?`
> ****

##### I’m going to {action}. Then I’m going to ask you to {decision}. 

If it's clearly in your best interest, I’ll ask you to say yes. 

If it's clearly not in your best interest, I'll ask you to say no. 

And if it's unclear, then let's discuss what's unclear until we determine whether it's a yes or a no. 

Fair enough?

##### Examples
I'm going to present the offer.
Then I'm going to ask you to make a decision.
If it's clearly in your best interest, I'll ask you to say yes.
If it's clearly not in your best interest, I'll ask you to say no.
And if it's unclear, then let's discuss what's unclear until we determine whether it's a yes or a no.
Fair enough?
---
At the end of this discussion, there are really only three possible outcomes.
We conclude this is a good fit.
We conclude it isn't.
Or we conclude we need more information.
I'm comfortable with any of those outcomes.
Are you?
---
Once you've heard the proposal, I'd like you to evaluate it on its merits.
If you believe it's the right move, support it.
If you believe it isn't, challenge it.
And if you're undecided, let's talk through your concerns.
Does that sound fair?
---
When we're finished, there are only three intellectually honest answers:
"Yes, this makes sense."
"No, this doesn't make sense."
Or
"I don't yet have enough information to know."
If it's yes, let's proceed.
If it's no, let's not.
If it's the third, let's determine exactly what information is missing.
Agreed?

#### Why It Works

##### Eliminates the "Maybe"
Most of us are afraid of commitment, even when it's in our best interest.
When we say yes, we give up the ability to say no.
When we say no, we close the door on the opportunity entirely.
As a result, we often drift toward maybe because it feels safer. Maybe allows us to keep all our options open. Maybe postpones the risk of being wrong. Maybe delays the responsibility of making a decision.
The problem is that maybe doesn’t serve us.
It's perfectly reasonable to be thoughtful. It's perfectly reasonable to gather information. But many times, "I need to think about it" is not actually a request for more information. It's a way to delay making a decision.
This pattern addresses that issue before it ever appears.

##### Creates a Closed Loop
The conversation now has a defined ending.
We're not talking just to talk.
We're talking to determine:
- yes
- no
- or what information is missing so we can get a yes or a no. 
That creates focus.

##### Aligns With Their Best Interest
The pattern explicitly gives permission to say no.
That dramatically lowers resistance.
We're not trying to get a yes.
We're trying to find the truth.
The prospect feels safer because we are positioned on the same side of the table.

##### Removes Hidden Agendas
Most sales conversations contain an unspoken expectation.
Everyone knows a decision is desired.
Nobody says it.
This pattern brings the expectation into the open.
That transparency creates trust.

##### Converts Commitment Into Evaluation
Many people fear commitment.
Few people fear evaluation.
The pattern reframes the conversation.
We are not committing.
We are evaluating.
The commitment only comes after the evaluation is complete.
That makes the process feel safer.

##### Protects Your Time
If a prospect can't even agree to this frame at the beginning of the conversation, they’ll never buy at at the end. 
Think about what you're asking them to agree to:
- If it's a fit, say yes.
- If it isn't a fit, say no.
- If it's unclear, let's discuss what's unclear.
That's a remarkably fair standard.
So when someone resists even that level of commitment, it often reveals that they aren't prepared to make a decision regardless of what you present.
This pattern surfaces that reality early.
Instead of spending forty-five minutes uncovering problems, discussing solutions, handling objections, and answering questions, only to discover they were never willing to make a decision in the first place, you find out within the first few minutes.
Then you can end the call immediately. You can be nice about it: 
> “Oh it seems like you're still very early on in the discovery stage of figuring out what it is you want and don't want. To save us both time on this conversation, is it okay if I send you some trainings that I've developed that may help assist you with gaining more clarity?”

#### What It Accomplishes
- Establishes a decision frame.
- Removes endless maybes.
- Creates trust.
- Lowers resistance.
- Makes “no” acceptable.
- Surfaces hidden objections.
- Creates a clear outcome for the conversation.
- Prevents wasted time.
- Positions you as objective.
- Creates expectation that a decision will be made.

## Hypothetical  [Commitment]
**Summary**: Use a condition to remove a limitation, excuse or constraint.
**Structure**: `If I could show you {desired outcome}, would you be willing to {commitment}?`
> ****

##### If I could show you {desired outcome}, would you be willing to {commitment}?

##### Examples
- If I could show you exactly how this could pay for itself within the first 90 days, would you be willing to make a yes decision today?
- If the investment turned out to be significantly smaller than the cost of the problem, would you be willing to proceed?
- If I could show you how clients in your exact situation recovered the cost in less than six months, would you be comfortable making a decision today?
- If we could show you a way to implement this without creating additional work for your team, would you be willing to move forward today?
- If I could demonstrate that getting started would take less than an hour of your time this week, would you feel comfortable saying yes?
- If we could show you a way to test this before making a larger commitment, would you be willing to proceed?
- If you became completely confident that we could deliver what we've discussed, would you be comfortable saying yes today?
- If you could reclaim ten or twenty hours a week without sacrificing growth, would you be comfortable saying yes?
- If we could eliminate that concern entirely, would there be any reason not to proceed?

##### Alternatives
- If it turned out that {desired outcome}, would you be willing to {commitment}?
- If we could prove {desired outcome}, would there be any reason not to {commitment}?
- If we could eliminate {objection}, would you be willing to {commitment}? 
- If we could accomplish {desired outcome} without {feared consequence}, would you be willing to {commitment}?
- If you discovered that {desired outcome} was actually achievable, would you be comfortable {commitment}?

#### Why It Works

##### Conditional Commitment
The prospect agrees to the decision before seeing the evidence.
Instead of arguing about whether they should buy, we establish that if the condition is met, the decision makes sense.
The conversation becomes about proving the condition rather than defending the offer.

##### Objection Isolation
The condition often reveals the real obstacle.
If they say no, we've learned that the stated concern wasn't actually the deciding factor.
If they say yes, we've identified a buying criterion.
Either way we gain clarity.

##### Future Pacing
We temporarily move them into a future where the desired outcome already exists.
That allows them to evaluate the decision from the perspective of success rather than uncertainty.

##### Decision Simplification
Most buying decisions contain dozens of variables.
This pattern reduces the entire decision to a single condition.
"If this is true, would you move forward?"
That creates a much easier question to answer.

##### Consistency Pressure
Once someone agrees that a condition would justify action, it becomes harder to reject the action after the condition has been satisfied.
The commitment is made before the evidence is presented.

##### Buying Criteria Discovery
The condition itself reveals what matters most.
Payback period.
Implementation speed.
Risk reduction.
Time investment.
Confidence.
The pattern helps uncover which variable is actually driving the decision.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates a conditional yes.
- Establishes buying criteria.
- Surfaces hidden objections.
- Simplifies complex decisions.
- Creates consistency pressure.
- Future paces the prospect into success.
- Tests whether an objection is genuine.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Application |
|---|---|
| Connect | If I had a perfect solution for you by the end of our conversation, would it be okay to share it in the last few minutes?  |
| Discover | If there was a way to fix this problem permanently, would you want to know about it? |
| Clarify | If we could prove that hiring isn't actually the bottleneck, would you be open to looking elsewhere for the cause? |
| Expand | If you could get back the fifteen hours you're losing every week to this issue, what would that be worth to you? And if we could make that happen in a way that made sense to you, would you be willing to act on it? |
| Commit | If I could show you exactly how this pays for itself within 90 days, would you be willing to make a decision today? |
| Resolve | If we could completely eliminate the concern about implementation, would there be any reason left not to sign up right now? |

## Permission  [Commitment]
**Summary**: Gains consent before introducing claim, suggestion, challenge or reframe.
**Structure**: `Would it be okay if I {reframe/challenge/suggest something}?`
> ****

##### Would it be okay if I {reframe / challenge / suggest something uncomfortable}?

##### Examples
- Would it be okay if I were to suggest an alternative way of looking at this?
- If I were to show you {outcome} for free and it was the best lesson you ever had on the subject, then would you at least consider the offer that I’ll make at the end? 
- May I suggest to you to be open to the idea that maybe you’re wrong about your limitations?
- Is it ok if I ask you to be at least 1% open to the idea that sometimes the best way to put a bad deal behind you is to start a new good deal right after?

#### Why It Works

##### Consent Lowers Resistance
The moment we ask permission, resistance almost disappears.
When someone grants permission, they've agreed to stay open before they've heard what's coming.

##### Creates a Contract
Asking permission creates a small agreement.
When someone says "sure, go ahead," they've committed to listen. They've briefly lowered their defenses.
Now what follows gets received differently than if we'd just said it without asking.

##### Positions You as Respectful, Not Pushy
Direct challenges can feel aggressive. Direct reframes can feel manipulative.
Asking first communicates respect. The listener feels in control because they technically said yes before they heard the reframe.

##### Frames What Follows as Significant
"Would it be okay if I offered a different perspective?" signals that something meaningful is coming.
The listener prepares to receive it with more attention than they'd give a casual remark.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Reduces resistance before a reframe or challenge
- Creates consent before delivering uncomfortable truths
- Signals respect for the other person's perspective
- Increases the weight and attention given to what follows
- Makes pushback less likely
- Creates a small pre-commitment to remain open

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "Would it be okay if I asked a few questions before we get started?" |
| Discover | “Is it fine if I ask you a lot of questions along the way to get the deepest understanding of your business possible?” |
| Clarify | "Would you be open to me suggesting a different cause for what you're describing?" |
| Expand | "Is it okay if I show you what this is actually costing per month?" |
| Commit | "Do I have your permission to show you what we can offer you?” |
| Decide | "Would it be okay if I asked you to sign up right now?” |
| Resolve | “May I suggest to you to be open to the idea that maybe you’re wrong about your limitations?” |

## Trial Close  [Commitment]
**Summary**: Measures interest, agreement and readiness to a bigger ask.
**Structure**: `How do you feel about {proposal/idea} so far?`
> ****

##### How do you feel about {proposal / idea / what we've covered} so far?

##### Examples
- "How do you feel about what we've discussed so far?”
- On a scale from 1 to 10, how well does this solve your current problem?”
- How do you see this fitting into your current workflow?
- Where do you see this having the biggest impact?
- Compared to how you're doing it today, how much improvement do you see?
- On a scale from 1-10, how much of a priority is solving this?
- What would you need to see to feel completely comfortable?
- Does this support the goals you've described?

#### Why It Works

##### Reveals Reality Before It's Too Late
Most salespeople wait until the close to find out whether the prospect is with them.
Trial closes check in throughout the conversation. This lets us course-correct in real time rather than hitting "let me think about it" at the very end.

##### The Answer Tells You Everything
A strong answer: "This is exactly what I've been looking for" tells you to keep moving with confidence.
A weak answer: "It sounds interesting" tells you there's more work to do.
A deflected answer: "I'm not sure yet" tells you there's something unspoken.
Each response gives you information you can use.

##### Creates Ownership
When someone evaluates how they feel about something, they stop being a passive listener and start forming a position.
Once they've expressed any level of investment, they have something at stake. That ownership makes the final close feel natural.

##### Trains for the Close
Trial closes are small rehearsals. Each time someone answers favorably, they practice saying yes.
By the time we ask for the real decision, the pattern feels familiar.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Reveals genuine interest level before the final close
- Creates checkpoints to catch problems early
- Encourages the prospect to begin forming and owning a position
- Makes the final close feel like a natural next step, not a sudden ask
- Gives permission to surface and handle concerns before they become deal-breakers

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "What made you decide to take this call today?" |
| Discover | "Does any of what you've described feel like it points toward a specific root cause?" |
| Clarify | "How does that compare to how you thought about the problem coming in?" |
| Expand | "How much more painful does this feel now that we've put a number to it?" |
| Commit | "If we could deliver what we've talked about, how close to a yes would that get you?" |
| Resolve | "Now that we've addressed that, how are you feeling about moving forward?" |

## When Would NOW  [Commitment]
**Summary**: Makes the present the most attractive time to act
**Structure**: `When would now be a good time to {desired action}?`
> ****

##### When would now be a good time to {desired action}?

##### Examples
- When would now be a good time to start increasing revenue?
- When would now be a good time to start recovering that lost opportunity?
- When would now be a good time to build the business?
- When would now be a good time to create the future you want?
- When would now be a good time to hire the help you've already determined you need?
- When would now be a good time to begin saving money? After you've become financially free?
- When would now be a good time to start creating content? After you've built an audience?

#### Why It Works

##### "Now" Is Already in the Question
The structure presupposes that "now" is on the table.
We're not asking "should we do this?" We're asking "when would now be a good time?"

##### Buries the Real Question
The real question is: "Will you do this?"
"When would now be a good time?" sounds like a scheduling question. But it functions as a close.

##### Creates Urgency Without Pressure
Direct urgency feels like demand: "You need to decide today."
This question creates urgency through structure, not pressure.
The word "now" is there, but the question is still soft. The listener doesn't feel pushed but "now" has been planted.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates urgency without applying direct pressure
- Presupposes that action will occur
- Surfaces hidden objections about timing
- Buries the close inside a softer scheduling question
- Moves the conversation from "if" to "when"

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Commit | "When would now be a good time to explore whether there's a solution worth investing in?" |
| Decide | "When would now be a good time to get started?" |
| Resolve | "When would now be a good time to move past the hesitation and make a real decision?" |

## Cost Conscious  [Constraints]
**Summary**: Exposes the hidden costs involved with inaction.
**Structure**: `How much is {problem} costing you in {money/time/opportunity}?`
> ****

#### How much is {problem} costing you in {money/time/opportunity}?

##### Examples
How much revenue are you losing because follow-up isn't happening consistently?
What do you estimate this inefficiency costs the company each year?
How much growth are you sacrificing to avoid discomfort?
How much attention is this stealing from more important things?
How much is this delaying the future you're working toward?
If nothing changes, how expensive does this become?
You mentioned this issue costs about five lost deals per month. At your average deal size, what is that worth annually?
You said the team spends ten hours a week manually doing this. What does that add up to over a year?
You said you've been dealing with this for three years. If you multiply the monthly cost by thirty-six months, what number do you get?

#### Why It Works

##### Hidden Costs Become Visible
Most people know they have a problem.
Few people know what that problem costs.
This pattern turns an invisible problem into a visible number.

##### Converts Abstract Feeling Into Painful Realities
People can ignore frustration.
People can ignore inconvenience.
People have a harder time ignoring:
> "$312,000."

##### Ownership of the Conclusion
You are not telling them what the problem costs.
They calculate it themselves.
People argue less with numbers they generated.

##### Turns a Static Problem Into a Growing Problem
Most people think about the current cost.
This pattern forces them to think about the accumulated cost.

##### Opportunity Cost
The pattern does not just measure losses.
It measures what could have existed instead.
The question becomes:
> What am I giving up by keeping this problem?

##### Makes Inaction Expensive
Many prospects focus on the cost of change.
This pattern shifts attention to the cost of staying the same.

##### Creates Financial Contrast
Once the problem has a price tag, future investments get compared against the problem instead of against zero.
A $10,000 solution feels different after someone realizes the problem is costing $180,000 per year.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Quantifies the pain.
- Creates urgency.
- Increases ownership.
- Makes inaction expensive.
- Creates a business case for change.
- Helps justify investment.
- Exposes hidden opportunity costs.
- Turns vague frustration into concrete numbers.

#### Variations
You mentioned your average customer stays six months. If better onboarding kept them for eight months instead, what would those extra two months be worth?
---
You told me your best engineer spends half his week fixing support tickets. What projects aren't getting built because of that?
---
You said you've wanted to launch this course for four years. If it could have generated just $15,000 a month, what opportunity have those four years cost you?

##### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Discover | What problems do you think costs you the most if they aren’t fixed?  |
| Clarify | You said every proposal takes three hours to build. At twenty proposals a month, how much time are you spending every year creating proposals? |
| Expand | You mentioned losing three employees last year because managers weren't trained properly. What did recruiting, onboarding, and lost productivity cost altogether? |
| Commit | You estimated this problem costs about $120,000 a year. If I had a solution that was a fraction of that investment, would you like to hear about it?  |
| Decide | If this problem costs $8,000 per month and the solution costs less than two months of the problem, is that enough for you to sign up right now?  |
| Resolve | You mentioned the investment feels expensive. You also mentioned you are losing $240,000 a year by not solving this problem. Everything is expensive. Which expensive do you want is the question.  |

## Could You Live With It?  [Constraints]
**Summary**: Reframes “failure” into something more valuable.
**Structure**: `If all that happened was {”worst” best-case}, would that still be acceptable?`
> ****

#### If all that happened was {”worst” best-case}, would that still be acceptable?

##### Examples
- If your first launch only breaks even and you don’t turn a profit until your second launch, is that something you could handle?
- If the worst case is you ask for a refund after giving this an honest try, could you live with that? 
- If the worst thing that happens is you get half the result in twice the time, would that be acceptable? 

#### Why It Works

##### Lowers the Decision Threshold
Most people evaluate opportunities against the best-case scenario.
This pattern gets them evaluating against the worst acceptable scenario.
The standard for moving forward becomes much easier to satisfy.

##### Removes Perfection
People often reject opportunities because they are uncertain they'll achieve the maximum result.
This pattern asks:
> What if you only achieved part of it?
Many objections disappear when perfection is removed from the equation.

##### Creates Favorable Risk Assessment
The prospect naturally starts comparing:
- downside
- minimum outcome
instead of:
- downside
- maximum outcome
The opportunity feels safer.

##### Future Regret Prevention
The question quietly asks:
> Would future-you still be glad you did this?
This shifts attention from immediate uncertainty to long-term satisfaction.

##### Acceptance Through Contrast
The listener usually enters the conversation thinking about the biggest promise with skepticism. 
You sidestep that skepticism but focusing on a “lesser” promise - but still a promise that would get them to want to buy. 
You leave open the idea that they could do more… but even if they did the least, it’s still enough.
The smaller outcome often feels surprisingly attractive by comparison.

##### Shrinks the Fear of Failure
It’s not failure per se we are afraid of - it’s complete failure. 
This pattern removes the possibility of "complete failure" and allows for failure to be okay.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Reduces perceived risk.
- Lowers resistance.
- Creates a more favorable comparison.
- Makes action feel safer.
- Helps prospects define success.
- Prevents all-or-nothing thinking.
- Reduces fear of disappointment.
- Creates acceptance of realistic outcomes.

#### Strong Variations
If the only thing this hiring process accomplished was helping you avoid one bad management hire next year, would that justify putting it in place?
---
Let's say your YouTube channel never hits a million subscribers.
If all it did was consistently send you three qualified buyers a week, would that still be valuable?
---
Let's assume you never sell the company for eight figures.
If all this did was let you stop working weekends and make it to your daughter's soccer games, would that still be a win?
---
If the only thing this AI agent did was helped you do was save ten hours a week on repetitive work, would that still be worth learning?
---

|---|---|
| Connect | Some days I want to be a billionaire but other days I think if I just get a bit more clarity on what to do, then that’s more than enough. Am I crazy?  |
| Discover | I'm going to be on the hunt for ways to triple your business, reduce your overhead by 50% or more, and free up 10 hours of your time per week. If all we did was grow your new product line revenue by 10%, would that make you happy?  |
| Clarify | If you didn't go viral but instead consistently got one new client a week, would that be a sufficient consolation prize?  |

| Resolve | You said you're unsure whether you'll get the full result.  Fair enough.  If all you achieved was cutting customer churn in half, instead of eliminating it, would you still be happy you moved forward? |

## Disappearing Act  [Constraints]
**Summary**: Weakens the excuse that would create a no.
**Structure**: `If you could eliminate only one {issue} about {situation}, what would it be?`
> ****

##### If you could eliminate only one {issue} about {situation}, what would it be?

##### Examples
- If there was one thing we could make disappear, what would it be? 
- If the biggest problem you faced with this were to disappear tomorrow, what specifically would go away? 
- If there was one specific issue that you no longer had to deal with anymore, what would it be? 

#### Why It Works

##### Loss Aversion
We are generally more motivated to avoid pain than pursue pleasure.
When you ask someone what they want to eliminate, you immediately move the conversation toward something emotionally charged.
The answer often contains more energy than asking:
> What do you want?
Because we're asking:
> What do you want to stop suffering through?

##### Thinking in Subtraction
Most people think in addition.
Need more leads.
Need another employee.
Need another software tool.
Need another strategy.
Need another hire.
This pattern forces the mind into subtraction.
Instead of asking:
> What do we add?
We ask:
> What do we remove?
That often produces better answers because many problems are caused by excess rather than deficiency.

##### Surfaces Hidden Frustrations
People often tolerate things for so long they stop noticing them.
The irritation becomes normal.
This pattern brings attention back to it.
Suddenly they realize:
> I've been dealing with this stupid problem every day for four years.
The question exposes pain that had faded into the background.

##### Creates Emotional Clarity
Most people have ten problems.
Very few problems bother them equally.
This pattern forces prioritization.
We discover what truly irritates them most.

##### Makes the Problem Concrete
When someone says:
> I'd eliminate the constant interruptions from my team.
The problem becomes real.
Specific.
Observable.
Now we have something we can work with.

##### Creates Future Contrast
The moment someone identifies what they would eliminate, they automatically begin imagining life without it.
That contrast creates desire.
They briefly experience relief before the problem has even been solved. 
Now they crave that relief and are more likely to invest in something that will give it to them.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Identifies the biggest frustration.
- Creates emotional engagement.
- Surfaces hidden pain.
- Forces prioritization.
- Creates future contrast.
- Highlights opportunity costs.
- Shifts thinking from addition to subtraction.
- Makes the value of removal visible.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | If one thing could disappear from your sales process tomorrow, where would you start? |
| Clarify | You mentioned growth has slowed. If you could remove one obstacle causing that slowdown, what would it be? |
| Commit | If solving this one problem removed 80% of the frustration you've described, would that be worth prioritizing? |
| Decide | If moving forward meant eliminating the exact issue you've spent the last hour talking about, what would stop you from signing up right now? |
| Resolve | If we could remove your concern about implementation, would there be any reason not to proceed? |

## Minimum Acceptable Outcome  [Constraints]
**Summary**: Embeds several big claims into an otherwise reasonable statement or question.
**Structure**: `If this didn't {extraordinary outcome(s)} and all it did was {modest but valuable outcome}, would that still be worth it?`
> ****

##### If this didn't {extraordinary outcome(s)} and all it did was {modest but genuinely valuable outcome}, would that still be worth it?

##### Examples
If this didn't double your revenue, increase your team's productivity, and free up ten hours a week...
and all it did was help you consistently generate 10 more qualified leads per week...
would that be worth it?
---
Suppose the universe was feeling stingy and our campaigns didn't double your sales, dramatically grow your audience, and dominate the market...
and all they did was produce customers profitably...
would that be acceptable?
---
If this didn't completely transform your life, unlock your full potential, and eliminate every limiting belief...
and all it did was help you keep promises to yourself...
would that be valuable?
---
If this initiative didn't completely transform the culture, eliminate turnover, and create perfect alignment...
and all it did was improve communication...
would that justify doing it?
---
If this project didn't produce every outcome we're discussing...
and all it did was uncover a million dollars in hidden opportunity...
would it have more than paid for itself?

#### Why It Works

##### Big Claims Are Hidden Inside Small Ones
The pattern makes a large claim feel conservative by embedding it inside a larger one.
"If this didn't completely transform your pipeline... and all it did was add ten qualified calls per week..."
Ten qualified calls per week is not a small outcome. 
But after "completely transform," it sounds modest.
The anchor does the work.

##### Bypasses Skepticism
Direct big claims trigger doubt. "This will transform your business" is easy to reject.
"This will add ten qualified calls per week" is much harder to reject, and it arrives after we've already acknowledged what we're not promising.
The listener's skepticism burns up on the first claim, leaving the second one unguarded.

##### Removes the Fear of Failure
Many people hesitate because they're afraid of not achieving the maximum result.
This pattern reframes the floor. Even the worst case is still valuable.
The fear of failure loses power when the minimum outcome is still clearly worth it.

##### Creates a Believability Ladder
The structure moves from "big and maybe unbelievable" to "small and obviously true."
By the time we land on the modest claim, the listener is relieved: "That seems totally reasonable." And reasonable is easy to say yes to.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Embeds large claims inside modest-sounding questions
- Bypasses skepticism by acknowledging limitations upfront
- Lowers the perceived risk of moving forward
- Makes even a partial outcome feel worth the investment
- Creates contrast that makes the modest claim feel like a bargain
- Encourages commitment by reducing the cost of being wrong

#### Strong Variations
- "Forget everything I said about the upside. If all this did was {one specific outcome}, would it be worth the investment?"
- "Let's say you only got 20% of the result we talked about. Would that still move the needle for you?"
- "What if this only worked half as well as I described? Would that still beat where you are now?"

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Expand | "If this never became a big revenue driver and only saved you twelve hours a week… would you still want to do it?" |
| Decide | "If this didn't exceed every expectation and all it did was deliver a solid 3x return on what you put in — would you move forward today?" |
| Resolve | "If we couldn't fix everything right away and all we did in the first 90 days was stop the bleeding — would that justify getting started?" |

## Move Away From  [Constraints]
**Summary**: Uses loss aversion to uncover what motivates the listener.
**Structure**: `What is it you don't want {to happen / to continue / to experience}?`
> ****

##### What is it you don't want {to happen / to continue / to experience}?

##### Examples
- What is it you don't want to happen with this project?
- What kinds of solutions are you specifically trying to avoid?
- What would be the worst-case outcome if nothing changed?
- What would make you regret staying with the current approach?
- What problems are you tired of waking up to?
- What do you not want to be spending your time on?
- What part of running the business drains you the most?
- What would success prevent?
- If you look back a year from now, what problem would you be disappointed to still have?
- What happens if this never gets solved?

#### Why It Works

##### Loss Aversion Hits Harder Than Desire
Losing something hurts more than gaining the same thing feels good.
Most persuasion focuses on what someone wants. This pattern focuses on what they want to avoid.
That negative motivation often contains more energy than the positive alternative.

##### We Know Our Pain Better Than Our Goals
Ask someone what they want and they might struggle to articulate it clearly.
Ask someone what they don't want and they almost always have an immediate answer.
Pain is concrete. Goals are abstract.
This pattern gives access to the most vivid part of the prospect's experience.

##### Creates Emotional Intensity
When we describe what we don't want, we often re-experience it to some degree.
The emotional intensity that comes with re-experiencing makes the conversation more urgent.
Now we're not talking about a theoretical future. We're talking about escape from something real.

##### Reveals the True Motivation
Sometimes what someone says they want and what's actually driving them are different.
The pain they want to avoid tells the real story.
A prospect might say they want more revenue, but what they actually want is to stop lying awake wondering if they'll make payroll.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Accesses loss aversion as a primary motivator
- Reveals pain that's more emotionally real than aspirational goals
- Creates urgency around avoiding a specific outcome
- Surfaces true underlying motivation
- Produces more vivid, specific answers than goal-oriented questions
- Connects the solution to relief rather than just gain

#### Variations
- "What's the worst version of where this ends up if you don't do anything?"
- "What's the outcome you're most committed to avoiding as you go into next year?"
- "What keeps you up about this when you let yourself think about it?"

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | "What's the version of this problem you're most tired of dealing with?" |
| Expand | "What happens to the business if this still isn't fixed eighteen months from now?" |
| Decide | "What are you most afraid of if you don't take action today?" |
| Resolve | "What's the worst case you're protecting yourself against by not deciding right now?" |

## Reason Not to?  [Constraints]
**Summary**: Reveals the limitation that would stop the action you want to occur.
**Structure**: `Is there any reason we couldn’t {action} right now?`
> ****

#### Is there any reason we couldn’t {action} right now?

##### Examples
- Is there any reason we couldn't get started today?
- Is there any reason we couldn't move forward right now?
- Is there anything preventing us from getting this implemented?
- Is there any reason we shouldn't take the next step?
- Is there any reason we couldn't begin getting these results now?
- Is there any reason we couldn't start moving toward that goal today?
- Is there anything preventing us from putting a plan in motion?
- Is there any reason we couldn't begin creating those outcomes immediately?
- Is there anything stopping us from making progress?
- Is there any reason we couldn't start solving this problem now?
- Is there any reason we couldn't begin capturing those opportunities immediately?
- Is there anything preventing us from executing the strategy we've agreed upon?
- Is there any reason we shouldn't move from discussion to implementation?
- Is there anything standing between the plan and execution?

##### Variations
- Is there anything standing between {plan} and {execution}?
- Is there any reason we couldn't {action}?
- Is there anything preventing us from {action}?
- Is there any reason we shouldn't move from {discussion} to {implementation}?

#### Why It works
1. Flips the Burden of Proof Traditional closing questions (e.g., "Are you ready to sign?") demand a positive "Yes," which naturally triggers a buyer's instinct to hesitate or put up their guard. By asking if there is a reason not to begin, you place the burden on finding a legitimate obstacle. If there isn't a solid reason to wait, moving forward becomes the most logical path.
2. Uncovers True Objections When a prospect says they aren't ready, it often masks a deeper hesitation (fear of failure, confusion, or a lack of understanding). Asking this question forces the other party to articulate their actual worries. Instead of dealing with vague pushback, you are handed the exact roadmap of what you need to resolve to get them to a "Yes".
3. Prevents Procrastination Human psychology is governed by inertia. Once you agree to start, the friction of taking that first small step disappears. This question disarms the "analysis paralysis" or the habit of deferring decisions to a later date by highlighting that there is no structural, logical barrier to taking action now.  
4. Establishes Collaborative Control Rather than sounding like a pushy salesperson or an demanding authority figure, this question positions you as a problem-solver. It gives the other person a sense of autonomy and control over the situation because you are handing them the opportunity to halt the process if they truly feel the timing isn't right.
5. Uses the Principle of Consistency: We desire to stay aligned with what we’ve already said or done. When someone fails to come up with a valid reason to wait, they logically commit to the path of action.

##### Psychological Mechanisms
1. Presupposition (You will start today if we can remove the constraint)
1. Embedded Command (Any reason we couldn’t get started today?)
1. Forces Specificity (”I’m not sure” → “I need to talk to my spouse”)

---

| Stage | Pattern | Specific Example |
|---|---|---|
| Connect | Is there any reason to think now is not the time to have this conversation?  | We have 45 minutes blocked on our calendar. Is there anything that could interfere with you giving your full attention to the meeting today?  |
| Discover | Is there anything preventing you from getting {result}? | You mentioned that you'd like to go from $100,000 a month to $250,000 a month. Is there anything preventing you from doing that within the next quarter?  |
| Clarify | When you say {information} is there anything stopping you from {action}? | When you say you can't find the talent, is there anything stopping you from hiring a recruiting agency to find it for you?  |
| Expand | Earlier you mentioned {information}. Would there be anything preventing you from {action}? | You mentioned you needed to run more ads. Is there anything preventing you from simply doubling the amount of creatives you make every week?  |
| Commit | Is there any reason we couldn’t go into the specific ways in which our firm can help you with {action}? | Is there any reason we couldn't go into the specific ways in which our firm can help you generate a more predictable flow of qualified leads? |
| Decide | Is there any reason why {objection} would prevent you from {result}? | Is there any reason beyond the guarantee that you feel puts you at a risk which would stop you from signing up right now?  |

## Claim Softening  [Contrast]
**Summary**: Makes a compelling claim more easy to accept.
**Structure**: `I'm not saying {big claim}. I'm merely suggesting {smaller believable claim}.`
> ****

#### I'm not saying {big claim}. I'm merely suggesting {smaller believable claim}.

##### Examples
I'm not saying you'll double your revenue in the next 30 days without spending another dollar on marketing.
I'm merely suggesting you could discover a way to add an extra million dollars a year to the business using assets you already have.
---
I'm not saying you'll eliminate every bottleneck in your business within the next month without hiring anyone.
I'm merely suggesting you can identify the one bottleneck that's creating most of the frustration and quickly eliminate it.
---
I'm not saying you'll lose 50 pounds in the next 60 days without changing your diet.
I'm merely suggesting this could be the last weight-loss program you ever need because it finally gives you a way of eating you can sustain for the rest of your life.
---
I'm not saying you'll solve every problem in your life by the end of the week without making difficult decisions.
I'm merely suggesting you could gain the kind of clarity that makes many of those problems far easier to solve.
---
I'm not saying your next video will get ten million views by tomorrow without understanding the algorithm.
I'm merely suggesting you could discover a repeatable framework that makes your future content consistently outperform your past content by 10x, 20x, 50x or more. 

#### Why It Works

##### Contrast Compression
The brain struggles to evaluate claims in isolation.
It evaluates them relative to something else.
The massive claim becomes the measuring stick that makes the smaller (yet still big) claim feel reasonable by comparison.

##### Believability Transfer
The listener unconsciously evaluates two claims:
- The outrageous claim
- The moderate claim
Even if the first claim gets rejected, the second inherits credibility simply because it is less extreme.

##### Objection Preemption
Instead of waiting for:
> "That sounds too good to be true."
You say it for them.
The prospect feels understood because the skepticism they were about to express has already been acknowledged.

##### The Double Bind
Both outcomes work.
If they reject the large claim:
> "Okay, but the smaller claim seems realistic."
You win.
If they accept the large claim:
You also win.
The listener is pushed toward acceptance regardless of which interpretation they choose.

##### Anchor Inflation
The first claim inflates the perceived ceiling.
Once the ceiling becomes enormous, even an objectively large claim can feel conservative.
For example:
> I'm not saying you'll make $10 million.
> I'm merely suggesting you could add an extra $1 million.
A million dollars is an enormous claim.
Yet after $10 million appears first, it feels modest.

##### Safety Through Retreat
The pattern feels like you're backing away.
Psychologically, people trust someone who appears to be retreating more than someone who appears to be advancing.

##### Embedded Command
> I’m not saying you will double your revenue in the next 60 days. I’m merely suggesting… 

##### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Connect | I'm not saying we're going to solve everything on this call. I am confident we can figure out how to solve this lead problem though.  |
| Discover | I can’t claim I know the exact answer for your business yet. I am reasonably sure though that we will figure out some easy answers though to really help you move the needle…  |
| Expand | I’m not saying it will be impossible for you to solve this problem on your own. I am suggesting though that we have to move some significant resources over to properly manage it.  |
| Commit | I’m not saying you have to give me all your business immediately after I show you what I can do for you. I’m merely suggesting we explore options to see which one is the right first step.  |
| Decide | I’m not saying this in itself will triple your lead flow instantly. I am saying that this is the best deal out there for you to start with right away…  |
| Resolve | I’m not suggesting that every single client who buys becomes a 100% exceptional success story that gets talked about for years and years. What I am saying is that when you commit to a bigger future, you unlock new capabilities that you previously didn't know existed.  |

## Echoing  [Contrast]
**Summary**: Allows hidden information to easily come to the surface.
**Structure**: `{Prospect statement}" → {Key phrase}?`
> ****

##### {Prospect statement}" → {Key phrase} ?

##### Examples
“It’s too expensive!”  “it’s… too expensive?”
"I don't have time." "You don't have time?"
"That would never work." "Never work?"
"I'm not ready." "Not ready?"

#### Why It Works

##### Avoids Defending
Most people hear an objection and immediately defend themselves.
> That's not true.
> Let me explain.
> Here's why you're wrong.
The moment we do that, we've accepted their frame.
Now we're reacting.
Now we're defending.
Now they're controlling the direction of the conversation.
Echoing does the opposite.
Instead of defending, we remain curious.
The burden shifts back to them.

##### Forces Elaboration
Most objections are conclusions.
Not explanations.
When someone says:
> It's too expensive.
We don't actually know what that means.
Compared to what?
Too expensive for whom?
Too expensive relative to the value?
Too expensive relative to their budget?
Too expensive right now?
The echo naturally encourages them to continue.
And often the next sentence contains the real information of what you need to address to move the conversation forward.

##### Separates Emotion From Logic
Many objections are emotional reactions delivered as logical conclusions.
Someone hears a price.
Feels discomfort.
Then says:
> It's too expensive.
The statement feels certain because the emotion feels certain.
Echoing slows the process down.
Now they have to explain the statement.
As they explain it, they often begin examining their own reasoning.
The emotional reaction becomes a discussion.
Not a knee-jerk conclusion. 

##### Encourages Self-Correction
People frequently modify their position when given space.
Prospect:
> It's too expensive.
Echo:
> Too expensive?
Prospect:
> Well, I get why you priced it that way because the value is there. But it’s just out of our budget… 
Now we're finally discussing the real issue.

##### Buys Time
This pattern also gives you a moment to think.
No scrambling.
No panic.
No rushing into a wrong response.

##### Creates Reflection
The prospect hears their own words again but spoken by someone else… 
That alone can change how they think about what they just said.
Many objections sound stronger inside the prospect's head than they do when reflected back to them.

##### Delivery is Important
The prospect says:
> "It's too expensive."
When we echo it back, we keep the words:
> "It's... too expensive?"
But change:
- the volume (lower)
- pacing (slower)
- intonation (asked as a question)
And we use a strategic pause. 
Prospect:
> "It's too expensive."
You:
> "It's..."
pause
> "...too expensive?"
The pause introduces doubt. 
So much of how we deliver persuasion patterns effects how well they are received. 
Just from this one technique, look at all the non-verbal elements we use:

| Delivery Element | Prospect | Echo | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume | "IT'S TOO EXPENSIVE." | "It's too expensive?" | Loud statements sound certain and emotionally charged. Lowering your volume removes intensity and makes the objection sound less absolute. |
| Pace | "It's too expensive." (fast) | "It's... too expensive?" (slow) | Fast speech signals certainty and reaction. Slowing down signals consideration and causes the prospect to reconsider what they just said. |
| Pause | "It's too expensive." | "It's..." pause "...too expensive?" | The pause creates doubt with the implication: “I’m searching to even begin to understand how you could think this!?” |
| Inflection | ↓ "It's too expensive." | ↑ "It's too expensive?" | You can turn any statement into a question if you add an upward inflection to it. |
| Rhythm | One continuous burst | Broken into pieces | A continuous delivery feels automatic and emotional. Breaking the rhythm forces the prospect to hear the statement differently than when they originally said it. |
| Emotional Tone | Frustration | Curiosity | Matching frustration escalates tension. Replacing it with curiosity helps the prospect shift from emotion into explanation. |
| Certainty Level | "I know this is true." | "Is this actually true?" | Every delivery change subtly lowers certainty and increases examination. The objection becomes a hypothesis instead of a fact. |

##### Volume changes meaning
When you saying something quieter, it doesn’t sound as serious. 

##### Transfers the Burden of Proof
The moment you start explaining, you're carrying the burden.
You now have to prove why they're wrong.
The echo quietly hands the burden back.
Prospect:
> "I don't have time."
Echo:
> "You don't have time?"
Now they must explain.
And often their explanation reveals the real issue.

##### Creates a Different Internal Experience
One of the most fascinating things about this pattern is that the prospect hears their own words delivered back differently.
For the first time, they experience their statement from another perspective.
Sometimes they immediately soften it.
> "Well, it's not that I don't have time..."
> "Well, it's not really too expensive..."
> "I guess I shouldn't say never..."
The objection starts collapsing without you attacking it.

##### Authority Through Calmness
Weak communicators react.
Strong communicators investigate.
When someone throws an objection on the table and you immediately defend yourself, it signals:
> That objection affected me.
When you calmly echo it back, it signals:
> Let's look at that more closely.
The calmness creates authority.
The curiosity creates control.
The prospect feels heard rather than challenged.

##### The Formula
The pattern is not:
> Repeat what they said.
The pattern is:
> Repeat what they said with less certainty than they used when saying it.
They deliver certainty.
You deliver curiosity.
They deliver emotion.
You deliver reflection.
They deliver a conclusion.
You deliver a question.
That's why the exact same words can produce an entirely different conversation.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Prevents defensive selling.
- Keeps control of the conversation.
- Encourages elaboration.
- Reveals hidden objections.
- Lowers emotional intensity.
- Creates reflection.
- Buys thinking time.
- Encourages self-correction.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | "I've been burned before." → "Burned before?" |
| Clarify | "The team just isn't accountable." → "Not accountable?" |
| Expand | "It's probably going to get worse over time." → "Get worse?" |
| Commit | "I should probably do something about it." → "Probably?" |
| Resolve | "I just don't know if it'll work for me." → "Work for you?" |

## This vs That  [Contrast]
**Summary**: Reframes problems by comparing interpretatoins, meanings, or alternatives
**Structure**: `Is it {current explanation} or {alternative explanation}?`
> ****

##### Is it {current explanation / framing} or {alternative explanation / framing}?

##### Examples
- too expensive? Compared to what?
- It feels risky? Is this the riskiest thing you’ve ever thought about?
- I’m not sure if it’s needed right now. What do you know for certain IS needed right now?
- Is it fear of failure that holds you back or something else? Perhaps it’s fear of success.
- If not this, then what?
- If not now, when?
- What’s more important, what it costs or what it can do for you?

#### Why It Works

##### Forces a Comparison
Most statements live in isolation. This pattern forces two interpretations into the same space.
When we compare them side-by-side, weak interpretations collapse.
"Is it a lead problem or a close problem?" creates a question that wasn't there before and now the listener actually has to think, not just react.

##### Breaks the Default Interpretation
Most people accept the first way they framed a problem.
This pattern interrupts that default. It says: there is another way to see this.
And because it's framed as a question, the listener investigates rather than defends.

##### Changes What Gets Evaluated
"Is it expensive, or is it a question of return on investment?"
Now the listener is no longer evaluating price. They're evaluating return.
Changing what's being measured changes the conclusion.

##### Creates Curiosity Without Confrontation
Direct challenges create defensiveness. This pattern creates curiosity.
"Is it X or Y?" feels like an honest question, not an attack. The listener starts genuinely wondering which one is more accurate.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Disrupts the default interpretation of a problem
- Forces comparison between two framings
- Shifts what's actually being evaluated
- Creates curiosity without triggering defensiveness
- Opens space for a reframe without requiring agreement upfront
- Softens resistance by presenting options rather than corrections

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "Is this the first time you've looked for a solution to this, or have you tried before?" |
| Discover | "Is the real problem lead volume, or what happens to leads once they come in?" |
| Clarify | "Is that a real constraint, or a proxy for something else you're worried about?" |
| Expand | "Is this costing you money, or is it costing you the momentum you could have had by now?" |
| Resolve | "Is this a no, or is it a 'not yet'? Because those require very different conversations." |

## Utility Test  [Contrast]
**Summary**: Evaluates whether a belief, behavior, or assumption is useful.
**Structure**: `Would holding {belief} help or hinder you in achieving {goal}?`
> ****

##### Would holding {belief} help or hinder you in achieving {goal}?

##### Examples
- How many opportunities has that belief allowed you to pursue versus postpone?
- If confidence only comes from evidence, where will the evidence come from if you don't start?
- If I gave you twice as much time tomorrow, would that solve the problem?
- If the market is saturated, why are new companies becoming successful every year?
- How much of what you're trying to learn can only be learned by doing?
- How much success can you tolerate if failure is never allowed?
- Will the people whose approval you seek also live with the consequences of your decision?

#### Why It Works

##### Evaluates Usefulness, Not Truth
Most belief challenges try to prove something wrong.
This pattern doesn't.
It sidesteps the question of whether the belief is true and asks a different question entirely: is this belief useful?
Even if the belief is technically accurate, it can still be limiting. This reframe changes the whole argument.

##### Puts the Listener in the Driver's Seat
We're not saying the belief is wrong. We're asking them to evaluate it.
This preserves their autonomy. The listener doesn't feel corrected, they feel invited to think.
And when they conclude the belief isn't serving them, it's their conclusion. Not ours.

##### Introduces a New Standard
Most people evaluate beliefs on one standard: is it true?
This pattern introduces a second standard: is it useful?
Once the listener sees that truth and usefulness are different things, they often start evaluating their beliefs differently.

##### Disarms Defensive Certainty
When someone is certain of something, arguing with the certainty usually makes it stronger.
This pattern doesn't argue with the certainty. It asks whether the certainty is helpful.
That's a question even highly certain people can consider without feeling attacked.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Evaluates beliefs based on utility rather than accuracy
- Puts the listener in control of the evaluation
- Introduces a new standard alongside the existing one
- Disarms defensiveness by not attacking the belief directly
- Creates space for change without requiring admission of error
- Surfaces limiting beliefs without triggering resistance

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | "Would holding the belief that 'ads don't work in this market' help or hurt you in figuring out the real problem?" |
| Clarify | "Would believing you can't close at that price point help or hinder you in even testing whether that's true?" |
| Expand | "Would holding the belief that 'this is as good as it gets' help or hinder you in growing past where you are?" |
| Commit | "Would believing results take a year help or hinder you if they could actually come in 90 days?" |
| Resolve | "Would holding onto that concern help or hinder you in making the best choice available to you right now?" |

## Win/Lose Contrast  [Contrast]
**Summary**: Compares extreme outcomes to make the one you want most favorable.
**Structure**: `Think of a time when you {success} and a time when you {failure}. What was the difference?`
> ****

##### Think of a time when you {had a success} and a time when you {had a failure}. What was the difference?

##### Examples
- "Think of the best hire you ever made and the worst one. What was different about how you made each of those decisions?"
- "Think of a time you moved quickly on something and it paid off. Now think of a time you waited and regretted it. What separated those two situations?"
- "Think of a vendor you trusted and it worked out, and a vendor who disappointed you. Looking back, what was the real difference?"
- "Think of a year where you hit your targets and a year where you didn't. What was actually different about those two years?"
- "Think of a decision you made fast and were glad you made, versus one you kept delaying. What happened?"

#### Why It Works

##### The Listener Provides the Evidence
We're not telling them what separates success from failure. We're asking them to discover it.
And when someone discovers the answer themselves, they own it. They believe it more deeply than if we'd told them.

##### Activates Both Emotional States
By asking them to recall both experiences, we briefly activate both emotional states.
The memory of success creates confidence and possibility.
The memory of failure creates contrast and urgency.
Both emotions become available to influence the current moment.

##### Reveals Personal Decision-Making Criteria
What someone sees as the difference between success and failure tells us everything about how they make decisions.
"The difference was I trusted my gut" signals that identity is the driver.
"The difference was I had better data" signals that certainty is the driver.
The pattern gives us a map to how this specific person decides.

##### Makes the Prospect Argue for Action
When someone describes a success, they describe the conditions that created it.
Those conditions almost always involve taking action, investing, moving quickly, or committing fully.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Gets the prospect to identify the conditions for their own success
- Activates emotional memory tied to both success and failure
- Reveals personal decision-making criteria
- Creates self-generated evidence for the value of acting
- Produces contrast between what worked and what didn't
- Creates momentum by having them describe what success looks and feels like

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | "Think of a time you trusted a vendor and it worked out, and a time it didn't. What separated the two?" |
| Clarify | "Think of a time you hired well and a time you hired wrong. What was different about your process each time?" |
| Commit | "Think of the best decision you ever made for your business. What made you pull the trigger on it?" |
| Resolve | "Think of the last time you had this same fear about a decision and moved forward anyway. What happened?" |

## Cards on the Table  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Isolates all the major objections.
**Structure**: `Other than {stated objection}, is there any reason we couldn't {desired action}?`

> ****

#### Other than {stated objection}, is there any reason we couldn't {desired action}?

##### Examples:
- Other than the investment, is there any reason we couldn't move forward today?
- Besides timing, is there anything else standing in the way?
- Aside from wanting to think it over, is there any reason this wouldn't make sense?
- Outside of budget considerations, do you see any reason not to do this?
- Other than getting internal approval, is there anything preventing us from getting started?

### Why It Works
Most salespeople make the mistake of treating the first objection as the real objection.
Prospect:
> "It's too expensive."
Salesperson:
> Handles price.
Prospect:
> "I also need to talk to my partner."
Salesperson:
> Handles partner.
Prospect:
> "I'm also concerned about implementation."
Salesperson:
> Handles implementation.
Now the conversation becomes a game.
The prospect produces objections.
The salesperson tries to eliminate them.
The prospect produces another.
The salesperson eliminates another.
This creates a negative rhythm where the prospect's job is to generate resistance and the salesperson's job is to react to it.
Every objection that gets handled creates room for another objection to appear.
This persuasion pattern fixes that. 
---

#### It Changes The Objective Of The Conversation
Most objection handling asks:
> "How do I overcome this objection?"
This pattern asks:
> "Is this actually the objection?"
Before you try to solve a problem, you first determine whether it's the only problem.
This prevents solving the wrong thing.

#### It Stops The Whac-A-Mole Cycle
Once all objections are visible, the conversation changes.
Instead of:
Objection → Response → New Objection → Response
You get:
Objection A
Objection B
Objection C
Then:
> "If we solved A, B, and C, would there be any reason not to move forward?"
Now you're solving a complete set of constraints rather than chasing an endless series of them.

#### Commitment Before The Objections Are Solved
After identifying all objections, you can ask:
> "If we were able to address those concerns, could you make a yes decision?"
The prospect now commits to the logic of the decision before the objections are resolved.
This creates consistency later.
If the objections are genuinely addressed, the prospect has already agreed that buying would make sense. 
---

#### Are the Objections Genuine?
Many prospects don't intentionally hide objections.
They simply haven't thought through them.
Which is why they still have the problem and need your help with solving it.
By asking a question like: 
> Other than the investment, is there any reason we couldn't move forward today?
You can proactively figure out all the potential things that could stop a person from buying. 
---

#### Evaluate the Whole, not the Parts
Normally, prospects evaluate one concern at a time.
This pattern causes them to step back and evaluate the opportunity as a whole.
For example:
> "Other than timing, is there any reason this wouldn't make sense?"
Now they're no longer focused exclusively on one aspect of the investment. 
They're evaluating the entire decision.

#### Preempts the "Think It Over" Objection
Because you've uncovered and addressed the prospect's concerns throughout the conversation, there's far less chance of reaching the end only to hear:
> "I need to think it over."
By the time a decision is requested, the major objections have already been identified, discussed, and resolved. 
Instead of discovering new concerns at the finish line, both parties have a clear understanding of what's standing in the way before going for the close. 

#### Provides Clarity
A prospect can only be in one of three states:

##### State 1: One Objection
> "No, that's the only thing."
Excellent.
Now you know exactly what needs to be addressed.
---

##### State 2: Multiple Objections
> "Actually there are a few other things..."
Excellent.
Now you can uncover them all before attempting to solve them.
---

##### State 3: Endless Objections
Also valuable.
Because now you're discovering that the issue is lack of fit. 
You don’t want these customers. Don’t close them. Refer them to your competitors instead!

### Use Cases

| Stage | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Connect | On your intake form, you mentioned that the number one priority was getting more leads. If we uncovered powerful strategies today to do just that, would that be sufficient for us to consider this a successful interaction or do other things also need to occur?  |
| Discover | Let's pretend you woke up tomorrow and your calendar was completely full of qualified appointments for the next ninety days. That in itself would be sufficient for you to solve your growth problem or would there be additional marketing functions that would need to also happen?  |
| Clarify | If I somehow gave you an extra three hours every day starting tomorrow, would that solve the problem completely, or would there still be challenges around prioritization, delegation, or focus? |
| Expand | Let's say webinars started producing customers consistently. Would that be enough, or would you also want the ability to launch future products, test new offers, and enter new markets without wondering where the customers are going to come from? |
| Commit | We've talked about getting from roughly $80,000 a month to $200,000 a month, building a stronger acquisition system, and creating a business that doesn't depend on you personally making every decision. If I showed you a path that realistically addressed those three areas, would that be enough for you to seriously consider moving forward today, or would there be other factors we'd still need to address? |

## Emotional Labeling  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Names an unspoken issue in a way to gain more information.
**Structure**: `It seems/sounds/feels like you're {emotion}.`
> ****

##### It seems/sounds/feels like you're {emotion}.

##### Examples
- It sounds like your {emotion}.
- You’re {emotion}. 
- It seems like you’re {emotion}
- It feels like you’re {emotion}
Can be framed positively or negatively. 

##### Negative Labels
You're upset.
---
It sounds like you're disappointed.
---
It seems like you feel like nobody is listening.

##### Positive Labels
It sounds like this is very important to you.
---
It seems like this solution fits the way you naturally like to operate.
---
It feels like you're more confident than when we started this conversation.

#### Why It Works

##### Helps People Feel Understood
One of the deepest human desires is to feel understood.
Not agreed with.
Not fixed.
Understood.
When someone feels that you've accurately identified what they're experiencing, rapport increases dramatically.
They feel seen.
They feel heard.
They feel less alone in the experience.

##### If You're Right, You're Really Right
When you accurately label an emotion, something interesting happens.
The other person often expands.
> Exactly!
Or:
> That's it!
Then they elaborate.
They provide more information.
They become more open.
The label acts as proof that you're paying attention.

##### If You’re wrong, It Invites Correction
Human beings have a powerful urge to correct inaccurate descriptions of themselves.
That's why emotional labeling works even when it misses.
The label creates a response.
Either:
> Yes, that's exactly it.
Or:
> No, it's actually this.
Both outcomes move the conversation forward.

##### Reduces Defensiveness
Questions can sometimes feel interrogative.
Advice can feel judgmental.
Labels feel observational.
Instead of:
> Why are you angry?
We get:
> It sounds like you're angry.
The second version feels far less confrontational.
The person doesn't feel examined.
They feel as if you’re really trying to understand them.

##### Slows the Conversation Down
People often speak from emotion before they've fully processed it.
Labeling slows the interaction.
The conversation moves from reaction to reflection.
Instead of immediately defending their position, they begin examining it.

##### Earns the Right to Be Heard
When someone feels misunderstood, they automatically discount your advice. 
It doesn't matter how intelligent, logical, or accurate your solution is. 
Emotional labeling closes that gap.
Every accurate label communicates:
> I get it.
> I see what's really going on here.
> I understand you. 
When someone feels deeply understood, your words carry more weight because they trust that your recommendation is being made from an accurate understanding of their situation rather than from assumptions.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates rapport.
- Increases emotional awareness.
- Encourages elaboration.
- Helps people feel understood.
- Surfaces hidden concerns.
- Reduces defensiveness.
- Creates clarification.
- Slows emotional reactions.
- Builds the deepest level of trust.

**Structure**: `If {condition is true}, how important is {solution/value}?`
> ****

##### If {condition is true}, how important is {solution/value}?

##### Examples
- If something breaks at 2 a.m., how important is it to have responsive customer support?
- If leads are expensive to acquire, how important is conversion?
- If attention is scarce, how important is having a compelling message?
- If customer acquisition costs continue rising, how important is referral generation?
- If growth creates operational complexity, how important are systems?
- If implementation is important to you, how important is having a team that helps you implement?
- If things don't go perfectly… and they rarely do… how important is it to have someone in your corner?

#### Why It Works

##### Creates Value Before Presenting the Solution
Most people try to prove the value of their solution after they've presented it.
Better is to get the prospect to acknowledge the value of the ingredients before they're connected to the solution.
Instead of saying:
> "Our support team is valuable."
You ask:
> "If something breaks at 2 a.m., how important is responsive support?"
Now the prospect creates and commits to the value. 

##### Establishes Agreement
This pattern functions as a soft trial close.
Every time someone answers:
> "Very important."
They are making a small commitment.

##### Stacks Reasons to Buy
One of the most powerful uses of this pattern is repetition.
Not of the exact question.
But of the structure.
For example:
> If leads are expensive, how important is conversion?
> If conversion matters, how critical is follow-up?
> If follow-up matters, how valuable is automation?
> If automation matters, how essential is reliability?
By the time the solution is presented, the prospect has already agreed that each component is valuable.
The close becomes less about persuasion and more about connecting the dots.

##### Lets the Prospect Sell Themselves
People trust conclusions they reach themselves more than conclusions imposed upon them.
This pattern works because you aren't declaring value.
You're eliciting it.
Instead of:
> "You need this."
You ask:
> "How important is this?"
The answer comes from them.
And self-generated conclusions tend to be far more persuasive than externally imposed ones.

##### Creates Ownership
Every agreement creates a small amount of psychological ownership.
The prospect is no longer evaluating your opinion.
They're evaluating their own.
When someone says:
> "That's extremely important."
That statement now belongs to them.
Not you.

##### Builds a Logical Chain
The best versions create a sequence of agreements.
Each answer supports the next answer.
Each conclusion makes the next conclusion easier to accept.
Eventually the solution feels like a natural extension of everything they've already agreed is important.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates value before presenting a solution.
- Generates small commitments.
- Functions as a soft trial close.
- Builds agreement incrementally.
- Lets prospects persuade themselves.
- Creates ownership.
- Establishes logical buying criteria.
- Reduces resistance.
- Makes closing feel natural.

|---|---|

| Clarify | If follow-up isn't happening consistently, how important is accountability? |
| Expand | If there were a way to solve this faster, how important would speed be? |
| Commit | If implementation determines success, how important is actually taking action? |

**Summary**: Uncovers the criteria someone needs to move forward.

That conditional commitment puts them one step closer to yes before we've done any more convincing.

The answer to this question often reveals objections that would have appeared much later.
Better to find them now than at the close.
Every condition they name is an objection we can handle while we still have time.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Turns resistance into a list of addressable conditions

- Produces conditional commitments from the prospect

- Eliminates guesswork about what the prospect needs
- Creates a much cleaner path to close

## Begin → Find  [Future Pacing]
**Summary**: Creates a positive outcome associated with the action you want to occur.
**Structure**: `As you begin {action}, you'll find yourself {beneficial realization/state}.`
> ****

#### As you begin {take action} you will find yourself {feeling a certain way} 

##### Examples
- As you begin publishing your ideas, you'll realize how much expertise you've been keeping to yourself.
- As you begin raising your prices, you'll attract clients who value outcomes over discounts.
- As you begin documenting your processes, you'll remove yourself as the bottleneck.
- As you begin addressing objections, you'll stop being surprised by them.
- As you begin publishing, you'll build confidence that can't be created through preparation alone.
- As you begin showing up, you'll remove much of the pressure you've been placing on yourself.
- As you begin focusing on the solution, you'll spend less time feeding the problem.
- As you begin considering the long-term impact, you'll place less emphasis on the short-term inconvenience.
- As you begin considering what's possible here, you'll notice how small today's obstacles look compared to tomorrow's opportunities.

#### Why It Works

##### 1. Presupposes Action
Rather than asking whether someone will take action, it assumes they already have. 
Compare:
- "Will you start publishing content?"
- "As you begin publishing content..."
---

##### 2. Uses Accepted Ideas to Smuggle In Bigger Ones
The mind naturally evaluates statements from left to right.
When the beginning of a statement feels believable, resistance often decreases toward what follows.
For example:
> As you begin publishing content...
Most people can imagine themselves doing that.
Then:
> ...you'll notice how easy and natural it feels...
Still fairly believable.
Then:
> ...which allows you to create the best content you've ever produced.
Now we're making a much larger claim.
But instead of presenting that claim directly, we've attached it to two ideas that were already accepted.
The larger outcome effectively arrives under the protection of the smaller ones.
This is why hypnotic language often starts with simple, observable, or easily accepted experiences before moving toward more ambitious suggestions.
The mind tends to follow a chain of agreement further than it will leap to a conclusion.
---

##### 3. Chunks Down Commitment
The word "begin" implies:
- Small
- Easy
- Temporary
- Low risk
- Reversible
Instead of asking someone to become an expert, transform their business, or change their life, you're only asking them to begin.
This dramatically lowers resistance.
---

##### 4. Shifts Attention from "Should I?" to "What Happens Next?"
The listener stops debating the action and starts mentally experiencing the future.
---

##### 5. Creates Future Pacing
The listener mentally rehearses a future experience before it occurs.
They imagine:
- Taking the action
- Experiencing the feeling
- Receiving the result
This makes the future feel familiar and believable.
People are naturally more comfortable moving toward experiences they have already mentally rehearsed.
---

##### 6. Creates Cause-and-Effect
Humans naturally look for causal relationships.
The structure:
> As you begin X, you'll notice Y.
automatically encourages the brain to connect the two.
Without explicitly proving the relationship, the mind starts treating the action as the cause and the result as the effect.
---

##### 7. Presents the Outcome as an Observation Rather Than a Claim
Direct claims invite argument.
For example:
> This program will make you confident.
By contrast:
> As you begin using this system, you'll notice confidence.
sounds like an observation someone discovers for themselves.
Observations generate far less resistance than assertions.
---

##### 8. Uses Experience Instead of Logic
Many versions of this pattern use experiential language:
- Notice
- Discover
- Realize
- Find yourself
- Become aware
These words direct attention toward experience rather than analysis.
---

##### 9. Becomes a Self-Fulfilling Prophecy
Once you tell someone what they are likely to notice, they begin looking for evidence of it.
---

##### 10. Creates Immediate Gratification
Many worthwhile outcomes are delayed.
This pattern inserts an immediate reward.
Instead of:
Action → Months of work → Reward
The pattern creates:
Action → Feeling → Reward
For example:
> As you begin publishing, you'll notice confidence...
The confidence arrives immediately rather than months later.
This increases motivation and reduces resistance.
---

##### 12. Feels Like Discovery Rather Than Instruction
People resist being told what to think.
They rarely resist discovering something for themselves.
Compare:
> Be more confident.
vs.
> As you begin publishing, you'll notice confidence.
---

##### 13. Opens a Mental Loop
The moment someone hears:
> As you begin...
their brain naturally asks:
> Then what happens?
The rest of the sentence satisfies that curiosity.
This creates a smooth, attention-holding structure that naturally pulls people forward.
---

##### Variation
> As you begin {action} you’ll notice {feeling} which {result}
- As you begin publishing content, you'll notice how easy and natural it begins to feel, which allows you to create the best content you've ever produced and get results faster than you ever imagined.
- As you begin to use our AI agents, you'll notice a sense of wonderment at how much better and faster these agents can perform tasks than you, which will get you so excited about the new capabilities you have to grow your business 10x, 50x, or even 100x. 

### Use Cases

| Stage | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Connect | As we begin our call today, I want you to be aware that by thinking through this together, we may uncover answers that neither of us would have discovered alone. |
| Discover | As we begin this fact-finding process, I want you to know that even rough or approximate answers can lead to insights that prove valuable regardless of what happens after this call. |
| Clarify | As we compare what worked with what didn't, I'd like you to focus on what you believe were the biggest differences between the two. |
| Expand | As you begin to tell me more about what you think it would take to double your business in the next many days, I really want you to focus in on the simplest actions that are already within the capabilities of you and the team.  |
| Commit | As we explore the possibility of working together, I want you to remember that we're simply evaluating options to determine what, if anything, makes the most sense moving forward. |
| Decide | As you begin to go to the sign up page you’ll notice all the proof of our clients is available for you to look over at any time to show how confident you can be with taking the first step forward to joining us. |
| Resolve | While you're considering whether now is the right time to move forward, I'd like you to think of a time when you knew "yes" was the right decision. As you do, notice whether any of the same factors are present here today. |

## Looking Back  [Future Pacing]
**Summary**: Uses the future self to uncover insight, action and priorities in the present.
**Structure**: `Looking back after {desired success}, what do you think made the biggest difference?`
> ****

##### Looking back after {desired success}, what do you think made the biggest difference?

##### Examples
- "Imagine it's two years from now and you've hit every goal we talked about. Looking back, what do you think the turning point was?"
- "If this problem is fully solved by next quarter, what do you think you'll say made the difference?"
- "Looking back after you've doubled the business, what do you think you'll say you had to stop doing to get there?"
- "If next year is the best year your company's ever had, what decision do you think you'll be most glad you made?"
- "When this is behind you, what do you think you'll wish you had done sooner?"

#### Why It Works

##### Uses the Future Self to Inform the Present
The pattern temporarily places the listener at a point after they've already succeeded.
From that vantage point, looking back makes the decision feel not just possible but inevitable.
It's no longer "will this work?" but "what made it work?"

##### Bypasses Resistance by Skipping the Decision
We assume success and ask about what caused it.
The listener has to engage with the future rather than argue with the decision.

##### Creates Insight About What's Required
When someone describes what they think "made the difference," they often name the very thing we're offering.
Or they name the qualities they need to show up with: commitment, speed, focus.
Either way, we learn how to help them succeed.

##### Activates the Feeling of Success
By asking the question, we cause the listener to briefly simulate having achieved the goal.
That simulated success is emotionally real. It creates desire for the actual outcome and provides a preview of what's possible.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Uses a future perspective to inform present action
- Bypasses current hesitation by assuming success
- Surfaces what the prospect believes is required to succeed
- Creates emotional preview of the desired outcome
- Reveals how the prospect thinks about their own success
- Makes the path to action feel natural and inevitable

#### Variations
- "Five years from now, what decision do you think will have mattered most?"
- "Imagine this is already solved. What do you think got you there?"
- "When you've beaten this problem, what would you tell someone who's where you are today?"

## Identity Crisis  [Identity]
**Summary**: Creates friction between values and behavior.
**Structure**: `I'm surprised you're {behavior} because I've always seen you as {positive identity}.`
> ****

##### I'm surprised you're {behavior} because I've always seen you as {positive identity}.

##### Examples
- "I'm surprised you're procrastinating on this because I've always seen you as someone who takes action."
- I'm surprised you're focusing so much on the price because I've had you pegged as someone who thinks long-term.
- I'm surprised you're trying to do all of this yourself because I've always thought of you as someone who values leverage.
- I'm surprised you're accepting those results because I've always seen you as someone who pushes for more.
- I'm surprised you're playing it this safe because you've always struck me as someone willing to bet on yourself.
- I'm surprised you're waiting for the perfect time because I've always seen you as someone who creates opportunities rather than waits for them.

#### Why It Works
Most people defend their identity harder than they defend their opinions.
When we create tension between a behavior and a positive identity, we make the behavior harder to justify.

##### Positive Confrontation
Direct confrontation creates resistance.
Identity Conflict softens the confrontation by wrapping it inside a compliment.
Instead of:
"You need to quit waiting for the perfect time”
We get:
> I'm surprised you're waiting for the perfect time because I've always seen you as someone who creates opportunities rather than waits for them.
The challenge lands without triggering as much defensiveness.

##### Consistency Pressure
People naturally want their actions to match who they believe they are.
Once we establish a positive identity, inconsistent behavior starts feeling uncomfortable.
The easiest way to remove that discomfort is often to change the behavior.

##### Self-Persuasion 
Rather than us telling them why they should act differently, they begin explaining why the behavior doesn't fit who they are.
That creates far more ownership.

##### Status Protection
Most people want to see themselves as competent, thoughtful, disciplined, courageous, successful, generous, or responsible.
When a behavior threatens one of those identities, protecting status becomes a powerful motivator.

##### Cold Read Agreement
Many positive identities are nearly impossible to reject.
Most people naturally agree with statements like:
- You're someone who likes to think things through.
- You strike me as someone who cares about doing things the right way.
- I've always found you to be pretty resourceful.
- You seem like someone who values growth.
- You don't seem like the type to quit easily.
Because these identities are broadly desirable, they are often accepted without much resistance.
Once accepted, they become useful anchors for future contrast.

##### Behavior Reframing
The behavior is no longer evaluated in isolation.
Now it's evaluated relative to a larger identity.
The question becomes:
"Does this behavior fit the person I believe myself to be?"
---

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates productive tension.
- Challenges behavior without direct attack.
- Increases consistency pressure.
- Strengthens commitment to positive identities.
- Makes objections harder to justify.
- Encourages self-persuasion.
- Reframes decisions as identity choices rather than tactical choices.
---

#### Strong Variations

##### Action vs Avoidance
I'm surprised you've been sitting on this for three months because I've always seen you as someone who moves quickly when something matters.

##### Fear vs Courage
I'm surprised you're letting uncertainty stop you because you seem like someone who's built a career taking calculated risks.

##### Short-Term vs Long-Term
I'm surprised you're so focused on this month's expense because I've always thought of you as someone who plays the long game.

##### Responsibility vs Excuse
I'm surprised you're blaming the market because you've always seemed like someone who takes ownership of results.

| Expand | You told me freedom was one of your biggest goals. I'm struggling to reconcile that with staying in a situation that's requiring sixty-hour work weeks. |
| Resolve | You don't seem like someone who lets fear make important decisions. So help me understand whether this is really about risk, or if something else is bothering you. |

> ****

Most people live into predictions made confidently by someone they trust. The prediction becomes a kind of self-fulfilling prophecy.

##### Signals That We Know Them
The deeper value of this pattern is what it signals: I understand you well enough to know what you'll think before you think it.
That signal creates comfort, trust, and the sense that we're genuinely looking out for them.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates deep rapport through accurate prediction

## Positive Identity Contradiction  [Identity]
**Summary**: Contrasts self-image with behavior.
**Structure**: `I know you're someone who values {positive trait}, which is why I'm concerned about {contradictory behavior}.`
> ****

##### I know you're someone who values {positive trait}, which is why I'm concerned about {contradictory behavior}.

##### Examples
- One of the things I've always respected about you is your commitment to excellence, which is why I'm concerned about the gap between the standard you're known for and what happened here.
- I know how much you care about customer experience, which is why I'm concerned about the onboarding process, because I know first impressions influence retention.
- I know you care about making smart financial decisions, which is why I'm concerned that you're focusing exclusively on the cost, because I know smart financial decisions are usually driven by return, not price.
- I know you're someone who values growth, which is why I'm concerned about how much energy is going toward protecting the status quo, because growth and status quo rarely coexist for very long.
- I know you're committed to solving this problem, which is why I'm concerned that we're spending more time discussing whether to solve it than how to solve it.
- I know you're someone who takes action when the numbers make sense, which is why I'm curious why we're still talking about whether to move forward when we've already agreed the numbers work.

#### Why It Works

##### Agreement Before Challenge
The pattern establishes agreement on a positive identity before pointing to the contradiction.
The listener can't resist the opening because it's flattering and true.
By the time the contradiction arrives, they're already nodding.  

##### Contradiction Creates Cognitive Dissonance
Cognitive dissonance (holding two conflicting beliefs simultaneously) is deeply uncomfortable.
The pattern creates it on purpose.
One belief: "I value smart financial decisions."
Contradicting behavior: "I'm hesitating over a return I've already agreed makes sense."
That dissonance creates pressure to resolve, usually by changing the behavior.

##### Concern Is More Effective Than Criticism
Saying "you're being inconsistent" triggers defensiveness.
Saying "I'm concerned that this might not match what you've told me you value" triggers reflection.
One is judgment. The other is care.
The pattern delivers the same message with far less resistance.

##### Identity Is the Most Durable Motivator
When someone does something that contradicts who they believe they are, it creates internal conflict.
That conflict is one of the most powerful motivators available.
We're not asking them to change. We're asking them to be consistent with who they already are.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates cognitive dissonance between stated identity and current behavior
- Challenges behavior without a direct attack
- Uses care and concern rather than judgment
- Activates identity-based motivation
- Creates pressure to align behavior with values
- Produces self-persuasion rather than external convincing

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Expand | "I know you care about your team, which is why I'm concerned that keeping this problem in place is slowly burning the best ones out." |
| Resolve | "I know you're someone who doesn't let fear drive decisions, which is why I want to understand what's actually causing the hesitation here." |

## Who / If?  [Identity]
**Summary**: Removes limiting beliefs and constrasts through identity.
**Structure**: `Who would you be if you no longer had {limiting belief/problem}?`
> ****

##### Who would you be if you no longer had {limiting belief / problem}?

##### Examples
- Who would you become if you no longer believed you needed all the answers before you started?
- How much faster would you move if you trusted yourself to figure things out along the way?
- How would you prioritize differently if more time was never coming?
- Who would you be if you no longer needed permission from other people before making a decision?
- Who would you be if you no longer believed success had to be difficult?
- What opportunities would you pursue if rejection no longer bothered you?
- How much larger would your goals become if you trusted your ability to grow into them?

#### Why It Works

##### Identity Questions Are More Powerful Than Behavior Questions
"What would you do differently?" produces a list.
"Who would you become?" produces a transformation.
Identity is deeper and more motivating than behavior.
This pattern reaches the level where real change actually happens.

##### Separates the Person from the Problem
The framing assumes the problem is external to identity.

##### The Answer Reveals the Real Desire
When someone describes who they'd be without the limitation, they often describe their ideal self.
That description is far more motivating than any outcome we could name.

##### Creates Distance from the Limitation
The question invites the listener to step outside the limitation and observe it from a distance.
Limitations feel permanent when we're inside them. Viewed from the outside, they often feel surprisingly optional.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Reaches the identity level where real change happens
- Separates the person from the limiting belief
- Reveals the listener's vision of their ideal self
- Creates psychological distance from the limitation
- Produces emotionally real answers about what freedom from the problem would feel like
- Makes the limitation feel temporary rather than permanent

## Current Action → Desired Outcome  [Linking]
**Summary**: Uses an existing behavior to make a statement more easy to accept.
**Structure**: `As/While {current action, observation, or experience}, you'll {desired thought, feeling, realization, or response}.`
> ****

#### As/While {current action, observation, or experience}, you will/may {desired thought, feeling, realization, or response}.

##### Examples
- As you look through the proposal, you'll naturally start seeing how the pieces fit together.
- As you hear the sound of my voice, you'll begin to notice certain ideas standing out.
- As you take in this information, you'll begin organizing it in your own way.
- As you shift in your chair, you'll find yourself settling into the discussion.
- As you notice the rhythm of the conversation, you'll become more engaged.
- As you listen to these words, you can allow your own experience to teach you something.
- And while the projector boots back up this will allow us to have a conversation that you can find particular enlightening   {in a seminar room}
- And as you begin to doubt whether you have what it takes to do this or not, you may also begin to doubt the limitations you have previously placed on yourself and may begin to find that there are hidden possibilities inside of that are only available after you’ve doubted yourself enough to consider a new decision. 

#### Why It Works

##### Reality as Proof
The first half of the pattern is undeniably true.
We link our suggestion to something already happening.
Because the first part is true, the second part receives less scrutiny.
> As you look over the numbers, you'll start seeing where the opportunity is.

##### Present-Moment Agreement
Most persuasion tries to get agreement about the future.
This pattern gets agreement about the present.
The listener unconsciously agrees with:
> Yes, I am doing that.
That agreement creates momentum for the linked suggestion.

##### Meaning Assignment
We take a neutral behavior and assign meaning to it.
Someone leans forward.
We assign:
> interest.
Someone takes notes.
We assign:
> commitment.
Someone asks a question.
We assign:
> serious consideration.
Most behavior is ambiguous.
This pattern allows us to interpret it in a direction that supports the conversation.

##### Bypasses Debate
If we directly said:
> You're becoming more open to this idea.
That might trigger resistance.
Instead:
> As you settle into your chair, you'll probably find yourself becoming more open to some of these ideas.
The suggestion arrives indirectly.
The listener focuses on the observable behavior rather than arguing with the conclusion.

##### Creates Self-Fulfilling Momentum
Once we assign meaning to a behavior, we often begin acting consistently with that meaning.
If we hear:
> As you write that down, you'll probably realize how important this is.
Writing notes suddenly feels like evidence of importance.
The behavior starts supporting the suggestion.

##### Uses Existing Attention
Instead of fighting for attention, we use attention that already exists.
Whatever the listener is already doing becomes the vehicle for the suggestion.
The pattern works best when we attach ourselves to something that would have happened anyway.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Links reality to suggestion.
- Makes suggestions feel natural.
- Reduces resistance.
- Assigns favorable meaning to behavior.
- Creates momentum.
- Increases receptivity.
- Uses existing attention instead of demanding new attention.
- Makes desired states feel like a natural consequence of current actions.

#### Strong Variations
As you flip through the pages of the proposal, you'll probably start noticing how much of this you've already been trying to solve on your own in vein. 
---
As you pull up your calendar and look at the last few months, you'll begin seeing exactly where your time has been disappearing.
---
While you're thinking through that question, you may notice you're already coming up with the answer.
---
As you compare those numbers side by side, you'll start to see why the business has felt harder to grow than it should.
---
While you're looking at those customer reviews, you'll begin noticing the same pattern showing up again and again.
---
As you pause for a moment before answering, you'll probably discover something deeper than most people are aware of… 

#### Utilization as a Core Philosophy
Never reject the listener’s behavior. Use it. 
Accept whatever the client brings. 
If the client is resistant, use it - make them resistant toward succumbing to their limiting beliefs. 
If the client is stubborn, use it - make them stubborn against failure. 
If the client is critical, use it - get them to become critical of the ways that don’t serve them. 
Don’t fight the behavior… use it.

##### MILTON ALERT
A man came to see Milton Erickson because he couldn't sit still.
The moment he entered the office, he began pacing.
Back and forth.
Back and forth.
He walked while he talked.
He walked while he thought.
He walked while he answered questions.
And he told Erickson that no one could treat him because of how much a nervous wreck he was. 
But instead of trying to get the man to stop pacing, Erickson described exactly what was the man was doing. 
“As you walk around my office I wonder if you can still hear my voice.”
“And as you turn left and then right, would you still be able to follow my instruction?”
“And as you speed up, I wonder if you could alter the speed ever so slightly, either faster or slower.” 
He accepted the man's reality exactly as it was.
No correction.
No resistance.
No argument.
Only agreement.
After establishing that rhythm, Erickson began making tiny changes.
Nothing dramatic.
Nothing obvious.
Simple stuff - like having the man only pace in one corner of the office. And then having him sit in the chair - but only for a fraction of a second. 
Erickson never fought the behavior.
He used it.
Eventually the pacing slowed to walking. 
The walking become standing.
The standing became sitting. 
The sitting became calm conversation.
What makes this story so powerful is that Erickson never tried to impose a new reality.
He started with the reality that already existed.
The man was pacing.
That was true.
And once enough agreement had been built around what was true, Erickson could begin leading him somewhere new.

## Fact → Meaning  [Linking]
**Summary**: Uses a fact to make a claim more easy to accept.
**Structure**: `The fact that {observable fact, behavior, or question} means {meaning you want to assign}.`
> ****

##### The fact that {observable fact, behavior, or question} means {meaning you want to assign}.

##### Examples
[Verifiable fact/action] means [meaning you want to assign].
- The fact that you're asking these questions means you're taking this decision seriously.
- The fact that you're comparing options means you're planning to make a smart decision.
- The fact that you're skeptical means you're thinking instead of reacting.
- The fact that you're still on this call means this matters to you.
- The fact that you're evaluating the numbers means you're interested in getting it right.
- The fact that you're concerned about implementation means you're already imagining using it.
- The fact that you're asking about risk means you're treating this like a real opportunity.
- The fact that you're nervous means you recognize the opportunity is significant.

#### Why It Works
The pattern begins with something that is undeniably true.
> The fact that you're asking these questions...
> The fact that you're still on this call...
> The fact that you're comparing options...
The listener cannot reasonably dispute the first half of the statement because they're actively doing it.
That dramatically lowers resistance.
Next, we attach a conclusion to something that is already true.
For example:
> The fact that you're still here means this matters to you.
The first half is undeniable.
The second half becomes easier to accept because it's linked to something real.
The meaning borrows credibility from the fact.

##### Assigns Meaning to Ambiguous Behavior
Most behavior has no fixed meaning.
Someone asks a question.
What does that mean?
- curiosity?
- skepticism?
- interest?
- concern?
All are possible.
This pattern allows us to assign a meaning that supports the conversation.
> The fact that you're asking these questions means you're taking this seriously.

##### Reframes Resistance
Many behaviors that would normally be viewed negatively can be reinterpreted positively.
> The fact that you're skeptical means you're thinking carefully.
> The fact that you're taking your time means you're trying to make the right decision.
> The fact that you're challenging this means you care about getting it right.
The strongest versions assign a positive identity.
Not:
> The fact that you're asking questions means you're confused.
But:
> The fact that you're asking questions means you're being thorough.
People naturally move toward identities they like.

##### Uses Behavior as Evidence
Most persuasion requires evidence.
This pattern gets the evidence from the listener themselves.
You are not saying:
> You're a thoughtful person.
You are saying:
> The fact that you're carefully comparing options means you're thoughtful.
Now their own behavior becomes the proof.

##### Creates Self-Fulfilling Meaning
Once people accept the interpretation, they often begin acting consistently with it.
If:
> Skepticism = careful decision making
Then skepticism becomes a sign of wisdom rather than resistance.
You can close wisdom way easier than resistance.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Links reality to interpretation that you control. 
- Makes claims more believable.
- Assigns positive meaning to behavior.
- Reframes resistance.
- Creates identity reinforcement.
- Reduces defensiveness.
- Builds momentum.
- Uses the listener's own actions as evidence.

#### Variations
The fact that you're asking detailed questions about implementation means you're already thinking beyond whether this works and into how you'll use it.
The fact that you're concerned about ROI means you're treating this like an investment rather than an expense.
The fact that this problem keeps coming up means it's important enough to solve.
The fact that you're frustrated means you've outgrown your current situation.
The fact that you're nervous means you care about the outcome.
The fact that you're worried about your team means you're trying to be a good leader.
The fact that you're concerned about culture means you understand how important it is.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | The fact that you took time out of your day for this conversation means finding a solution matters to you. |
| Clarify | The fact that you unsure is a sign there is real potential here.  |
| Expand | The fact that you've tolerated this for three years means the cost may be way larger than you've realized. |
| Commit | The fact that you're exploring solutions means you're ready for something to change. |
| Resolve | The fact that you're asking these questions means you're trying to make the best decision possible. |

### Advanced Lesson
You can interpret any “fact” with any intention behind it. 
Look at how easy it is:

|---|---|---|---|
| They're asking a lot of questions. | They're difficult. | They need information. | They're taking the decision seriously. |

Now for each “resistance” you used to get, come up with a positive interpretation for it so you can get excited to address it the next time it comes up.

## Gentle Bind  [Linking]
**Summary**: Makes every choice lead to the same result.
**Structure**: `You may discover {Option A}, or you may notice {Option B}.`
> ****

##### You may discover [Big Claim], or you can simple notice [Slightly Smaller Claim].

##### Examples
- You may discover that you're far more capable than you previously believed, or you can notice simply how many challenges you've already overcome.
- You may discover that fear affects you less than it once did, or you can notice simply that you're taking action more quickly.
- You may discover that your audience responds more enthusiastically, or you can notice simply that you're communicating more clearly.
- You may discover that growth becomes easier to sustain, or you can notice simply that fewer things require your personal attention.
- You may discover that the business becomes dramatically more scalable, or you can notice simply that problems get solved faster.
- You may discover that the team becomes highly aligned, or you can notice simply that confusion decreases.
- You may discover that you've already learned a lot, or you can notice simply how much more familiar these concepts feel.
- You may discover that the outcome exceeds expectations, or you can notice simply that it delivered exactly what you hoped.
- You may discover that something important begins changing, or you can notice simply that you're thinking about things differently.
- You may discover a new understanding, or you can notice simply an old misunderstanding disappearing.
- You may discover a completely different way of approaching this, or you can notice simply that your current approach improves dramatically.

### Why It Works

##### No-Rejection Language
Most persuasive language gets rejected because it makes a claim.
> You will become more confident.
> You will understand this.
> You will be successful.
Those can all be challenged.
But those pattern instead moves into possibility.
> You may discover...
> You can notice...
> You might become aware...
The listener doesn't need to agree.
They only need to remain open.
The suggestion can be accepted.
Ignored.
Explored.
But it's difficult to directly reject.

##### Both Choices Lead Forward
> You may discover you're far more capable than you believed.
Or:
> You can notice simply how many challenges you've already overcome.
Both lead toward:
> I am more capable than I thought.
The listener receives two paths to the same conclusion.

##### The Contrast Makes Option B Easier
Option A is usually larger.
Option B is usually smaller.
For example:
> You may discover that your business becomes dramatically more scalable. Or you can notice simply that problems get solved faster.
The first statement is ambitious.
The second statement feels modest.
Reasonable.
Easy to believe.
But "Problems get solved faster" is actually a huge improvement.
It only feels smaller because it is contrasted against something even larger.

##### Acceptance Through Downgrading
Most persuasion attempts to get acceptance by increasing certainty.
This pattern gets acceptance by reducing the required commitment.
The listener can mentally downgrade.
> Maybe not that.
> But probably this.
That makes agreement much easier.

##### Both Outcomes Feel Like Wins
Neither option contains failure.
The only choice is:
> Bigger improvement.
Or:
> Smaller improvement.
The discussion is no longer:
> Will improvement happen?
The discussion becomes:
> How much improvement happens?

##### Preserves Autonomy
This language feels collaborative rather than confrontational.
The listener feels like they're reaching their own conclusion.

### What It Accomplishes
- Softens large claims.
- Makes ambitious outcomes easier to accept.
- Creates a double bind (either choice moves the listener forward).
- Uses contrast to increase believability.
- Reduces resistance.
- Preserves autonomy.
- Creates multiple paths to agreement.
- Makes meaningful improvements feel modest.
- Allows transformational claims to be introduced safely.
> ****

#### MILTON ALERT
The above pattern is what’s called a double bind. Very few people understand and properly use double binds. But if you study Erickson’s work, you see them everywhere. 
For example, Erickson often encountered  patients who insisted:
> "I can't."
Instead of arguing, he would create situations where both compliance or resistance produced the desired outcome.
A classic Ericksonian principle was:
> If they resist, use the resistance.
For example, if a patient claimed they couldn't relax, Erickson might instruct them to deliberately become even more tense.
If they complied, they became aware they had control over tension.
If they resisted the instruction, they relaxed.
Either way, progress occurred.
The most famous double bind example comes from Erickson's childhood story about the stubborn calf.
His father was pulling the calf toward the barn.
The calf resisted.
Erickson grabbed the tail and pulled away from the barn.
Now the calf had a choice:
- Resist the father.
- Resist Erickson.
Either way, the calf moved toward the barn. Erickson later described this as one of his earliest intentional uses of a double bind.

#### The Stubborn Child
Erickson frequently prescribed symptoms.
A child who constantly fought instructions might be instructed to deliberately perform the unwanted behavior under controlled conditions.
Now the child faced a dilemma.
Obeying meant disobeying.
Disobeying meant obeying.
The behavior pattern often collapsed because the structure supporting it disappeared.

#### Why Double Binds Are So Powerful

##### They Attack the Structure
Weak persuasion attacks the content.
Good persuasion attack the structure.
For example, if someone says to you:
> I lack confidence.
Most people respond:
> No you don't.
But here’s how I respond:
> How confident are you that you lack confidence?
Now the structure breaks.
To answer the question, they must acknowledge confidence.
The problem contains its own contradiction.

##### Forcing Resource Access
If someone says to you:
> I am not certain of what to do.
You can use this double bind:
> How certain are you about your uncertainty?
To answer that question, the person must temporarily access certainty.
The very resource they claim is missing.
The pattern forces them into the state they're denying.
They have certainty after all. And if they have enough of it, they can now make a decision. 

##### The Symptom Becomes the Solution
When someone says to me:
> I procrastinate.
I respond:
> Great. Let's procrastinate on procrastinating.
The symptom is now aimed at itself.
Which makes it harder to use it as a limiting belief.
Most limiting beliefs operate because nobody examines them.
Double binds force conflicting truths into the same space.
Consider:
> I can't say no.
And your response:
> Then say no to me right now.
Now both choices challenge the original belief.
The mind has to reorganize.
The old pattern can no longer operate normally.

## Implication Frame  [Linking]
**Summary**: Uses truisms to empower someone to a better behavior or belief.
**Structure**: `If [verifiable fact] is true for you, it means [meaning you want to assign].`
> ****

##### If [verifiable fact] is true for you, it means [meaning you want to assign].

##### Examples
If [verifiable fact] is true for you, it means [meaning you want to assign].
If you've handled difficult situations before, it means you're more resilient than you sometimes give yourself credit for.
If you've succeeded in areas where you were once a beginner, it means you're capable of learning this too.
If you're constantly solving the same problem, it means you have a systems issue, not a people issue.
If people are giving you honest feedback, it means you've created enough safety for honesty to exist.
If you're noticing the problem, it means you've stopped being completely controlled by it.
If you're questioning your assumptions, it means growth is already happening.
If you've been living with this issue for years, it means solving it probably matters more than the investment required.
If you've already identified the cost of the problem, it means you know doing nothing isn't free.
If you've kept going despite setbacks, it means persistence is already part of who you are.
If you've achieved goals that once seemed impossible, it means your future capabilities are probably larger than your current assumptions.
If you're willing to ask for help, it means your ego isn't standing in the way of your progress.
If you're noticing this now, it means some part of you was ready to notice it.
If you're paying attention to this, it means something about it is relevant to you.

#### Why It Works

##### Meaning Assignment
Facts rarely persuade people.
The meaning attached to facts does.
We attach a new meaning to a limitation in order to break it. 

##### Uses Existing Evidence
Most persuasion requires proving something.
This pattern borrows evidence that already exists.
The prospect has already accepted the fact.
We simply connect it to a new conclusion.

##### Creates Logical Momentum
Once the fact is accepted, the meaning feels like a natural extension.
Instead of introducing a completely new idea, we build on something already established.

##### Resource State Activation
We connect past successes to present challenges.
This reminds people of capabilities they already possess.
Rather than focusing on what's missing, we focus on evidence of strength.

##### Reframes Objections
An objection is often just a thought with a negative interpretation attached.
This pattern replaces the interpretation without arguing about the thought itself.

##### Identity Reinforcement
Many implications point back toward competence, resilience, discipline, courage, persistence, resourcefulness, or growth.
The pattern strengthens identities that support action.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Assigns new meaning to existing thoughts.
- Creates empowering interpretations.
- Strengthens resourceful identities.
- Reframes objections.
- Increases confidence.
- Builds logical momentum.
- Changes how problems are understood.
- Turns past evidence into present leverage.
---

#### Strong Variations

##### Past Success → Present Capability
If you've built a company from scratch, it means you've already solved harder problems than this.
If you've learned an industry from zero before, it means you're capable of learning this too.
If you've survived previous downturns, it means uncertainty isn't new territory for you.
If you've figured things out without a roadmap before, it means you don't need perfect clarity to move forward.

##### Pain → Importance
If this problem keeps showing up every quarter, it means it probably deserves more attention than it's getting.
If you've been talking about fixing this for two years, it means it's important enough to keep demanding your attention.
If you're losing sleep over it, it means the cost is bigger than what's showing up on a spreadsheet.
If the issue keeps following you from employee to employee, it means the problem probably isn't the employee.
If it's frustrating you this much, it means the outcome matters to you.

##### Awareness → Progress
If you're noticing the pattern, it means you're no longer trapped inside it.
If you're questioning it, it means you've already started separating yourself from it.
If you're becoming aware of the problem, it means change is already underway.
If you're catching yourself doing it, it means you're further ahead than you think.
If you can describe the issue clearly, it means you're closer to solving it than someone who can't even see it.

##### Investment → Commitment
If you're spending this much time evaluating the decision, it means the outcome matters.
If you're asking these questions, it means you're taking the decision seriously.
If you've made it this far in the conversation, it means solving the problem is important to you.

##### Objection → Opposite Meaning
If you're nervous about the investment, it means you understand the opportunity is meaningful.

##### Persistence → Identity
If you've kept showing up despite the setbacks, it means persistence is already part of who you are.
If you're still working on this after all these years, it means quitting isn't really your style.
If you've survived everything that came before this, it means you're capable of handling what comes next.

## Binary Breaker  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Removes rigid and inflexible beliefs and behaviors.
**Structure**: `Are we talking {absolute/extreme} or {more nuanced version}?`
Pattern 1: Absolute to %
> ****

#### How much {resource}, as a percent, would you need in order to {action}?
Examples:
- How much confident, as a %, would you need to be in order to move forward? 
- How much certainty, as a %, would you need before making a decision? 34%? 43%? 77.2%?
- How prepared, as a %, would you need to feel before taking action?
Pattern 2: Collapse 
> ****

#### When you say {absolute statement}, does that mean {ridiculous absolute extension}?
Examples: 
- When you say webinars don't work, does that mean every webinar ever run has failed?
- When you say nobody buys during a recession, does that mean nobody bought anything during 2008?
- When you say women don’t like bald guys, does that mean that Jeff Bezo’s new wife is really an AI? 
Pattern 3: Breakthrough
> ****

#### People think {complete overgeneralization}. The best {results} I’ve ever gotten came when {some variance existed}. 
Examples:
- People think they have to be a hundred percent ready before they commit to anything. All the best breakthroughs I've ever had in my life came from times where I was only SOMEWHAT SURE if it was gonna work or not.
- People think they need a perfect business plan before they launch. The biggest breakthroughs I've ever had came when I had a rough idea, a deadline, and just enough confidence to get started.
- People think they need to eliminate all risk before they invest. Some of the best investments I've ever made were the ones where there was still uncertainty, but the upside was obvious.
Pattern 4: Both-And
> ****

#### We can acknowledge that {massive constraint} and also recognize that {empowering truth}.
Examples: 
- We can acknowledge that both we have lots of responsibilities and challenges and that we also should do things that make sense for us.
- We can acknowledge that you're already working 60-hour weeks and also recognize that continuing to do the same things probably won't get you from $100,000 a month to $250,000 a month.
- We can acknowledge that you've started and stopped fitness programs more times than you'd like and also recognize that your past inconsistency doesn't determine your future capability.

Pattern 5: Sure Enough
> ****

#### You don't need to be {maximum state}. You only need to be {minimum sufficient state}.
Examples: 
- You don't have to be a hundred percent sure of yourself to sign up. You just have to be sure enough
- You don't need to know how you'll hire your next 50 employees. You only need to know how to hire the next one.
- You don't need to create a viral masterpiece. You only need content that helps one person solve one problem. Then repeat. 

### Why It Works
Most people get stuck because they tend to think in absolutes:
- Always
- Never
- Everyone
- Nobody
- All
- None
- Every
The problem with absolutes is that they create binary thinking.
Instead of evaluating reality on a spectrum, people begin evaluating it as either:
- Ready or not ready
- Certain or uncertain
- Success or failure
- Safe or dangerous
- Perfect or worthless
Once someone enters a binary state, they often stop thinking altogether.
They move into autopilot.
And autopilot almost always favors safety over progress.
The result is that people begin requiring impossible conditions before they'll take action:
- Complete certainty before making a decision.
- Complete confidence before taking action.
- Complete knowledge before getting started.
- Complete safety before taking a risk.
- Complete readiness before making a commitment.
---

#### The Goal of These Patterns
…is to break those impossible conditions that stop action from occuring. 
When someone says:
> "Facebook ads don't work."
or
> "Nobody buys during a recession."
or
> "I'm not ready."
they are speaking from generalizations and are not even aware those generalizations are keeping them stuck. 
---

#### Shifting From Absolutes
Instead of:
> "Am I ready?"
You shift the thinking to:
> "How ready do I actually need to be?"
Instead of:
> "Facebook ads don't work."
The shift is:
> "Under what circumstances might they work?"
Instead of:
> "I need certainty."
The shift is:
> "How much certainty is enough?"
The moment a person moves from absolutes to degrees, they regain flexibility.
---

#### Why This Changes Decisions
Many decisions are delayed not because the opportunity is bad, but because the person is stuck. 
When these patterns are used, the listener realizes: 
- Progress requires less certainty than they thought.
- Action requires less confidence than they thought.
- Decisions require less information than they thought.
Most breakthroughs occur when people stop demanding perfection and start accepting sufficiency.
> ****

#### MILTON ALERT
I learned the absolute→percent pattern while studying Milton Erickson.
I remember reading a case study about a man who was severely claustrophobic. He came into Erickson's office insisting that he could never be comfortable in a room unless the door was open.
Erickson didn’t argue. He let the door be open as he started the hypnosis session. 
Then, while under hypnosis, Erickson asked the man a simple question:
> "Could you tell the difference between a door that is completely open and one that is closed by one millimeter?"
The man admitted he probably couldn't.
Then Erickson continued.
Could he tell the difference between one millimeter and two millimeters?
Two millimeters and three?
Millimeter by millimeter, Erickson slowly transformed an absolute into a spectrum.
What had originally been:
> Open or closed.
became:
> How open? How closed?
As the hours went on, Erickson kept expanding what open meant. 
Eventually he asked an even more interesting question:
> "Is a door really closed if it has a keyhole in it?"
After all, air could still pass through. Light could still pass through. 
By that point, the patient's rigid definition of "closed" had begun to collapse.
This is an extreme example, but it perfectly illustrates the deeper lesson.
Most limiting beliefs are built on absolutes.
People say things like:
- "I'm not ready."
- "I'm not confident."
- "That never works."
- "Nobody buys right now."
- "I'm bad at sales."
What Erickson was doing was introducing enough uncertainty into the certainty. 
(Which reminds me of another pattern I like: “Are you certain enough to feel uncertain?”)
That's why I love turning absolutes into percentages.
What's fascinating is that you usually don't need to replace the old belief.
You only need to weaken its rigidity.
The unconscious mind is remarkably good at keeping the useful parts of a belief while discarding the parts that no longer serve it… once you bring the right awareness to it. 
Doubt is great at brining that awareness. 
Once enough doubt is introduced into an absolute, people often begin updating the belief naturally.
They carry forward what's true, let go of what's limiting, and become open to possibilities they couldn't see before.

## Blessing in Disguise  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Turns a disadvantage into an advantage.
**Structure**: `{Perceived limitation} but {hidden advantage}.`
> ****

#### {Perceived limitation} but {hidden advantage}.
Examples:
- It sucks that you don’t have any experience yet but because you’re fresh then you’re going to get the most out of the experience when you get it. 
- I know you don’t have much money to invest but that will allow you to be more committed than these rich assholes who throw money at everything and think that is sufficient enough to solve the problem. 
- I hear you that there is a lot of stuff in this program but because we also developed an AI, you can now get both the encyclopedia version with an instant “answer” butler to fetch it FOR you quicker than ever. 
- It sucks that you've never done a webinar before, but that's exactly why you're more likely to follow the process than the people trying to reinvent it before they even understand it.
- Success is expensive but failure even more so… 
- I know your email list is small, but until you can make an offer work with 500 people, you’re not ready yet to make it to 50,000 people. 
- It sucks that you don't have much spare time, but people with unlimited time usually waste it on Instagram anyway. 
- I know you're uncomfortable on camera but audiences resonate more with somebody who's figuring it out alongside them than someone who sounds like a well-polished AI-looking fake-ass personality. 

### Why it Works

#### It Eliminates Resistance By Agreeing First
Most people make the mistake of fighting the objection.
Prospect:
> "I don't have much experience."
Weak response:
> "Experience doesn't matter."
A stronger response is:
> "It sucks that you don't have much experience yet..."
Now you've acknowledged their reality.
You're standing beside them rather than across from them.
The prospect doesn't feel the need to defend the limitation because you've already accepted it.
Once resistance drops, the reframe becomes easier to accept.

#### Weakness Becomes Advantage
It transforms the limitation into the reason someone may outperform others.
For example:
> It sucks that you don't have much money to invest, but that's often what creates commitment.
Now the prospect isn't merely "okay."
They're potentially better positioned than someone without the limitation.
The limitation becomes a source of strength.

#### It Creates Contrast
The greater the perceived limitation, the more impressive the hidden advantage feels.
For example:
> It sucks that you've never done a webinar before...
creates a fairly negative expectation.
Then:
> ...but that's exactly why you're more likely to follow the process than people trying to reinvent it.

#### Exaggeration To Trigger Self-Persuasion
One of the most effective ways to weaken an objection is to exaggerate it.
When you exaggerate a limitation, people often instinctively correct you.
For example:
> 
“The worst thing that could happen when you do your first webinar is not only that nobody would buy from you but that somebody may get so upset that they would find where you live, drag you by your neck out in the street and cream to every passerby what a loser you are. 
The prospect immediately thinks:
> "No, it's not that actually bad."
Notice what just happened.
You didn't convince them.
They convinced you.
The moment someone begins arguing against their own limitation, the limitation starts losing its power.
People believe conclusions they arrive at themselves far more than conclusions given to them by someone else.
So “I know money is so incredibly tight with you right now that even ordering Door Dash is a serious financial commitment but you’ve showed up here today and invested something even more important - your time to improve your situation.”
I’m shifting identity from “I’m broke” to “I’m a person who invests”. And if they invest time, is that much different than money? Especially when I made them defend their postion with “okay, maybe I’m not that broke!”

#### It Breaks Automatic Interpretation
Most objections are not facts.
They are facts plus interpretations.
For example:
Fact:
> I have a small email list.
Interpretation:
> Therefore I can't succeed.
The pattern separates the two.
> I know your email list is small, but until you can make an offer work with 500 people, you're not ready for 50,000.
Now the same fact points toward a completely different conclusion.
Once people realize their interpretation is optional, new possibilities become available.

### The Strategic Use of But
Consider:
> You're right, but...
versus
> You're right, and...
The moment someone hears:
> You're right, but...
But deletes, minimizes, or discounts what precedes it and puts the emphasis on what follows it. 
- I’m sorry but…
- No offense but…
- I understand how you feel, but...
- I’m not racist but…
See what I mean? 
To use but effectively, place the objections first, followed by “but” and end on the positive, forward-looking point you want to make. 
Compare: 

| "I know we have a tight budget this quarter, but we still need to upgrade our software." | Our current software is severely slowing down our workflow, but upgrading to the new system will save us 10 hours a week.  |
|---|---|

#### Do the “But” Swap
In your own communication, audit your “but” and consider replacing it with and to see which is more empowering. 

| The house has a great layout, but the kitchen needs work | The house has a great layout, and the kitchen needs work |
|---|---|
| I missed my workout yesterday, but I'll get back on track today. | I missed my workout yesterday, and I'll get back on track today. |
| The market is crowded, but I think there's room for us. | The market is crowded, and I think there's room for us. |
| I understand how you feel, but… | I understand how you feel, and… |
Alternatively, you can use even though. 
"I want to go, but I'm tired” → "I want to go, even though I'm tired" 

#### But as a “Takeaway”
Take-away selling is the persuasive technique of increasing desire by reducing availability, eligibility, or certainty of access. It flips the dynamic: instead of chasing the prospect, you make them chase you.
But can be an excellent takeaway. 
Notice how I snuck a takeaway in here: 
> I know your email list is small, but until you can make an offer work with 500 people, you're not ready for 50,000.

##### Examples
- "This is the version most of our serious clients go with, but it might be more than you're looking to spend.”
- I'm 100% sure this can help you but I'm not sure if your commitment level is appropriate for this option. 
- I can't imagine a better solution for you but it seems like your schedule is already stretched a lot. I know it's difficult to make room for even the most important things. 

#### Use Cases

| Stage | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Connect | To truly understand this, we would need several hours and multiple different calls to really get an idea, but I'm confident we can get to the heart of it in a single call and give you clarity on exactly what you need to do next.” |
| Discover | "I know admitting you don't really have a system yet feels embarrassing, but it's actually the easiest starting point: there's nothing broken to undo, just something to build.” |
| Clarify | "You mentioned you're involved in every decision. That's clearly slowing things down, but I imagine you continue to do it because you haven’t found a better way yet.” |
| Expand | You said you tried many agencies in the past and the only thing they gave you was a headache. Would that mean you're opposed to ever working with agencies in the future? If the right agency comes along, would you consider partnering with them?  |
| Commit | Now we get to the awkward part of our conversation, where I'm going to make an offer to you. Because I am so shy and frankly not a very good salesperson, I've really just stacked this offer so incredibly in your favor. I think you will be pleasantly surprised with what I have for you.  |
| Decide | I know this was beyond what your budget was coming into the call but I can tell you’re a person who invests based on what makes sense once you’ve heard the facts, not just a number that may no longer make sense.  |
| Resolve | I know you're uncomfortable on camera but audiences resonate more with somebody who's figuring it out alongside them than someone who sounds like a well-polished AI-looking fake-ass personality.  |

## Limit Softener  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Weakens rigid labels, absolutes, and generalizations.
**Structure**: `Are we talking {hard label} or {less severe version}?`
> ****

##### Are we talking {hard label} or {less severe version}?

##### Examples
Are we talking {limit} or {less limited}? [breaking generalizations]
- Are we talking procrastination or hesitation?
- Are we talking impossible or just difficult?
- Are we talking a lack of confidence or a lack of certainty?
- Are we talking self-doubt or healthy caution?
- Are we talking saturated or competitive?
- Are we talking cost or investment?
- Are we talking spending money or reallocating money?
- Are we talking a lack of motivation or a lack of direction?
- Are we talking incapable or inexperienced?
- Are we talking danger or discomfort?
- Are we talking forever or for now?

##### Alternative Structures
- Is it really {hard label}, or is it closer to {less severe version}?
- Are we dealing with {hard label}, or something more like {less severe version}?
- When you say {hard label}, do you mean literally {hard label}, or do you mean {less severe version}?

#### Why It Works
Many limitations feel permanent because they are described with permanent language.
The moment we replace a large label with a smaller one, the problem becomes easier to solve.
"Incapable" feels permanent.
"Inexperienced" feels temporary.

##### Breaks False Binaries
People often describe challenges in absolute terms.
Either:
- Confident or not confident.
- Success or failure.
- Ready or not ready.
- Certain or uncertain.
The pattern introduces a middle ground.
Most objections become weaker the moment a spectrum appears.

##### Preserves Agreement
Direct contradiction creates resistance.
If someone says:
"I can't do this."
And we respond:
"Yes you can."
We create an argument.
Instead, we ask:
"Are we talking that you can’t do this under any circumstance or there even one possible way out of millions where you could do this?"
Now we're refining rather than contradicting.
The prospect stays engaged.

##### Creates Diagnostic Precision
Many objections collapse under closer inspection.
Someone says:
"I don't have confidence."
After exploration:
"What exactly are you uncertain about?"
The real issue might be:
- Lack of experience.
- Lack of proof.
- Fear of consequences.
But these real issues hide inside an overly-generalized label such as “I don’t have confidence.” 

##### Reduces Emotional Intensity
Language amplifies emotions.
The labels people choose often determine how they feel.
Compare:
- Danger vs discomfort.
- Failure vs feedback.
- Rejection vs redirection.
- Overwhelmed vs unclear.
The softer label creates a more manageable emotional state.

##### Creates Cognitive Flexibility
When someone becomes attached to a label, they become attached to everything that label implies.
This language pattern loosens the attachment.
Once a stronger label becomes questionable, alternative interpretations become possible.

##### Opens the Door for Future Reframes
This pattern rarely solves the objection by itself.
It weakens the objection enough for other patterns to work.
A prospect who believes something is impossible is difficult to influence.
A prospect who believes something is difficult but possible is much easier to influence.

##### Positions You As A Consultant, Not Salesperson
Often the listener will ask:
"What's the difference?"
Or
"What do you mean?"
The moment they ask the question, they have effectively requested the distinction.
Now we're no longer forcing a reframe onto them.
We're answering a question they asked.
That changes the dynamic of the conversation.
Because the explanation was requested rather than imposed, it is often accepted with far less resistance.
For example:
> Are we talking a lack of confidence or a lack of certainty?
"What's the difference?"
> Confidence often comes out of experience but until you try something, you can't have experience. The issue then becomes that people don't ever try anything new because they're waiting to get experience before they experience something. Isn't that crazy?

Certainty, on the other hand, is being able to gain experience because you're not afraid something bad will happen to you. If you knew that you could move forward and only good things could happen, then would you make the decision to move forward?
The prospect now becomes much more likely to consider the distinction because they invited the explanation themselves.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Weakens limiting beliefs.
- Breaks generalizations.
- Creates nuance.
- Reduces emotional intensity.
- Increases flexibility.
- Makes problems feel temporary.
- Creates more accurate diagnoses.
- Prepares the objection for future reframes.
- Positions you as a consultant, not a salesperson. 

#### Strong Variations

##### Ability
Are we talking incapable or inexperienced?
Are we talking unqualified or simply unpracticed?
Are we talking unable or unfamiliar?
Are we talking lack of talent or lack of repetition?
Are we talking incompetence or lack of exposure?

##### Confidence
Are we talking lack of confidence or lack of certainty?
Are we talking self-doubt or incomplete information?
Are we talking fear or caution?
Are we talking insecurity or unfamiliarity?
Are we talking hesitation or inability?

##### Business Growth
Are we talking saturated or competitive?
Are we talking no opportunity or just higher standards?
Are we  talking a bad market or a more demanding market?
Are we talking impossible growth or slower growth?
Are we talking no demand or tougher differentiation?

Are we talking expensive or profitable?
Are we talking unaffordable or simply uncomfortable?
Are we talking financial risk or calculated investment?

Are we talking overwhelmed or disorganized?
Are we talking busy or distracted?
Are we talking lack of time or lack of allocation?
Are we talking impossible to fit in or simply inconvenient?

Are we talking risk or uncertainty?

Are we talking lazy or discouraged?

Are we talking weakness or exhaustion?

##### Time Horizon
Are we talking forever or for now?
Are we talking permanent or temporary?
Are we talking always or recently?
Are we talking impossible or not yet?
Are we talking never or not currently?

## Objection Collapse  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Redirects objections to a different interpretation.
**Structure**: `You say {objection}, but couldn’t that actually suggest {new interpretation}?`
> ****

##### You say {objection}, but couldn't that actually suggest {new interpretation}?

##### Examples
- "You say you've been burned before… but couldn't that actually suggest you now know exactly what you're not willing to settle for?"
- "You say you don't have time… but couldn't that be exactly why you need a system that doesn't depend on your time?"
- "You say the team isn't ready… but isn’t that when you need it the most?”
- "You say you want to wait… but couldn't that be the same pattern that's kept this problem alive for the last three years?"
- You say you don’t have time even though you sat on this webinar for 3 hours now. 
- You say you don’t have money even though you bought a flight ticket and hotel room to be here. 
- You say you lack confidence but you say it with the certainty of someone who has confidence. 
- You say you’re unsure, but damn if you don’t say so sure on how unsure you think you are.

#### Why It Works

##### The Objection Becomes the Evidence
This pattern takes the exact words the prospect used to resist and turns them into support for action.
"You said you don't have time" becomes "That's exactly why you need a system that doesn't depend on your time."
The objection itself becomes the reason to move forward.

##### Curiosity Enters Through "Couldn't That..."
The phrase "couldn't that actually suggest" is important.
We're not telling them their objection means something different.
We're asking them to consider it.
That invitation bypasses defensiveness. The listener investigates rather than defends.

##### Validates Before Redirecting
"You say X" validates that we've heard them.  
Then "but couldn't that suggest Y" offers a reinterpretation.
The validation makes the reinterpretation easier to receive.

##### Forces a New Association
Most objections are attached to automatic interpretations.
"I've been burned before" automatically means "I shouldn't trust this."
This pattern creates a new association: "I've been burned before" might mean "I know what to watch for now, which makes me better positioned to succeed this time."

#### What It Accomplishes
- Turns objections into evidence for action
- Creates curiosity around a reinterpretation
- Validates the listener before redirecting
- Forces a new association with a familiar experience
- Reduces the power of the objection without dismissing it
- Makes the listener consider whether their resistance is actually serving them

#### Variations
- "That sounds less like a reason not to try and more like a reason this needs to happen."
- "I wonder if what you're calling a reason to wait is actually a reason to move faster."
- "You just described exactly why this has been such a persistent problem."

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Discover | "You said this has always been a problem. Couldn't that suggest you've been solving the symptom rather than the cause?" |
| Clarify | "You said your team doesn't follow through. Couldn't that actually suggest the process hasn't been designed to make following through easy?" |
| Expand | "You said this doesn't feel urgent enough. Couldn't that feeling actually be the symptom of a much bigger problem… that you've gotten too comfortable with something that's costing you?" |
| Resolve | "You said you're not sure. Couldn't that uncertainty mean this deserves a more careful look rather than a quick no?" |

## Pattern Interrupt  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Disrupts haibtual thinking.
**Structure**: `{Unexpected statement/question that breaks the current thought pattern}.`
> ****

##### {Unexpected statement, question, or pause that breaks the current thought pattern}

##### Examples
- Is it the red shoes? (e.g. when a prospect says they are unsure of themselves.)
- Have you always blinked out of sync? 
- Hold up? Are you an only child?
- STOP! (finger snap)… what if it was actually…? {shifting focus}

#### Why It Works

##### Habitual Thinking Runs on Autopilot
Most conversations follow predictable scripts.
Objection → defense. Question → answer. Pitch → "let me think about it."
When we break the script unexpectedly, the autopilot turns off.
The listener suddenly has to pay attention again.

##### Confusion Creates Openness
When the brain encounters something it can't immediately process, it briefly enters a search state.
During that search, the brain is much more suggestible. 
The listener is temporarily more open to new ideas than they were one second before.

##### Creates an Interruption in Emotional State
Pattern interrupts are especially valuable when someone is stuck in a negative or resistant state.
Frustration, defensiveness, boredom…  these all run through habitual patterns.
A sharp interruption breaks the loop. The listener comes out the other side in a different state.

##### The Unexpected Is Remembered
Unusual moments are disproportionately remembered.
If we want something to land and stick, delivering it after a pattern interrupt increases the odds significantly.
The brain tags unusual experiences as significant. What follows the interruption gets processed differently.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Breaks habitual thought patterns and conversational scripts
- Creates a brief state of openness through confusion
- Interrupts negative or stuck emotional states
- Makes what follows more memorable and impactful
- Recaptures attention when it has drifted
- Creates an opportunity to reframe or redirect without a direct argument

## Wrong, But  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Lowers resistance to provide more information.
**Structure**: `I may be wrong, but {tentative interpretation}.`
> ****

##### I may be wrong, but {tentative interpretation}.

##### Examples
- I may be wrong, but it sounds like this isn't really about the money.
- I may be wrong, but it seems like you're more afraid of wasting time than losing money.
- I may be wrong, but it sounds like you're worried about what happens if it actually works.
- I may be wrong, but your standards may be helping you succeed and exhausting you at the same time.
- I may be wrong, but I don't think you're the type of person who quits easily.
- I may be wrong, but I get the impression that you're more resilient than you give yourself credit for.
- I may be wrong, but I don't think you're looking for the cheapest solution.

#### Why It Works

##### Permission to Be Wrong Removes Defensiveness
"I may be wrong" signals that we're not attached to being right.
The listener relaxes. They're not preparing to defend against a claim, they're invited to evaluate a possibility.

##### The Interpretation Lands Anyway
Despite the qualifier, the interpretation still gets delivered.
"I may be wrong, but I don't think this is really about the money" still delivers the reframe.
The listener still hears it. They still consider it.
But they heard it from someone who isn't certain, which makes them more willing to evaluate it honestly.

##### Creates a Useful Double Bind
If the listener agrees: "You're right, it's not about the money, it's about..."
We've moved past the stated objection into the real one.
If the listener disagrees: "No, it really is about the money."
We've confirmed that the stated objection is genuine, which is equally valuable.
Either way, the conversation advances.

##### Models Epistemic Humility
People trust communicators who admit they could be wrong.
In a world full of confident pitches, admitting uncertainty is disarming.
It builds credibility by demonstrating genuine curiosity rather than strategic positioning.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Delivers interpretations with significantly less resistance
- Creates a useful double bind: agreement or denial both move the conversation forward
- Models humility that builds trust
- Moves past stated objections to discover real ones
- Invites evaluation rather than defense
- Signals genuine curiosity rather than manipulation

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "I may be wrong, but something tells me you've had this exact conversation before and it didn't go anywhere. Am I close?" |
| Discover | "I may be wrong, but it sounds like the problem you came in with and the problem that's actually keeping you up at night might be two different things." |
| Clarify | "I may be wrong, but I don't think it's a lead problem. I think it's a positioning problem that's creating a lead problem." |
| Expand | "I may be wrong, but I think the real cost of this isn't the $20,000 a month you described. I think it's the momentum you've lost." |
| Resolve | "I may be wrong, but I don't think that's the real objection. I think there's something underneath it we haven't talked about yet." |

## Yes (but) And  [Reframe]
**Summary**: Validating perspective while smoothly shifting it.
**Structure**: `It's understandable that {current perspective}, and {empowering reframe}.`
> ****

##### It's understandable that {current perspective}, and {empowering reframe}.

##### Examples
- It's perfectly natural that you've been playing small. At one point that strategy probably protected you. The question is whether it's still protecting you or just limiting you.
- Of course you've been hesitant. Caution has likely saved you from making mistakes before. The challenge is that it can also keep you from opportunities
- Considering your past experiences, your reluctance makes perfect sense. You can appreciate the role that fear played without continuing to put it in charge.
- It's understandable that you'd want certainty. The challenge is that certainty tends to show up after the decision rather than before it.
- The interesting thing is that people rarely ask whether they can afford the mistake of never solving the problem.

#### Why It Works

##### "And" Preserves Both Truths Simultaneously
"But" erases what came before it.
"And" holds both things as true at once.
"It's understandable that you're nervous, and nervousness doesn’t have to stop a good decision"
The listener doesn't have to give up their perspective to accept the reframe.

##### Validation Creates Permission
When we validate the listener's perspective, we earn the right to extend it.
If they feel heard, they're open. If they feel dismissed, they're defensive.
The pattern leads with validation precisely so the reframe can follow without resistance.

##### The Reframe Is Linked, Not Opposed
Traditional counterargument is structured as opposition.
"You're nervous, but you've done hard things before." The "but" frames confidence as the opposite of nervousness.
"You're nervous, and that nervousness is actually evidence of how much this matters to you" links the two.
The original feeling becomes a bridge to the new perspective.

##### Breaks the All-or-Nothing Pattern
Resistance often lives in binary thinking: "Either I'm confident or I'm not."
The "and" structure disrupts the binary.
Both things exist. Both are true. And the second one opens a path forward.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Validates the listener's current perspective before reframing it
- Uses "and" to hold both truths simultaneously rather than replacing one with another
- Creates permission to reframe through validation
- Disrupts binary thinking
- Shifts the emotional tone of resistance from confrontation to collaboration
- Makes the listener feel heard while still moving the conversation forward

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "It's understandable that you're a bit guarded, and that tells me you've cared enough about this to be disappointed before." |
| Discover | "It makes sense that you've adapted to this problem over time, and that adaptation might be hiding how much it's actually costing." |
| Clarify | "It's completely fair to think the problem is your team, and we also need to look at what they've been set up to work with." |
| Decide | "It's natural to want more certainty, and certainty almost never comes before the decision - it usually comes after." |
| Resolve | "It makes complete sense that you've been burned before, and everything about how we've built this is a direct response to exactly that experience." |

## Decision Primer  [Resource]
**Summary**: Prepares for the decision about to occur.
**Structure**: `Before you {desired decision}, first {mental prerequisite}.`
> ****

##### Before you {desired decision}, first {easy internal action}.

##### Examples
- Before you make a decision, imagine you've already implemented it and ask yourself what would happen next.
- Before you commit, think about what would need to occur for this to become one of your best business decisions.
- Before you sign up, find the part of yourself that is most excited for the new future you’re about to enroll in.
- Before saying yes today, first congratulate yourself on accepting a new and exciting journey you’re about to go on. 

#### Why It Works

##### Assumes the Decision
The pattern quietly presupposes that the decision is going to happen.
> Before you decide...
Not:
> If you decide...
The mind is subtly moved past the question of whether a decision will occur and toward what should happen before it occurs.

##### Uses One Yes to Create Another
The brilliance of this pattern is in the request of the tiny action that comes before the decision.
For example:
> Before you sign up, find the part of yourself that is most excited for the new future you’re about to enroll in.
The yes of finding the part of yourself that is most excited is an attractive prospect. Easy to say yes to. Fun, even. And when you say yes to that, you also say yes to the bigger decision (signing up). 

##### Links to an Existing Future
This is very similar to telling a child: 
> Turn off the lights after you take the trash out. 
We link a request to an action that is expected to happen anyway.
The trash is already being taken out.
The lights get attached to the behavior.
We treat the future decision as the thing that is already happening.
Then we attach a smaller action to it.
And once the smaller action is accepted, the larger action feels more natural too.

##### Creates a Mental On-Ramp
Many decisions feel large.
Many prerequisite actions feel tiny.
Instead of asking someone to jump directly into the decision, we give them a small mental step first.
Imagine.
Consider.
Reflect.
Picture.
Remember.
Evaluate.
The mind enters through the small door and often ends up arriving at the larger conclusion on its own.

##### Reduces Resistance
A direct request often creates pressure.
A mental prerequisite rarely does.
Very few people resist:
> Before you decide, think about...
The request feels reasonable.
The suggestion within it receives much less resistance.

##### Creates Consistency
Once we perform the prerequisite, we often begin behaving consistently with it.
If we imagine ourselves succeeding, we become more open to any action that can help with that success.
If we imagine ourselves implementing, we become more open to any action that helps with implementation.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Presupposes a future decision.
- Creates momentum toward action.
- Makes large decisions feel smaller.
- Reduces resistance.
- Creates consistency.
- Gets prospects mentally involved.
- Moves attention away from whether and toward how.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | Before we get into the details, first think about what you'd like this conversation to accomplish. |
| Discover | Before we talk about solutions, first walk me through what happens on a normal day. |
| Clarify | Before we conclude the problem is lead generation, first look at how many leads are already sitting untouched in the CRM. |
| Commit | Before I present this offer to you, I want you to consider how much easier your life becomes when you no longer have this problem anymore.  |
| Decide | Before you sign up, find the part of yourself that is most excited for the new future you’re about to enroll in. |
| Resolve | Before you conclude if this investment makes sense, first compare it against the cost of keeping the problem. |
> ****

#### MILTON ALERT
If you listened to a lot of Milton Erickson’s hypnosis sessions, you hear these phrases used over and over again:
- “You can begin to imagine… “
- “It’s possible to consider…”
- “You may become aware of…”
- “You can notice…” 
And often they are combined with suggestions:
> How much more successful you can become. 

| “You can begin to imagine… “ | How much more successful you can become.  |
|---|---|
| “It’s possible to consider…” | How much more successful you can become.  |
| “You may become aware of…” | How much more successful you can become.  |
| “You can notice…”  | How much more successful you can become.  |
And also commands: 
> When you make the right investments in yourself.
So now we get:

| “You can begin to imagine… “ | How much more successful you can become.  | When you make the right investments in yourself. |
|---|---|---|
| “It’s possible to consider…” | How much more successful you can become.  | When you make the right investments in yourself. |
| “You may become aware of…” | How much more successful you can become.  | When you make the right investments in yourself. |
| “You can notice…”  | How much more successful you can become.  | When you make the right investments in yourself. |
Nothing is asserted directly.
A direct statement can be rejected.
> "You are becoming more confident."
The listener can immediately respond:
> "No, I'm not."
But it's much harder to reject:
> "Imagine becoming more confident."
The statement isn't claiming that confidence exists.
It's only inviting the listener to consider it.
The mind can accept the invitation or ignore it, but there is very little to argue with.
That's what makes words like imagine, consider, suppose, and wonder so powerful. They create possibilities instead of assertions.
When we say:
> Imagine closing your biggest client.
The mind doesn't start by analyzing.
It starts by simulating.
The prospect begins creating images, feelings, sounds, and sensations associated with the experience.
The word imagine gives us psychological safety.
We can explore ideas without committing to them.
We can mentally rehearse success without needing to believe it will happen.
We can explore a new identity without abandoning the old one.
That's why children naturally use imagination to learn.

##### "Begin To" Makes It Even Harder to Reject
This is where Erickson became especially elegant.
Most suggestions ask for an immediate result.
> Imagine feeling more confident.
But:
> You can begin to imagine feeling more confident.
Now the demand becomes microscopic.
The listener doesn't have to imagine confidence.
They only have to begin.
Erickson constantly reduced the size of the requested response.
Not:
> Relax.
But:
> You can begin to relax.
Not:
> Understand.
But:
> You may begin to understand.
Not:
> Change.
But:
> You can start noticing changes.
The smaller the request, the harder it becomes to reject.
A huge amount of Ericksonian language can be reduced to:
1. Preserve reality.
1. Introduce possibility.
1. Reduce the size of the request.
Instead of:
> This is true.
We get:
> Imagine if this were true.
Instead of:
> You feel this.
We get:
> You may begin to notice this.
Instead of:
> This will happen.
We get:
> I wonder what would happen if...
The listener never has to fight the suggestion because the suggestion never attacks their current reality.

## How / When  [Resource]
**Summary**: Reveals the inner process on how decisions are made.
**Structure**: `How do you know when {internal process}?`
> ****

##### How do you know when {internal process}?

##### Examples
- How will you know when you've found the right solution?
- How do you know when to procrastinate? 
- How do you know when you’re motivated versus excited? 
- How do you know when something is a good decision? 
- How do you know when things aren’t going to plan? 
- How do you know when you have an adequate amount of confidence to move forward? 
- How do you know when to listen to your heart and when to listen to your head? 

#### Why It Works

##### Assumes the Process Exists
This is one of the most elegant aspects of the pattern.
The question does not ask whether a process exists.
It assumes one exists.
For example:
> How do you know when you're motivated?
The question presupposes that somewhere inside the person's experience, there is already a process that distinguishes motivation from not-motivation.
The person may never have consciously examined it.
But the process is there.
The question simply directs attention toward it.

##### Brings the Unconscious Into Awareness
Most decisions are made unconsciously.
People often know they do something without knowing how they do it.
For example:
> How do you know when someone is trustworthy?
Most people can answer.
But very few have consciously studied the criteria they use.
The moment they start answering, they begin uncovering the internal rules they've been following automatically.

##### Reveals Decision Criteria
This pattern is incredibly valuable because it exposes the standards people use to make decisions.
Consider:
> How do you know when something is a good decision?
The answer might reveal:
- certainty
- emotion
- logic
- social proof
- intuition
- risk tolerance
Now you're no longer guessing how they make decisions.
They're telling you.

##### Avoids Defensiveness
Compare:
> Why are you procrastinating?
Versus:
> How do you know when to procrastinate?
The first feels accusatory.
The second feels curious.
The first assumes something is wrong.
The second assumes a process exists.
People naturally become more reflective and less defensive.

##### Moves From Content to Structure
Most conversations stay stuck at the content level.
For example:
> I procrastinate.
That's content.
This pattern investigates the structure underneath the behavior.
> How do you know when to procrastinate?
Now we're exploring:
- triggers
- cues
- criteria
- internal dialogue
- emotional states
That's where change happens.

##### Creates Self-Discovery
Another reason this pattern is so powerful is that people often discover things while answering.
You aren't telling them.
You're helping them observe themselves.
And insights that people discover themselves are usually more persuasive than insights imposed from the outside.

##### Exposes Faulty Criteria
Many limiting behaviors survive because their underlying criteria remain hidden.
For example:
> How do you know when you have enough confidence to move forward?
Someone might answer:
> When I'm 100% certain.
Now the real issue becomes visible.
The problem wasn't confidence.
The problem was an impossible standard.
Once exposed, the conscious mind can evaluate whether that criterion is useful.

##### Creates New Possibilities
Once a process becomes visible, it becomes editable.
What was previously automatic becomes a choice.
People can keep the parts that serve them.
They can modify the parts that don't.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Reveals unconscious decision-making.
- Exposes hidden criteria.
- Avoids judgment.
- Reduces defensiveness.
- Creates self-awareness.
- Uncovers limiting beliefs.
- Surfaces useful distinctions.
- Moves from behavior to process.
- Creates opportunities for change.

#### Why "How" Is Often Better Than "Why"
One of the classic observations in NLP is that "why" questions often produce justifications.
People explain.
Defend.
Rationalize.
Construct stories.
For example:
> Why do you procrastinate?
Common answer:
> Because I'm busy.
> Because I'm overwhelmed.
> Because I work better under pressure.
These are usually explanations.
Not mechanisms.
But:
> How do you know when to procrastinate?
Now they must examine the process.
They might say:
> I start feeling overwhelmed.
> I notice myself opening other tabs.
> I tell myself I'll do it later.
> I wait until the pressure gets high enough.
Now we're learning how the behavior operates.
That's far more useful than learning how they explain it.

##### The Difference

| Question | Tends to Produce |
|---|---|
| Why? | Reasons, explanations, justifications |
| What? | Information, descriptions |
| How? | Process, structure, mechanisms |
| When? | Triggers, criteria, thresholds |
That's why "how" and "when" questions are often so powerful.
They reveal the machinery behind the behavior.

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | How did you know it was a good idea to book this call? |
| Discover | How do you know when a project is succeeding? |
| Clarify | How do you know when you're procrastinating versus strategically waiting? |
| Expand | How do you know when an opportunity is bigger than it first appears? |
| Commit | How do you know when you're ready to take action? |
| Resolve | How do you know when you have enough information to make a decision? |
> ****

##### NLP Alert
I first discovered this “how / when” pattern while studying Richard Bandler, co-creator of NLP (Neuro-Linguistic Programming). In one of the sessions documented on the Marshall tapes, Bandler worked with a woman who would become extremely distressed whenever someone was late. If a person didn't arrive when expected, she would begin panicking, catastrophizing, and literally started rolling on the floor out of pure terror.
Rather than asking why she reacted that way, Bandler became curious about how she did it.
He used role play and said something to the effect of:
> "I'd like to fill in for you for a day. I want to do your job for you, but I need to know exactly how. If someone is late, how do I know when to start panicking? What specifically do I do?"
He took her process literally.
How did she know it was time to panic?
What pictures was she making in her mind?
What did she say to herself?
What feelings followed?
By eliciting the process in detail, Bandler transformed an automatic reaction into a sequence of observable steps. Once the process became visible, it became editable.
Now he could ask questions like:
> "Have you tried not making that picture?"
Or:
> "What would happen if you made a different picture instead?"
The problem was no longer some mysterious emotional reaction. It became a process that could be examined, interrupted, and changed.
I once used this same principle with a client who told me he felt unproductive.
Instead of asking why, I asked:
> "How do you know when you're productive?"
He thought about it and explained that he felt productive whenever he worked beyond the time he would normally stop for the day.
At the time, his quitting time was 4:00 PM.
So I asked:
> "If you worked until 4:30, would you feel productive?"
"Yes."
> "What about 4:15?"
"Yes."
> "What about 4:05?"
"Yes."
Eventually we got down to:
> "What about 4:01?"
And he laughed.
"Yeah, I guess I'd feel productive."
What he realized was that he had unconsciously created a binary rule. Before 4:00 PM meant unproductive. After 4:00 PM meant productive.
The difference between failure and success wasn't an extra hour. It wasn't even thirty minutes. 
It was one minute.
Once the rule became visible, it lost much of its power. What had seemed like a massive hurdle became something almost trivial. More importantly, he began questioning the validity of the rule itself.
Another example came from a client who struggled with public speaking.
Rather than trying to convince her that speaking in public wasn't scary, I became curious and I asked:
> "How do you know the difference between speaking to a large group privately versus speaking to a large group publicly?"
At first, she didn't know.
Like most people, she had never consciously examined the process.
So I started exploring the boundaries of her experience.
I asked:
> "Imagine we're sitting around a table with nine people. A tenth person walks up and joins us. Is this now public speaking?"
She laughed.
"No."
Eventually, as we explored the distinction, we uncovered something fascinating.
The trigger wasn't the number of people.
It wasn't the stage.
It wasn't the microphone.
It wasn't public speaking at all.
What actually caused the fear was that she believed she needed to see every person's face and individually read each person's reaction in order to feel safe.
The moment the audience became large enough that she could no longer monitor every face, her anxiety would begin to rise.
That was how she knew it was time to become afraid.
Once we discovered it, I told her:
> "No wonder public speaking feels difficult. If I believed I had to continuously monitor and interpret every person's facial expression in the room before I could feel safe speaking, I would probably be even more afraid than you are."
For the first time, she began looking at the rule itself instead of merely experiencing its consequences.
Then I asked:
> "Is there another way you could know you're safe while speaking besides having to read every single face in the audience?"
She thought about it.
And almost immediately she began generating alternative criteria.
Not long afterward, she sent me a photograph of herself speaking on stage in front of 175 people.
The breakthrough didn't come from giving her confidence.
Two other way I successfully used this pattern:
- To a friend’s kid who bit his nails - “How do you know which nail to bite first?”
- To a friend who used to cut her self - “How do you know where to cut first?” 
When you start from a position of curiosity and genuine interest as opposed to judgment or advice, you gain instant deep rapport.

## Magic Wand  [Resource]
**Summary**: Reveals ideal outcomes by removing limitations and censorship.
**Structure**: `If you could have everything you wanted with {outcome}, what would it look like?`
> ****

##### If you could have everything you wanted with {outcome}, what would it look like?

##### Examples
- If this generated the exact number of leads you wanted, what would that mean for the business?
- If this solved the problem completely, what would success look like?
- If we could build the ideal solution for you, what would it do?
- If you could design the perfect outcome, what would it include?
- If everything went your way over the next twelve months, what would happen?
- If this turned out better than expected, what would that look like?
- If the company became everything you hoped it would become, what would be true?
- If you could build the ideal business, how would it operate?
- If everything clicked, what would success actually look like?

#### Why It Works

##### Removes the Filter of Practicality
Most of the time, people censor their desires.
They ask "what's realistic?" before they ask "what do I want?"
This pattern bypasses the filter.
"If you could have everything you wanted" signals: don't censor, don't qualify, don't edit.
We get access to the real desire, not the managed version.

##### Reveals the True Target
The censored version of a goal is vague: "I want more revenue."
The uncensored version is specific: "I want to hit $350k a month so I can stop working Saturdays and take my family somewhere for a month in the summer."

##### Creates a Standard for the Solution
Once we know what the full dream looks like, we can position any solution against it.
"Everything you just described is exactly what we've helped other clients build."
Or: "We can't get you all the way there in year one, but here's what we can get you."

##### Generates Emotional Connection to the Outcome
Describing what you want in detail creates emotional attachment to it.
The listener isn't just thinking about the goal abstractly. They're experiencing it.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Removes the self-censorship that produces vague goals
- Reveals genuine underlying desires
- Creates a clear standard the solution can be positioned alongside 
- Generates emotional investment in the desired outcome
- Makes the prospect describe and own their own ideal future
- Produces much richer information than standard goal-oriented questions

#### Variations
- "If you had to describe the perfect version of this, what would you say?"
- "What would you want to be able to say about this time next year if everything went right?"
- "If this exceeded even the most wildest dream you ever had, what would have to happen?”

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "If this conversation went exactly as well as you could imagine, what would be true by the end of it?" |
| Discover | "If you could redesign the business from scratch, what would you keep and what would you throw out?" |
| Clarify | "If you could have the root cause identified and fixed completely, what would be different about the day-to-day?" |
| Expand | "If you could have everything you wanted out of a solution to this problem, what would it deliver?" |
| Commit | "If a partnership like this could give you everything you've described, what would be left to think about?" |
| Decide | "If everything you've told me you want from this is achievable, then signing up is as easy as…”  |

## Priming  [Resource]
**Summary**: Actives desire identity, mindset, belief or frame before an ask.
**Structure**: `Do you consider yourself someone who {desired identity trait}?`
> ****

##### Do you consider yourself someone who {desired identity trait}?

##### Examples
Do you consider yourself an adventurous person?
Would you consider yourself truthful?
Are you someone who stands by your commitments?
Do you consider yourself resourceful?
Would you consider yourself a person who keeps commitments to yourself?
Do you consider yourself open-minded?
Are you someone who likes to stay ahead of problems rather than react to them?
Would you say you're someone who holds yourself to a high standard?
Are you someone who finishes what they start?
Do you consider yourself ambitious?

#### Why It Works

##### Identity Questions Activate Self-Concept
When we ask "do you consider yourself someone who X?", we direct attention to self-concept.
The listener evaluates themselves against the trait.
Almost universally, they identify with positive traits.
The activation of that identity then influences the decisions that follow.

##### Pre-Commitment Through Self-Description
When someone says "yes, I consider myself someone who makes decisions based on data," they've made a commitment.
Now if the data supports moving forward, they have to act consistently with the identity they just claimed.
Consistency is one of the most durable drivers of human behavior.

##### Almost Everyone Agrees
The traits we prime with are universally desirable.
Decisive. Action-oriented. Values results. Makes smart investments.
Nobody says "no, I don't consider myself that kind of person."
The agreement is nearly guaranteed, which means the pre-commitment lands every time.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Activates a positive identity before a key moment
- Creates self-consistency pressure
- Produces pre-commitment through identity agreement
- Makes the subsequent ask feel like an expression of who they are
- Builds rapport by acknowledging their positive qualities
- Creates internal alignment between identity and action

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "Before we dive in, do you consider yourself someone who implements good ideas when they hear them?” |
| Discover | "Do you consider yourself someone who trusts their gut about where the problem is, or do you need to see the data first?" |
| Clarify | "Do you consider yourself someone who'd rather know the uncomfortable truth than a comfortable version of it?" |
| Expand | "Would you consider yourself ambitious?” |
| Commit | "Do you consider yourself someone who takes action on worthy pursuits?” |

## Reawakening  [Resource]
**Summary**: Reconnects to a powerful past state or capability
**Structure**: `Can you remember a time when you felt {resourceful state}…?`
> ****

##### Can you remember a time when you felt {resourceful state}...?

##### Examples
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely certain... where making the decision felt almost effortless?
- Can you remember a time when you felt unstoppable... where everything seemed to be working in your favor?
- Can you remember a time when you felt totally clear about what to do next... where the path forward seemed obvious?
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely energized by your business... where you couldn't wait to get back to work?
- Can you remember a time when you felt totally decisive... where making difficult decisions felt surprisingly simple?
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely confident... where you surprised yourself with what you were capable of?
- Can you remember a time when you felt absolutely certain... where the outcome felt inevitable?
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely absorbed in something... where the outside world seemed to fade away?
- Can you remember a time when you became so interested in something that you lost track of time?
- Can you remember a time when you knew more than you realized you knew?
- Can you remember a time when you discovered you were capable of more than you expected?
- Can you remember a time when you were learning something quickly... and suddenly it all made sense?
- Can you remember a time when you were nervous about something... and then afterward wondered why you worried at all?

#### Why It Works

##### Memory Activates the State It Contains
When we remember a time when we felt confident, we briefly re-experience that confidence.
The brain partially re-creates the neurological experience of what it's remembering.
We don't have to give the listener confidence. We help them access it from within.

##### Resources Are Never Missing, Only Unavailable
Most limiting beliefs are built on the idea that a resource is absent.
"I'm not confident." "I'm not decisive." "I'm not good at this."
The Reawakening pattern challenges this at the root.
If we can find one memory of the state, we prove that the listener is capable of doing something they thought they weren’t. 

##### Sets the Stage for Transfer
Once the state is activated, we can bridge it to the current situation.
The implication is: "You had that feeling before. You can have it here."
The pattern creates the state so we can direct it toward what needs it most.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Activates resourceful states through memory
- Demonstrates that the resource already exists within the listener
- Creates internally generated motivation rather than externally provided inspiration
- Produces a state that can be transferred to the current decision
- Makes limiting beliefs about absent resources feel less certain
- Builds internal confidence and capability before a critical moment

#### Conversational Ladder Applications

| Stage | Example |
|---|---|
| Connect | "Can you remember a time early in your career when you had to figure something out with almost no resources and you made it work anyway?" |
| Discover | "Can you remember a time when you looked at a problem from a completely different angle and found an answer no one else saw?" |
| Clarify | "Can you remember a time when your gut told you something was wrong before you had proof — and it was right?" |
| Expand | "Can you remember a time when you let something go too long and regretted it? What did the delay actually cost?" |
| Commit | "Can you remember a time when you committed fully to something and it had a profoundly positive result on your life?” |
| Resolve | "Can you remember a time when you were afraid of making the wrong choice, did it anyway, and were glad you did?   |

## Speaking as a Child  [Resource]
**Summary**: Puts listener in a child state.
**Structure**: `As a child, wasn’t it amazing how…?`
> ****

#### As a child, wasn’t it amazing how…?

##### Examples:
- As a child, remember how normal it was to try something simply to see what happened?
- {speaking to you} As a child, wasn't it interesting how you could spend hours trying to figure something out without needing to know the answer first?
- As a child, wasn't persistence almost automatic?
- As a child, wasn't it fascinating how easily you could imagine possibilities?
- As a child, do you remember when a cardboard box could become a spaceship?

##### Purpose:
Reconnect someone with a resourceful psychological state they naturally possessed as a child, making that state more available in the present moment.

##### Mechanism:
By recalling childhood experiences, the listener temporarily accesses associated emotions, attitudes, and ways of thinking, often reducing resistance and increasing openness to new ideas.

### Why It Works

#### 1. It Reactivates a Resource State
A child naturally exhibits many states that are useful for influence:
- Curiosity
- Imagination
- Experimentation
- Persistence
- Wonder
- Playfulness
- Exploration
- Optimism
- Learning
For example:
> As a child, wasn't it fascinating how easily you could imagine possibilities?
The conscious content is about childhood.
The subconscious effect is:
> "Imagine possibilities."
The state becomes available in the present.
---

#### 2. It Temporarily Suspends Adult Limitations
Adults often ask:
> Will this work?
Children often ask:
> What happens if I try it?
Those are radically different frames.
Example:
> As a child, remember how normal it was to try something simply to see what happened?
This gently moves someone from:
> Evaluation
to
> Exploration
which is often where persuasion becomes easier.
---

#### 3. It Provides Evidence
One reason people resist new behaviors is:
> "That's not who I am."
This pattern provides counter-evidence.
Example:
> As a child, wasn't persistence almost automatic?
Now persistence isn't foreign.
They've already been persistent before.
The pattern says:
> You already possess this quality.
You may simply not be using it currently.
---

#### 4. It Accesses Episodic Memory
When people remember experiences, they often partially re-experience them.
Example:
> Do you remember when a cardboard box could become a spaceship?
This reactivates 
- imagination
- possibility
- creativity
Those emotions become more accessible during the conversation.

#### 5. It Lowers Defensiveness
If I say:
> You need to be more creative.
Resistance.
If I say:
> As a child, wasn't it amazing how easily ideas came to you?
No resistance.
The same suggestion enters indirectly.

### The Power of States
The states are in italics:
A curious person evaluates differently than a skeptical person.
A creative person evaluates differently than a fearful person.
A playful person evaluates differently than a defensive person.
The pattern changes the state through which information is processed. We are all both curious and skeptical, creative and fearful, playful and defensive… it just matters which state we are in. 
> ****

#### MILTON ALERT
I first discovered this pattern from Milton Erickison. 
One reason Erickson would occasionally use phrases like:
> "Speaking to you as a child..."
is that the statement is not fully specified.
The conscious mind often wants to know:
- Am I the child?
- Is he speaking about himself as a child?
- Is he speaking to my childhood self?
- Is he speaking about children generally?
The ambiguity creates a moment where the listener searches for meaning.
During that search, attention shifts inward.
Instead of analyzing the speaker's argument, the listener begins exploring their own memories, associations, and experiences.
In Ericksonian hypnosis, this kind of ambiguity was often useful because the conscious mind becomes occupied trying to resolve the meaning while the unconscious mind begins generating its own interpretations.

##### Regression to an Earlier Version of the Self
Another reason Erickson frequently referenced childhood is that childhood represents a time before many limitations became fully established.
An adult might think:
> "I've always been shy."
"I've never been creative."
"I've never been confident."
Yet childhood memories often contain evidence to the contrary.
A person who currently believes they lack confidence may remember:
- learning to ride a bike
- climbing a tree
- making new friends
- performing in front of a class
The childhood reference helps reconnect them with experiences that existed before the limiting narrative became dominant.
---

##### The Psychological "Do-Over"
One particularly interesting aspect of childhood regression is that it can create the feeling of a fresh start.
When someone mentally revisits an earlier point in life, they often experience less attachment to their current limitations.
Instead of viewing themselves through the lens of:
> "This is who I am."
they begin viewing themselves through the lens of:
> "This is who I became."
That distinction matters.
The first implies permanence.
The second implies change.
In many cases, a person can mentally revisit an earlier version of themselves and imagine making different choices, trying new approaches, or responding differently than they did before.
The past is no longer experienced as proof that change is impossible. Instead, it becomes evidence that alternative paths could have been taken and can still be taken now.
---

##### Accessing Resource States
Children naturally display many of the states that are useful for change:
- Curiosity
- Creativity
- Persistence
- Imagination
- Playfulness
- Exploration
- Wonder
When Erickson directed attention toward childhood memories, he was often indirectly activating these resourceful states.
For example:
> "Speaking to you as a child, wasn't it amazing how you could spend hours trying to figure something out?"
The conscious content appears to be about childhood.
The unconscious effect is to reactivate:
- persistence
- fascination
- experimentation
- learning
Those states become more available in the present moment.
