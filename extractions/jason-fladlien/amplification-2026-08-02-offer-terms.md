## Executive Summary

- **Core genius:** Jason evaluates an offer by the life reconfiguration it demands from the buyer. Better offers recover time, compress effort, fit existing routines, make value easy to compare, and produce legitimate identity, connection, and visible progress.
- **What is different:** Existing offer systems usually optimize the package or pitch. TERMS diagnoses what the buyer must spend, change, endure, believe, and become in order to succeed.
- **Deployable capability:** Find one primary buyer burden, redesign it without creating a worse burden elsewhere, and route only the affected issue into the smallest existing offer workflow.
- **Hidden knowledge captured:** Adoption and proof are not downstream chores. The initial win, social check-in, and screenshot moment can be designed into the offer before it is sold.

## Hidden Knowledge

1. **The real cost of an offer is life reconfiguration.** Price is only one term; a cheap offer can be expensive in time, effort, routine disruption, and identity risk.
2. **The objectively best solution can be the commercially worst offer.** If it demands too much time or behavior change, quality prevents adoption.
3. **Compliance is designed, not demanded.** Context, cues, and chunk size often matter more than motivation or instruction.
4. **The first win begins before purchase.** Future-pacing a decision win and a tangible first win reduces buyer’s remorse and clarifies value.
5. **Free is a comparison category, not merely a zero price.** The economically identical deal can feel different depending on which familiar element becomes free.
6. **Smaller can be more persuasive when it is more believable.** Magnitude without certainty can reduce demand.
7. **Status belongs in fulfillment.** Internal confidence, connection, and visible progress are delivery mechanics, not luxury-brand decoration.
8. **Proof can be an output of product design.** A visible success event can generate evidence without coercive testimonial requests, but only with permission and scope controls.

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Recovered-Time Bargain

- **Range:** 05:08–06:44.
- **Demonstration:** Interview the buyer’s real day, remove wasted work, return part of the recovered time, and request only part for the new behavior.
- **Why it matters:** The offer earns capacity before requesting it.

### Twenty to Ten to Five

- **Range:** 08:01–09:12.
- **Demonstration:** Start with the ideal 20-hour solution, preserve nearly the same useful result in ten hours, then compress again to five.
- **Why it matters:** It treats time compression as product design rather than copy.

### Swiss-Army Subtraction

- **Range:** 16:40–17:20.
- **Demonstration:** Reduce a 15-feature software product to two features, improve those two, and charge more for a result buyers can actually use.
- **Why it matters:** More capability can reduce perceived and realized value.

### Amazon Screenshot Moment

- **Range:** 51:33–52:15.
- **Demonstration:** A visible sales dashboard creates a naturally shareable proof event without a direct testimonial request.
- **Why it matters:** Fulfillment, status, community, and proof become one designed loop.

### Anti-Exemplar — The Unlimited-Time Ideal Shipped as Reality

- **Failure:** The seller builds the objectively complete solution and assumes the buyer can devote thousands of hours to it.
- **Why it fails:** It optimizes theoretical quality while ignoring adoption capacity.

### Anti-Exemplar — Guilt-Based Consumption

- **Failure:** A provider asks an inactive buyer why they have not used the thing they paid for.
- **Why it fails:** It converts non-use into shame and weakens belonging. The source discusses cancellation in this context, but no causal rate is established.

## Signature Moves

1. Spy on the buyer’s calendar before designing the solution.
2. Remove time before requesting time.
3. Halve the ideal solution twice and name what must remain.
4. Design initial, first, and ideal wins separately.
5. Delete actions until every remaining step earns its place.
6. Require only one pivotal habit change.
7. Attach behavior to an existing cue and context.
8. Make a familiar high-value element feel free.
9. Shrink the initial promise before enlarging the market.
10. Turn earned progress into a permissioned screenshot moment.

## Methodology

1. Establish offer truth, buyer evidence, and preservation locks.
2. Confirm the game is viable; stop on PASS or WAIT.
3. Audit all five TERMS dimensions and 18 named levers.
4. Mark each lever as supported, friction, blocker, or unknown.
5. Select one primary burden and no more than two supporting changes.
6. Apply the smallest source-grounded intervention.
7. Route only the affected issue into an existing specialist.
8. Activate only the adoption or proof submodules supported by a named trigger. This conditional control is an Antigravity adaptation.
9. Reassemble under one owner and run the cross-term non-regression check.
10. Report component validity separately from market proof.

The prioritization rule, three-change ceiling, cross-term check, conditional submodule gate, permission chain, and market-state reporting above are Antigravity runtime controls. Jason’s source supplies the 18 mechanics; it does not supply this orchestration contract.
