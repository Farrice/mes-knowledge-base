## Reference Asset

**Six rungs between an ingredient and a finished-product claim**

“Science-backed” can describe six different things on the same product page.

At one end, a compound changed something in a cell. At the other, the finished formula, at the sold dose, in the intended buyer, produced the outcome named in the claim. The distance between those points is where many evidence reviews lose their footing.

For a founder or claims operator, the useful move is to mark the rung each claim can defend.

**1. The compound changes something in a cell or animal model**

Mechanistic and preclinical work can show that an idea deserves more study. It cannot, on its own, establish that a person will experience the advertised result. Keep the model, endpoint, and limits attached to the citation.

**2. The ingredient has a research literature**

A paper trail around an ingredient is more informative than a lone mechanism, but “there are studies” is still broad. The literature may examine different goals, forms, amounts, populations, or outcomes. A long bibliography can remain several steps away from the sentence in the ad.

**3. The ingredient has evidence for the specific goal being claimed**

This is the first rung where the claimed outcome becomes the center of the audit. The search has to match the ingredient to the goal in the language researchers actually used.

One public supplement-evidence operator reported a useful self-audit inside his own map. His system had scored 475 ingredient-goal pairings at zero. After a better re-query, he found at least one real randomized controlled trial for 277 of those pairings.

The numbers belong to one operator’s self-audit. They are verified as his first-party account alongside a public dataset and site caveat. They are not a measured category-wide error rate.

The public dataset contains 2,156 cleaned supplement-goal pairings. The published CSV is the pre-correction version. That boundary matters. A reader cannot treat the public file as if all 277 corrections have already been folded into it.

This is also where a research gap and a search gap split. A zero result may mean that no qualifying trial exists. It may also reflect naming, outcome language, synonyms, indexing, or dataset coverage. Record the search path before turning zero into a claim.

**4. The studied dose matches the product dose**

An ingredient-goal match still leaves a basic comparison: the amount used in the research and the amount present in the product. If they differ, the citation is less direct evidence for the finished-product claim. The page should not let the shared ingredient name hide that distance.

This is an evidence-fit check, not consumer dosing guidance.

**5. The studied form and population match the product and buyer**

Formulation details and study population change relevance. A paper involving one chemical form, extract specification, age range, or clinical group does not automatically support a claim aimed at another.

The audit should carry those fields beside the headline result: studied form, product form, studied population, intended buyer, and any mismatch. No inference about interaction or absorption belongs here unless the cited evidence supports it.

**6. The finished formula itself has been tested**

This is the closest rung to the object being sold. Evidence for each ingredient separately does not establish that the combination produces the marketed outcome. Formula-level testing can narrow that gap.

Evidence generation closer to the finished formula is a LIKELY emerging differentiator. The six-rung framework does not make it a universal legal requirement.

A separate 2022 JAMA Network Open analysis shows why the finished object deserves attention beyond ingredient citations. In its sample of 30 Amazon immune-support supplements, 17 had inaccurate labels and none displayed third-party certification seals. Keep the year and sample attached. The finding does not establish the condition of every supplement or resolve whether any formula works.

The regulatory boundary matters too. FTC health-claim guidance expects competent and reliable scientific evidence and asks whether the research is relevant to the specific claim. It does not supply this six-rung ladder, and relevance is not reduced here to a universal fixed number of trials.

The ladder is operational synthesis, not law.

Use it claim by claim. For each sentence on a product page, log the rung, the exact source, the studied goal, amount, form, population, and whether the finished formula was tested. Add the search terms, dates, exclusions, and correction status when a database returns zero.

Then separate three possible conclusions:

- evidence found at the rung the claim needs;
- evidence found, but at a more distant rung;
- no evidence found yet, with research gap and search gap still distinguished.

That record gives copy, science, and legal reviewers the same object to inspect. It also stops a broad ingredient citation from doing work it was never designed to do.

The honest claim ends where the evidence stops.
