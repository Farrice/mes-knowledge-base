## Reference Asset

# Six rungs between an ingredient and a claim

Most arguments about supplement evidence happen at different resolutions.

One person points to a molecule. Another points to a finished bottle bought for a specific problem. Both say "the science" while standing on different rungs.

The useful question is not whether science exists. It is how far that science travels toward the exact claim on the page.

## Rung one: something happened in a model

A compound changes something in a cell, test tube, or animal model.

That is a reason to keep investigating. It is not yet a claim about a person.

## Rung two: the ingredient has a literature

Popular ingredients often have many papers attached to their names. "Science-backed" usually stops here because this is the broadest and easiest evidence surface to find.

The existence of a literature does not tell you whether the research studied the outcome the buyer wants.

## Rung three: the ingredient was studied for the goal

The buyer is not purchasing an ingredient in the abstract. They are purchasing the possibility of better sleep, fewer cramps, more energy, or another named outcome.

This rung asks whether human research examined that goal rather than a neighboring biomarker or a different use.

It also exposes a search problem. One supplement-evidence operator publicly reported that his map had labeled 475 ingredient-goal pairings as having zero evidence. A better re-query found at least one real randomized trial for 277 of them.

That is his system's self-audit, supported by his public dataset and site caveat. It is not evidence that 58% of the entire category is wrong.

It teaches a narrower lesson: a zero on a dashboard can mean no trial exists, or it can mean the search missed the way the ingredient or outcome was named.

## Rung four: the dose matches

A trial at one dose does not automatically support a product using another.

The page should name the amount studied and the amount present. If those numbers differ, the difference is part of the claim review, not a detail to hide in a footnote.

## Rung five: the form and population match

Chemical forms, extract specifications, and study populations can change how relevant a paper is to the product in front of the buyer.

A study in one population does not become a claim about every population because the ingredient name stayed the same.

## Rung six: the finished formula was tested

This is the narrowest and usually most expensive rung.

Evidence for several ingredients does not by itself prove that the finished combination produces the marketed outcome. Formula-level evidence is becoming a competitive differentiator, but it is not a universal legal requirement simply because the ladder names it.

## Two rules underneath the ladder

**A missing result is not automatically missing evidence.** Record the search method, synonyms, databases, dates, and exclusions before calling a gap real.

**A high paper count is not automatically strong evidence.** Relevance, study design, independence, dose, population, and outcome matter more than the size of the search result.

A 2022 JAMA Network Open analysis offers a separate warning about the finished object: 17 of 30 Amazon immune-support supplements in that sample had inaccurate labels, and none displayed third-party certification seals. The date and sample size belong beside the finding every time it appears.

FTC guidance asks whether health-claim evidence is competent, reliable, and relevant to the specific claim. The six rungs are not the law. They are a way to make that relevance question operational before copy reaches the page.

Find the rung the product can actually defend.

Then write at that resolution.
