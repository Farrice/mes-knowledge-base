## Reference Asset

Women used to stop me behind a Vitamin Shoppe counter and ask whether creatine would make them look like the man on the tub.

I spent five years behind that counter. The question was never just, “Does creatine work?” It was closer to: Is this made for someone like me, at my age, in the form and amount I am about to buy?

That question is now being answered in public by scientists, podcasts, morning television, and scare content. Brand pages are only one voice in that mix.

When measured on August 1, 2026, a midlife creatine masterclass had 165,455 YouTube views. A podcast clip titled with the buyer’s question had 62,944. A Good Morning America segment had 29,034.

Those are dated attention receipts. They do not prove a measured shift in the market, purchase intent, or demand for any specific offer.

The evidence underneath the conversation needs the same restraint.

PubMed and the registration for NCT06660004 verify a randomized trial involving 36 peri- or menopausal women. It had four arms, ran for eight weeks, and used creatine hydrochloride at 750 to 1,500 mg.

That is the verified trial design. Effect sizes from the primary paper were not verified for this brief, so they do not belong in the copy. The study also cannot carry a general efficacy claim, a safety assurance, or a recommendation for consumers from 36 participants.

Now put that beside what is likely sitting on a product page.

The category-level description is LIKELY, not a product-by-product audit: creatine products commonly sold use creatine monohydrate around a 5 g serving. That means the study and the typical offer may differ in form and amount. It does not prove the product fails. It marks the point where a broad “science-backed” line can outrun the exact evidence being cited.

A kidney-scare TikTok item was also found circulating. Its performance was UNCONFIRMED. Calling it viral would turn an observed item into a reach claim the evidence does not support.

For a founder, the practical problem is evidence fit.

The buyer sees one ingredient name. The supporting material may involve another form, another amount, a narrow population, or a different outcome. If the product page smooths over those gaps, someone else gets to explain them. Sometimes that is a scientist who keeps the caveats. Sometimes it is a short clip that does not.

A stronger claims review separates four records before creative is approved:

- what the wider monohydrate literature supports;
- what this 36-person hydrochloride trial actually examined;
- what the finished product contains and claims;
- what remains unknown, unverified, or only likely.

A page built this way is stronger because it answers the buyer’s real question at the same resolution she is asking it.

State the form, then put the studied and product amounts side by side. Identify the population. Keep the uncertainty on the page.

The brand does not need to promise more than the evidence can hold. It needs to stop making the buyer assemble the truth from five different screens.
