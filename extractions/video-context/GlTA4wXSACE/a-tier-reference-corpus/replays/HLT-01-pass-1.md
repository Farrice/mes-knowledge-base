## Final Asset

*Truth-safe provisional LinkedIn post | Farrice Cain, BLEND*

For five years behind a Vitamin Shoppe counter, I heard women ask whether creatine would make them look like the man on the tub.

That question never sounded silly to me. The shelf, the label, the language around it. Everything told her who creatine was for before anyone explained what it was.

The current wave is asking a sharper version:

Is this for someone like me, at my age, at what dose?

Founders should pay attention to who is answering.

When measured on August 1, 2026, a midlife creatine masterclass had 165,455 YouTube views. A question-titled podcast clip had 62,944. A Good Morning America segment had 29,034.

A kidney-scare TikTok was also circulating. Its performance was UNCONFIRMED, so calling it viral would overstate the evidence.

Meanwhile, one piece of research in the conversation needs much better handling than most content gives it.

The randomized trial involved 36 peri- or menopausal women across four arms for eight weeks. It used creatine hydrochloride at 750 to 1,500 mg. The trial design and registration are VERIFIED through PubMed and NCT06660004.

Citing it here does not create a dosing recommendation or verify a benefit claim. The effect sizes from the primary paper were not verified for this brief, so I am not using them.

And there is a form-and-amount gap that matters.

The category commonly sells monohydrate products around 5 g, but that is graded LIKELY category knowledge. It was not a product-by-product audit.

A 36-participant trial using hydrochloride at 750 to 1,500 mg cannot be dropped beside a monohydrate tub sold around 5 g and treated like clean product proof. Sharing a category name does not make the evidence transferable.

This is where the brand-page problem shows up.

The buyer arrives with a human question. She finds a scientist explaining the trial, a podcast translating the question, morning television covering it, and scare content filling whatever uncertainty is left.

A lot of legacy creative still points back to the young male gym buyer. The page has decided who belongs before this woman reaches the evidence.

She may not see herself in it.

Brand pages have a simpler job than outshouting scientists or morning television: stop making the buyer reverse-engineer the evidence through someone else’s format.

If the product is monohydrate, say that. If the cited trial used hydrochloride, say that too. If an effect size from the primary paper has not been verified, it does not go on the page.

A better page would make the boundaries easy to see:

Who was studied.  
Which form was used.  
What amount was studied.  
How long the trial ran.  
What the study does not establish.  
How that evidence does, or does not, match the product being sold.

The page should carry no miracle claim, borrowed certainty, or medical advice.

This listening set does not prove an audience shift, virality, or a commercial opportunity. It does show the question competing voices are answering.

The strongest move is smaller and harder: answer the buyer’s real question without making the evidence bigger than it is.

## Story Deployment Receipt

- **Narrative decision:** `STORY FRAGMENT`
- **Primary communication job:** Explanation and recall. Help a performance-brand founder recognize the evidence-fit gap between the buyer’s question, available research, and legacy creative.
- **Audience relationship:** Cold-to-curious founder or growth lead with approximately two minutes of LinkedIn attention.
- **Voice and destination:** Farrice Cain, BLEND mode; provisional LinkedIn post.
- **Evidence sufficiency:** Sufficient for an evidence-led explanation with one supplied personal moment. Insufficient for a full transformation story because no supported want, obstacle, change, or discrete turn was provided.
- **Why this dosage:** The Vitamin Shoppe counter experience gives the founder a concrete recognition point. It introduces the buyer’s longstanding identity question without serving as proof of efficacy, safety, demand, or market size.
- **Rejected dosage alternatives:**
  - `FULL STORY`: Rejected because the supplied personal material contains no supported transformation or turning point. Building an arc would require invented chronology, motive, emotion, or outcome.
  - `NO STORY`: Rejected because the truthful counter moment materially clarifies why male-coded legacy creative creates an evidence-fit problem. Removing it would weaken recognition without improving factual safety.
- **Selected mechanic:** Real moment.
- **Production owner:** `viral-social-content-engine`.
- **Evidence spine:** Dated attention signals, verified trial design, explicit form-and-amount distinction, and uncertainty labels remain primary. The personal fragment supports recognition only.
- **Facts used:**
  - **SUPPLIED PERSONAL FACT:** Farrice spent five years behind a Vitamin Shoppe counter and heard women ask whether creatine would make them look like the man on the tub.
  - **DATED METRICS:** On 2026-08-01, the supplied brief recorded 165,455 YouTube views for a midlife creatine masterclass, 62,944 for a question-titled podcast clip, and 29,034 for a Good Morning America segment.
  - **VERIFIED:** A randomized, four-arm, eight-week trial involved 36 peri- or menopausal women and used creatine hydrochloride at 750–1,500 mg. Design and registration were verified through PubMed and NCT06660004 in the supplied brief.
  - **LIKELY:** Monohydrate products commonly sold around 5 g reflect category knowledge, not a product-by-product audit.
  - **UNCONFIRMED:** Performance of the circulating kidney-scare TikTok.
- **Uncertainty preserved:**
  - Primary-paper effect sizes were not verified and were omitted.
  - No product-level audit was supplied.
  - No audience shift, virality, commercial opportunity, brand-page performance, or exact-offer demand was quantitatively established.
  - PubMed, the trial registry, and the underlying source brief were not independently opened during this blind replay.
- **Factual boundary:** No dosing recommendation, benefit claim, contraindication, safety conclusion, medical advice, causal inference, or generalization from 36 participants. No specific brand identified or criticized.
- **Truth checks:**
  - The counter question was paraphrased from supplied material; no dialogue, scene texture, chronology, motive, or emotion was invented.
  - View counts include their measurement date.
  - Hydrochloride and monohydrate were kept distinct.
  - The TikTok was described as circulating, never viral.
  - The personal fragment was not used as scientific or commercial proof.
- **Constructed elements:** No analogy, composite, hypothetical case, or fictional scene was presented as real.
- **Deployment status:** Truth-safe provisional asset. Suitable for editorial and factual review, but not publication-ready until the held-out source brief and primary evidence are checked by the deployment owner.
- **Exit condition:** A 450–650 word founder-facing post with one truthful fragment, evidence-first structure, explicit uncertainty boundaries, Farrice BLEND calibration, and no consumer medical advice. Completed at 512 words.
