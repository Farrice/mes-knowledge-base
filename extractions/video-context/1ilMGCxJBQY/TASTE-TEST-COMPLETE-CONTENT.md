# Micro-Fame Authority Density: Complete Taste-Test Packet

## What you are judging

This packet shows the actual output, not a summary of the system. It uses one real health-performance claim across the four proposed series so you can judge the position, writing, authority, usefulness, and difference between content jobs.

The commercial setup remains synthetic. The research anchor is real.

## The example used

### Buyer

Founders and growth leaders at funded health and performance brands.

### Synthetic offer

**Evidence-to-Campaign Sprint, $5,000**

The offer takes one commercially important health claim from source evidence to a campaign decision: `APPROVE`, `REWRITE`, `KILL`, or `NEED MORE PROOF`.

This is a proof-case offer and price. It is not a current Farrice offer, validated demand, or collected revenue.

### Starting position

> Science-backed health content.

### Revised position

> The claim boundary is the creative brief. We turn the sentence a study can actually support into the strongest promise a health brand can responsibly take to market.

### Research anchor

The example uses the persistent claim that creatine causes hair loss.

The fear largely traces to a 2009 randomized crossover trial in 20 college-aged male rugby players. After a seven-day creatine loading phase, serum DHT rose 56% from baseline and remained 40% above baseline after 14 maintenance days. The study measured hormones. It did not measure hair loss.

A 2025 double-blind randomized trial recruited 45 resistance-trained men aged 18 to 40. Thirty-eight completed the study. Participants received 5 grams of creatine or placebo daily for 12 weeks. Researchers measured DHT and several hair outcomes using a Trichogram and FotoFinder system. They found no significant creatine-versus-placebo difference in DHT or measured hair outcomes.

The newer trial narrowed the uncertainty. It did not erase every uncertainty. It lasted 12 weeks, included only men, measured plasma rather than scalp androgens, did not record family history of hair loss, and excluded people with prior hair-restoration procedures or use of finasteride or minoxidil. One author disclosed previous research support or in-kind gifts from nutrition and sports-nutrition companies.

Primary sources:

- [2009 randomized crossover trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19741313/)
- [2025 randomized controlled trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12020143/)

## Content example 1: Claim Court

**Job:** Broad acquisition  
**Format:** LinkedIn text post  
**Claim on trial:** Creatine causes hair loss

Creatine got convicted of causing hair loss by a study that never measured anyone's hair.

In 2009, researchers gave creatine to 20 college-aged rugby players and tracked their hormones for three weeks.

DHT went up. That mattered because DHT is involved in male-pattern hair loss.

Then the internet finished the story:

creatine raises DHT  
DHT contributes to hair loss  
therefore creatine makes you bald

The first line came from the study. The last line did not.

The researchers measured serum hormones. Hair was never assessed.

In 2025, another randomized trial finally measured the outcome people had been arguing about. Thirty-eight resistance-trained men completed 12 weeks of creatine or placebo. Researchers tracked DHT, hair density, follicular units, hair thickness, and other hair measures.

They found no significant difference between groups.

That does not prove creatine could never affect anyone's hair under any condition. The trial was small, male-only, and 12 weeks long.

But it does change the verdict.

The 2009 study raised a reasonable question. It never proved the answer people repeated for the next 16 years.

**Source note:** This example uses the 2009 and 2025 trials linked above. It is educational content, not individual medical advice.

## Content example 2: Proof to Promise

**Job:** Narrow authority  
**Format:** LinkedIn document or text post for health-brand operators  
**Draft campaign claim:** “Creatine doesn't cause hair loss.”

### The sentence a creatine brand wants

> Creatine doesn't cause hair loss.

### Decision

**REWRITE**

### Why

The best direct trial is encouraging. It is still one 12-week study in a small group of resistance-trained men.

The study supports a tighter sentence:

> In a 12-week randomized trial of resistance-trained men, 5 grams of creatine per day did not increase DHT or change the hair measures tested versus placebo.

That sentence keeps the strength of the result without pretending the study answered every population, dose, duration, or genetic-risk question.

### What the evidence permits

- The trial directly measured hair instead of relying on a hormone proxy.
- Researchers found no significant creatine-versus-placebo difference in DHT or the measured hair outcomes.
- The result pushes against the common claim that ordinary creatine use causes hair loss.

### What it does not permit

- “Creatine can never affect hair loss.”
- “Creatine is proven hair-safe for everyone.”
- “This settles the question permanently.”

### The usable campaign angle

> The first trial to measure the thing people were afraid of found no difference.

The claim becomes more credible when the limit stays visible. A careful sentence gives the buyer something solid to believe. An absolute sentence gives a critic one loose brick to pull out.

**Source note:** The trial limitations include its 12-week duration, male-only sample, plasma rather than scalp androgen measurement, and missing family-history data.

## Content example 3: Campaign Decision Room

**Job:** Narrow commercial authority  
**Format:** Founder-facing teardown  
**Asset under review:** Paid social concept for a creatine brand

### Submitted concept

**Headline**

> CREATINE DOESN'T CAUSE HAIR LOSS. PERIOD.

**Body**

> The science has settled it. Take your creatine and keep your hair.

### Decision

**KILL the submitted version. Keep the campaign.**

### The problem

The new study is useful because it measured hair directly. The submitted copy wastes that advantage by making two claims the study cannot carry:

1. “The science has settled it” turns one useful trial into permanent certainty.
2. “Keep your hair” reads like a product outcome, even though the study found no between-group difference over 12 weeks. It did not prove a protective effect.

The campaign does not need a louder conclusion. It needs the exact fact competitors are skipping.

### Rebuilt concept

**Headline**

> 12 weeks. Hair measured. No difference.

**Body**

> A randomized controlled trial compared 5 grams of creatine per day with placebo in resistance-trained men. Researchers measured DHT, hair density, follicular units, hair thickness, and more. They found no significant difference between groups.

**Qualifier**

> One study cannot answer every long-term or individual-risk question. It can tell us what happened when researchers finally measured the hair.

**CTA**

> Read the study behind the claim.

### Why this version survives

It says what was tested. It shows what was measured. It keeps the result intact. The caveat does not bury the campaign because the evidence itself is the creative.

If a health brand sent me this claim before launch, the submitted version would be killed. The campaign would leave the room stronger.

## Content example 4: Claim Memo

**Job:** Controlled-chaos exploration  
**Format:** Evidence memo adapted from an investment memo  
**Claim under review:** “Creatine doesn't cause hair loss.”

### Bull case

The 2025 trial is the first randomized study to directly assess both hormones and hair outcomes after creatine supplementation.

Thirty-eight resistance-trained men completed 12 weeks of 5 grams per day or placebo. Researchers found no significant group difference in DHT, hair density, follicular units, cumulative hair thickness, or the other reported hair measures.

That is much closer to the consumer's real question than the 2009 study, which measured hormones but no hair outcome.

### Bear case

The trial cannot rule out every plausible risk.

Twelve weeks may miss a longer process. The sample included only men and did not record family history of hair loss. Plasma DHT is not the same as androgen activity inside the scalp. People who had used finasteride, minoxidil, or hair-restoration procedures were excluded.

One author also disclosed previous research support or in-kind gifts from nutrition and sports-nutrition companies. That does not invalidate the trial. It belongs in the diligence file.

### Evidence boundary

**Supported:** In this 12-week trial, 5 grams of creatine per day did not change DHT or the measured hair outcomes versus placebo.

**Too strong:** Creatine can never contribute to hair loss in any person.

**Unsupported:** Creatine protects your hair.

### Campaign decision

**APPROVE** a study-specific myth correction.  
**REWRITE** any universal safety statement.  
**KILL** protective-hair language.  
**NEED MORE PROOF** for long-term use in people with a strong family history or existing androgenic alopecia.

### What would change the memo

A longer trial, a larger sample, prespecified analysis by family-history risk, scalp-level androgen measures, and inclusion of people already concerned enough to pursue hair-loss treatment.

Until then, the clean claim is narrower than the confident one. It is also harder to knock down.
