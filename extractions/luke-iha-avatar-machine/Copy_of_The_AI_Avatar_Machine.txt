**YOUNG VS. OLD:**

1\. Young vs. Old:  
   Young individuals are typically more energetic, adaptable, and open to new experiences, while older individuals often have more life experience, wisdom, and established habits.

2\. Difference in reading ability:  
   Reading ability can vary significantly between age groups. Children are still developing their reading skills, while adults generally have a higher level of reading comprehension. However, some older individuals may experience declining vision or cognitive abilities that can impact their reading ability.

3\. Making things big:  
   When designing for older audiences, it is important to consider making visual elements, such as text and icons, larger to accommodate potential vision impairments. Increasing font sizes and using high-contrast colors can improve readability for older users.

4\. Slowing things down:  
   Older individuals may require more time to process information and navigate digital interfaces. Slowing down animations, providing longer timeouts, and allowing users to control the pace of content can enhance usability for older users.

5\. Understanding differences in technology:  
   Younger generations tend to be more tech-savvy and comfortable with adopting new technologies. Older generations may have less familiarity with modern digital tools and may require more guidance and support when using new technologies.

6\. Younger individuals more risk-tolerant than older ones:  
   Younger people are often more willing to take risks and try new things, as they have fewer responsibilities and more time to recover from potential setbacks. Older individuals tend to be more risk-averse, as they often have more to lose and may prioritize stability and security.

7\. Differences in values:  
   Different generations often have distinct values shaped by their experiences and the societal norms prevalent during their formative years. For example, older generations may prioritize tradition, loyalty, and hard work, while younger generations may value flexibility, diversity, and work-life balance.

8\. Attention spans:  
   In today's fast-paced, digital world, attention spans are shortening across all age groups. However, younger generations who have grown up with constant digital stimulation may have even shorter attention spans compared to older generations. This highlights the importance of creating engaging, concise, and easily digestible content for all audiences.

**CULTURAL BACKGROUND:**

The 4C Model of Cultural Dimensions in Consumer Behavior

Overview:  
The 4C model, developed by Shavitt et al. (2008), integrates insights from various cultural theories to identify four key dimensions that influence consumer behavior across cultures.

1\. Collectivism vs. Individualism (COL):  
\- Collectivistic cultures emphasize group harmony and interdependence.  
\- Individualistic cultures value individual uniqueness and independence.  
\- Implications: Collectivistic consumers may prefer products that reinforce social status, while individualistic consumers may seek products that express their unique identity.

2\. Certainty vs. Uncertainty (CER):  
\- High-certainty cultures prefer established brands and predictable outcomes.  
\- Low-certainty cultures are more open to novelty and risk.  
\- Implications: High-certainty consumers may favor well-known brands and proven products, while low-certainty consumers may be more willing to try new and innovative offerings.

3\. Contextuality vs. Universalism (CON):  
\- High-context cultures rely on implicit communication and particularistic norms.  
\- Low-context cultures value explicit communication and universal rules.  
\- Implications: High-context consumers may respond to subtle, implicit marketing cues, while low-context consumers may prefer explicit, information-rich appeals.

4\. Conformity vs. Creativity (CNF):  
\- Conformity-oriented cultures emphasize adherence to social norms and tradition.  
\- Creativity-oriented cultures value openness to change and self-expression.  
\- Implications: Conformity-oriented consumers may prefer products that reflect traditional values, while creativity-oriented consumers may seek products that enable self-expression and novelty.

The 4C dimensions interact to shape consumer behavior in complex ways. For example, collectivistic and high-certainty cultures may prioritize products that reinforce social status and reduce risk, while individualistic and low-certainty cultures may seek products that enable self-expression and adventure.

The 4C model offers a useful framework for understanding and predicting cross-cultural differences in consumer behavior, but should be applied thoughtfully and in conjunction with other research methods to account for nuances and variations within and across cultures.

**DESCRIPTIONS & RULES FOR EACH DIMENSION:**

**PHYSIOLOGICAL → PSYCHOLOGICAL**

This dimension explores whether a problem is primarily rooted in the mind (psychological) or the body (physiological).

Dimension Examples (High \- Psychological):  
1\. Men's dating woes: Deep-seated insecurities, social anxiety, and limiting beliefs about their own desirability often hold men back from finding love.

Dimension Examples (Low \- Physiological):  
1\. Joint pain: Arthritis and other joint issues are largely caused by physical factors like inflammation and cartilage breakdown, with a smaller psychological component.

For psychologically-rooted problems, marketers must dig deep and address the emotional and mental barriers holding people back. This means:  
\- Telling relatable stories that show you understand their struggles  
\- Tackling limiting beliefs and negative self-talk head-on

For physically-based problems, marketers should focus on:  
\- Emphasizing tangible, physical benefits like reduced pain or improved health markers  
\- Leveraging scientific evidence and expert endorsements to prove effectiveness  
\- Providing clear, practical instructions on how the solution works  
\- Focusing on the functional aspects of the problem and solution

**CAUSAL CLARITY (LOW → HIGH)**

Causal Clarity refers to how well-defined and widely accepted the root cause of a problem is in a given market. In markets with high causal clarity, there's a strong consensus about what's causing the issue. In medium causal clarity markets, there are some established causes, but there may be diagnostic uncertainty or individual variations. In low causal clarity markets, there are competing theories and a lack of agreement on the underlying cause.

Dimension Examples (High Causal Clarity):

1\. Fine lines and wrinkles: As we age, our skin naturally loses elasticity and collagen, leading to the appearance of fine lines and wrinkles. This process is widely understood and accepted as a natural part of the aging process.

Dimension Examples (Medium Causal Clarity):

1\. Back pain: Common causes of back pain include poor posture, muscle strain, and herniated discs, but identifying the specific cause often requires individual assessment.

Dimension Examples (Low Causal Clarity):

1\. Difficulty finding love: Complex factors like personal preferences, past experiences, self-esteem, and societal expectations can all play a role in someone's struggle to find a suitable romantic partner, making the exact cause unclear.

Marketing Consequences:

In high causal clarity markets, marketers can:

- Directly contradict mainstream advice to create maximum curiosity.   
- Focus primarily on SOLUTIONS, rather than investigating the root of the problem.

In medium causal clarity markets, marketers should:

- Offer diagnostic tools or assessments to help customers identify their specific causes.  
- Be careful not to “limit” their marketing by only focusing on ONE belief about the cause, or directly contradicting the market’s beliefs.

In low causal clarity markets, marketers should:

- Investigate the “why” behind the problem, and offer simple explanations.   
- Simplify the complexity of the problem to a single point. 

**VISIBILITY (LOW → HIGH)**

The visibility dimension refers to how noticeable or apparent a problem is to others. Highly visible problems are easily seen or observed, while less visible problems are more private or hidden from view. The level of visibility can significantly impact how people experience and seek solutions for their issues.

Dimension Examples (High Visibility):  
1\. Balding: Hair loss is a visible problem that can affect one's appearance and self-image.

Dimension Examples (Medium Visibility):  
1\. Bad breath: While not always immediately apparent, bad breath can become noticeable in close social interactions.

Dimension Examples (Low Visibility):  
1\. Chronic back pain: Many people with chronic back pain may not show visible signs of their condition, making it a largely invisible struggle that others may not fully understand or appreciate.

Marketing Consequences:

For highly visible problems, marketers should:  
\- Emphasize fast, noticeable results to address the urgency of the issue  
\- Use before-and-after visuals to showcase the transformation  
\- Highlight the social and confidence benefits of solving the visible problem

For less visible problems, marketers must:  
\- Validate the person's experiences and feelings, even if others can't see the struggle  
\- Avoid making it seem like the problem’s “all in their head.”

**URGENCY (LOW → HIGH)**

Urgency refers to how pressing or time-sensitive a problem feels to the individual experiencing it. Highly urgent problems demand immediate action and can cause significant stress or anxiety if left unaddressed. Less urgent problems may feel important but don't necessarily require instant solutions.

Dimension Examples (High Urgency):  
1\. Last-ditch effort to save a failing relationship: When a long-term relationship is on the brink of collapse, one or both partners may feel a strong sense of urgency to take action, such as attending couples therapy or making significant changes, in a final attempt to salvage the relationship.

Dimension Examples (Medium Urgency):  
1\. Chronic acid reflux: Persistent, recurring acid reflux that causes discomfort, interrupts sleep, and requires ongoing management.

Dimension Examples (Low Urgency):  
1\. Lack of retirement savings: The nagging worry about not having enough money saved for a comfortable retirement down the road.

Marketing Consequences:

For high urgency problems, marketers must:  
\- Use powerful, emotionally triggering language that taps into the audience's deepest fears and desires  
\- Offer immediate, tangible solutions that provide instant gratification or relief  
\- Create a sense of scarcity or limited-time opportunity to compel action

For low urgency problems, marketers can:  
\- Drive the prospect into a pain state, by painting a vivid, emotionally resonant picture of the future consequences of inaction (often through story).   
\- Generate urgency by collapsing timelines of inaction.   
\- Tap into the audience's deepest aspirations and fears to create a sense of urgency around long-term outcomes  
\- Use social proof and testimonials to make the abstract feel more concrete and urgent.

**SOCIAL STIGMA (LOW → HIGH)**

Social Stigma in marketing refers to the level of shame, embarrassment, or social disapproval associated with a particular problem or need. It reflects the deep-seated fears and vulnerabilities that prospects may experience when facing issues stigmatized by society. The dimension ranges from high social stigma, where problems carry profound shame and fear of judgment, to low social stigma, where issues are more socially acceptable and openly discussed.

Dimension Examples (High Social Stigma):  
1\. Erectile dysfunction: The deep shame and emasculation felt by men struggling with the inability to perform sexually.

Dimension Examples (Medium Social Stigma):  
1\. Credit card debt: The embarrassment and self-blame that comes with accumulating significant credit card debt.

Dimension Examples (Low Social Stigma):  
1\. Fitness challenges for busy professionals: The struggle to maintain a healthy lifestyle amidst a hectic work schedule.

Marketing Consequences:

For high social stigma issues, marketers can:   
\- Approach the market with utmost empathy, compassion, and non-judgment. Make sure they feel like the problem is SHARED widely.  
\- Provide a safe, confidential space for prospects to express their fears and vulnerabilities.

For low social stigma issues, marketers can:  
\- Take a more direct approach. 

**MEASURABILITY (LOW → HIGH)**

Dimension Explanation:  
The Measurability dimension in marketing refers to the ease or difficulty of quantifying the progress or success of addressing a particular problem. It considers whether the desired outcome can be easily measured using clear, objective metrics (high measurability) or if it is more subjective and harder to gauge (low measurability).

Dimension Examples (High Measurability):  
1\. Income growth: The increase in annual salary, freelance rates, or business revenue.

Dimension Examples (Medium Measurability):  
1\. Language learning: Progress in vocabulary acquisition, grammar mastery, or conversational fluency.

Dimension Examples (Low Measurability):  
1\. Stress reduction: Decreased feelings of anxiety, improved ability to relax, or greater overall sense of calm.

Marketing Consequences:

For high measurability problems, marketers can:   
\- Focus on the direct, concrete and measurable results they’ll get.  
\- Make tangible promises with clear timelines.

For low measurability problems, marketers should:  
\- Use highly visual, concrete imagery to create an image of life with the problem solved to make it objective in their mind. 

**PROBLEM FREQUENCY (LOW → HIGH)**

Dimension Explanation:  
The Problem Frequency dimension considers how often the prospect experiences the problem, ranging from daily struggles to rare but significant events.

Dimension Examples (High Frequency):  
1\. Chronic joint pain: The constant, nagging ache in the knees or hips that makes every step a painful reminder of the problem.

Dimension Examples (Medium Frequency):  
1\. Dating dry spells: The intermittent periods of limited romantic prospects or lack of success in finding a compatible partner.

Dimension Examples (Low Frequency):  
1\. Midlife career change: The rare but significant decision to pivot professionally, requiring a reassessment of skills, interests, and financial stability.

Marketing Consequences:

For high-frequency problems, marketers should emphasize the immediate relief and long-term benefits of their solution. 

For low-frequency problems, marketers should acknowledge the profound impact of the rare but significant event and offer support and guidance through the transformative experience. 

**LOCUS OF CONTROL (INTERNAL → EXTERNAL)**

Dimension Explanation:  
The Locus of Control dimension refers to the degree to which the prospect believes they have control over the problem and its solution. An internal locus of control suggests that the prospect feels they have the power to influence the situation, while an external locus of control indicates that the prospect believes external factors, such as luck or other people, have more control over the outcome.

Dimension Examples (Internal Locus of Control):  
1\. Copywriting skill development: The conviction that dedicated practice, learning, and effort will lead to improved writing abilities and career advancement.

Dimension Examples (Moderate Locus of Control):  
1\. Joint pain management: The understanding that while lifestyle changes and treatment can help, factors like age and genetics also play a role in joint health.

Dimension Examples (External Locus of Control):  
1\. Finding a soulmate: The belief that meeting "the one" is a matter of destiny or serendipity, rather than proactive effort and personal growth.

Marketing Consequences:

For prospects with a strong internal locus of control, marketers should:  
\- Make sure to empathize with the market’s “judgment of self” and remove it by implying or saying that it’s “not their fault.”  
\- Highlight how the PRODUCT itself will help create the transformation within them.  
\- Externalize the blame as much as possible (especially onto “other solutions”).

For prospects with a strong external locus of control, marketers should:  
\- Empathize with the “victimization” and feeling of external blame the market shares.  
\- Open up new alternative “pathways for control” that are unexpected and allow the market to take back some of that power. 

**EMOTIONAL INTENSITY (LOW → HIGH)**

The Emotional Intensity dimension refers to the level of emotional arousal and depth of feelings associated with the problem and its impact on the prospect's life. It ranges from high emotional intensity, where the problem evokes strong, visceral reactions and a profound sense of urgency, to low emotional intensity, where the problem is perceived as less emotionally charged and more practical in nature.

Dimension Examples (High Emotional Intensity):  
1\. Betrayal in a marriage: The gut-wrenching discovery of a spouse's infidelity, shattering trust, self-esteem, and the very foundation of the relationship.

Dimension Examples (Medium Emotional Intensity):  
1\. Underperforming in sales: The nagging worry and self-doubt that arises when failing to meet quotas, fearing for job security and financial stability.

Dimension Examples (Low Emotional Intensity):  
1\. Organizing a closet: The slight overwhelm and indecision that arises when trying to declutter and optimize storage space.

For high emotional intensity problems, marketers should:  
\- Be very thoughtful, and empathetic to avoid triggering a sensitive issue.   
\- Be careful not to invalidate the prospect’s pain, while also offering them a solution.

For low emotional intensity problems, marketers should:

\- Use emotional stories of pain/benefit to generate more emotion, by connecting to broader consequences in their lives.   
\- Offer more direct solutions. 

**FIRST PERSON (SELF → OTHER)**

Dimension Explanation:  
The First Person dimension considers who is primarily affected by the problem \- whether it's the prospect themselves, the prospect and others, or solely other people or entities. Understanding this dimension helps marketers tailor their messaging to address the specific concerns and motivations of the prospect, based on their level of personal involvement and the impact on those they care about.

Dimension Examples (Self):  
1\. Chronic insomnia: The maddening frustration and exhaustion of endless sleepless nights, struggling to function during the day and feeling trapped in a cycle of fatigue and despair.

Dimension Examples (Self, with impact on Others):  
1\. Erectile dysfunction affecting a marriage: The deep shame and fear of a man unable to perform intimately, worrying that his wife will lose interest and leave him for someone else.

Dimension Examples (Others):  
1\. Puppy with severe diarrhea: The concern and frustration of a new pet owner trying to identify the cause of their puppy's persistent diarrhea, while cleaning up messes and worrying about their young dog's health and well-being.

Marketing Consequences:  
For problems primarily affecting the self, marketers should:  
\- Focus primarily on the prospect’s pain point.   
\- Give them “permission to be selfish” by painting it as a selfless act that ripples outwards.

For problems primarily affecting others, marketers should:  
\- Highlight the deep connection the prospect has to the other (pet, child, family member).   
\- Rely on reciprocity to drive action (imagine what your dog would do FOR YOU).

1\. Physical appearance and attractiveness  
2\. Financial wealth and material possessions  
3\. Social status and influence  
4\. Professional titles and achievements  
5\. Fame and celebrity  
6\. Youth and vitality  
7\. Relationships and emotional dependencies  
8\. Intellectual prowess and knowledge acquisition  
9\. Cultural or societal validation  
10\. Sensory pleasures and indulgences  
11\. Independence  
12\. Power and control over others

*(I call them “false” because these are the impermanent and external resources that we mostly deal with in Direct Response marketing).*

Depending on our unique position in life: gender, age, talent, upbringing, social standing, environment, etc…

We build up these Ontological Resources differently in order to protect us against this feeling of DOOM (a.k.a. the “Core Wound”).

We experience pain when these Ontological Resources exist in two states:

1. Desire to Have  
2. Fear of Loss 


It’s these different dynamics that create the unique expression of pain that our market experiences.

Men in this market may feel that they are in competition with younger, more attractive, more successful, and more famous men, leading to a sense of inadequacy and the fear of being unable to find or keep a partner. The desire to gain fame, while not as strong as some other factors, still contributes to the overall desire to be seen as a high-value, desirable partner in the dating market.

**WOMEN’S SKINCARE (WRINKLES):**

The core wound in the women's skincare (wrinkles) market is the fear of becoming invisible and irrelevant as they age. This fear stems from the societal pressure on women to maintain a youthful, attractive appearance and the belief that their value is closely tied to their looks. As wrinkles and other signs of aging appear, women may feel that they are losing their identity, social status, and the validation they once received from others. This core wound is deeply rooted in the fear of being discarded, forgotten, or replaced by younger, more attractive women in various aspects of life, including personal relationships and professional settings
