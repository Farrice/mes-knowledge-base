Identity Process Theory (IPT) is a social psychological theory that explains how individuals construct and maintain their sense of identity. The theory was developed by Glynis Breakwell in the 1980s and has been further elaborated by other researchers since then.

Key features of Identity Process Theory include:

**Identity Structure**: IPT proposes that identity is a dynamic, multi-faceted structure that encompasses personal characteristics, social roles, and **group memberships**.

**Identity Processes**: The theory outlines two key processes involved in identity formation and maintenance:

a. **Assimilation-Accommodation**: Assimilation refers to the incorporation of new information into the existing identity structure, while accommodation involves adjusting the identity structure to fit new information.

b. **Evaluation**: This process involves assessing the content and value of identity components.

**Identity Principles**: IPT posits that individuals strive to maintain and enhance their identity in accordance with four key principles:  
a. Continuity: The desire to maintain a consistent sense of self over time.  
b. Distinctiveness: The need to feel unique and differentiated from others.  
c. Self-esteem: The motivation to maintain a positive self-image.  
d. Self-efficacy: The belief in one's ability to control and influence their environment.

**Identity Threats**: When an individual perceives a discrepancy between their identity and their experiences or social context, they may experience identity threats. These threats can be managed through various coping strategies, such as denial, re-interpretation, or adaptation.

Identity Process Theory has been applied to various domains, including health psychology, organizational psychology, and the study of social identities. It provides a framework for understanding how individuals navigate their sense of self in complex social environments and how they respond to challenges to their identity.
