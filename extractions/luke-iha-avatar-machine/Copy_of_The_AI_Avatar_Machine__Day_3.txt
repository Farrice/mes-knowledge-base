**“Garden of Eden” Story**

A good "Garden of Eden" story in marketing should evoke a sense of nostalgia, happiness, and perfection that directly contrasts with the core wound experienced by the target audience. The story should paint a vivid picture of an ideal state that the audience deeply desires but feels is currently out of reach. Here are some key features that make a compelling "Garden of Eden" story:

1\. Emotional connection: The story should create a strong emotional connection with the audience by tapping into their deepest desires and aspirations.

2\. Sensory details: Incorporating sensory details (sights, sounds, smells, etc.) helps immerse the audience in the story and makes the "Garden of Eden" feel more tangible and real.

3\. Relatability: The characters and their experiences should be relatable to the target audience, making it easier for them to see themselves in the story.

4\. Contrast with the core wound: The "Garden of Eden" should represent the exact opposite of the core wound, highlighting what the audience feels they are missing in their current lives.

5\. Simplicity and clarity: The story should be easy to understand and follow, with a clear message that resonates with the audience.

6\. Authenticity: The story should feel genuine and not overly manipulative, as audiences are more likely to connect with stories that feel authentic.

7\. Transformation: The "Garden of Eden" story should hint at the possibility of transformation, suggesting that the audience can move from their current state (core wound) to the ideal state depicted in the story.

**“Slow Descent” & “False Idols”**

The "Slow Descent" and "False Idols" narrative is a powerful storytelling technique that highlights the gradual worsening of the problem and the ineffectiveness of the solutions the prospect has tried. This approach resonates with the audience by creating a relatable and emotional journey. Let's analyze the patterns and features that make this example effective:

1\. Contrast: The story begins with a reminder of the "Garden of Eden" state, emphasizing the contrast between the ideal past and the current problematic situation.

2\. Gradual deterioration: The problem is introduced gradually, showing how it worsens over time. This allows the audience to empathize with the character's struggle.

3\. Relatable challenges: The character faces common challenges that the target audience can identify with, such as weight gain after pregnancy and the difficulty of losing weight.

4\. False solutions: The character tries various solutions (the "False Idols") that are often promoted as effective but ultimately fail to deliver results. This mirrors the audience's own experiences with unsuccessful attempts to solve their problem.

5\. Emotional impact: The story delves into the emotional consequences of the problem, such as frustration, disappointment, depression, and feelings of betrayal and failure. This emotional depth helps the audience connect with the character on a deeper level.

6\. Interpersonal consequences: The problem's impact on the character's personal life, such as their relationship and intimacy with their partner, is highlighted. This demonstrates how the issue can have far-reaching effects beyond the individual.

7\. Vivid descriptions: The story uses descriptive language to paint a clear picture of the character's struggles, making it easier for the audience to visualize and relate to the situation.

8\. Progressive intensity: The narrative builds up the intensity of the problem, showing how it gradually consumes the character's life and diminishes their "inner light." This progression keeps the audience engaged and invested in the story.

9\. Cliffhanger: The story ends on a cliffhanger, leaving the audience wanting to know how the character will overcome their challenge. This creates anticipation and sets the stage for introducing the proposed solution.

**FALL OF MAN (PIG STORY) —** 

Some Guidelines:

1. Add Stakes — Public setting, important event, something to lose.   
2. Add Delusion — Have the prospect feel at the top of the world, right before everything comes crashing down.   
3. Reflect the Core Wound — If your prospect feels worried about becoming invisible, reflect that directly (“Oh my God, I didn’t see you\!”).  
4. Make People Real, Not Assholes — It’s easy to dismiss someone being mean as just being an asshole. It’s harder if they’re just admititng the truth.

In general, PIG STORIES:

1. Relatable protagonist: Each story features a main character that the target audience can easily relate to, such as a mother, father, or someone struggling with a common problem like weight, skin issues, dental problems, or toenail fungus.  
2. Embarrassing or humiliating incident: In each story, the protagonist experiences a highly embarrassing or humiliating event that exposes their problem to others, such as a dolphin ride gone wrong, being mistaken for their twin's mother, losing a tooth during a speech, passing toenail fungus to their son, or getting stuck on a zipline.  
3. Public setting: The embarrassing incidents often occur in public settings with many witnesses, amplifying the shame and humiliation felt by the protagonist. Examples include a family vacation, a high school reunion, a work event, a swim meet, and a zipline ride.  
4. Negative impact on loved ones: The protagonist's problem not only affects them but also has a negative impact on their loved ones, such as disappointing their daughter, being compared unfavorably to a twin sister, embarrassing their son, or ruining a family activity.  
5. Vivid, descriptive language: The stories use detailed, vivid language to create a strong emotional response in the reader, such as describing the heartbroken look on the daughter's face, the metallic taste of blood in the mouth, or the shock and pity of onlookers.  
6. Cliffhanger ending: Each story ends with the protagonist in a state of distress, leaving the reader wanting a solution to their problem. This creates an opportunity to introduce the product or service being marketed as the solution.  
7. Have the protagonist feeling confident and on top of the world, only to have the rug ripped out from under him, exposing his deep insecurities and feelings of inadequacy:

**DARK NIGHT OF THE SOUL —** 

"Dark Night of the Soul" pieces effectively confront the protagonist's core wounds by following a similar structure and utilizing several key features:

1. Relatable situation: Each piece starts by describing a situation that the target audience can easily relate to, such as aging, chronic pain, or financial struggles.  
2. Gradual buildup: The problems are not presented all at once but gradually build up, making the reader empathize with the protagonist's mounting difficulties.  
3. Emotional language: The language used is highly emotional and descriptive, allowing the reader to feel the protagonist's pain, frustration, and despair.  
4. Internal monologue: The pieces are written from the protagonist's perspective, giving the reader direct access to their thoughts and feelings, which heightens the emotional impact.  
5. Specific details: Each piece includes specific, concrete details that make the situation more vivid and relatable, such as the lack of catcalls, the husband's lack of understanding, or skipping family vacations.  
6. Sense of isolation: The protagonists feel isolated and misunderstood by those around them, which amplifies their emotional distress and makes the reader more invested in their struggle.  
7. Mounting desperation: As the pieces progress, the protagonists' situations become increasingly desperate, leading to a breaking point or a crucial realization.  
8. Confronting the core wound: Each piece culminates in the protagonist directly confronting their core wound, such as feeling invisible, losing their old life, or being trapped in a cycle of financial strain.  
9. Open-ended conclusion: The pieces end without providing a clear resolution, leaving the reader hanging and more likely to seek out a solution to the protagonist's problem.

Really, what we’re doing is creating DISTINCTIONS within underlying assumptions.

For example…

Our prospect might believe… 

*"I'm just destined to have eye bags, it runs in my family."*

Which gets “bundled up” in the brain as…

This is a “bundled” concept. 

It’s an assumption. 

And we’re SPLITTING this assumption into the following…

1. Eye bags have a genetic component  
2. The genetic component isn’t the most important 

This is both congruent (non-aggressive) with their original belief… 

And it creates a NEW possibility that pushes them into a new space (buying our product).

This isn’t a new idea either…

**It comes directly from the Harvard Graduate School of Education.**

It’s a process called the [Immunity to Change Approach](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/hgse100/story/changing-better#:~:text=Based%20on%2030%20years%20of,distortions%20that%20keep%20you%20from).

These frameworks will follow this pattern:

1. Agree   
2. Wedge

Agree \= Acknowledge and agree with their “Constraint”  
Wedge \= Drive a “wedge” that creates a new distinction within their underlying assumptions.  
Elaborate \= Elaborate on the consequences of this new possibility.

This is the “AWE” Formula.

Could it be that \[new distinction\], making \[new possibility\] more achievable than we thought?"
