# The Questions Behind the Results

INTERVIEWER: You are young, you have a growing audience, and you talk about strong client results. Let us start with the uncomfortable version. Why should anyone believe this was skill and not luck?

CONSULTANT: They should not believe it just because I say it. My age does not prove I am unusually talented, and the fact that some outcomes appear in a spreadsheet does not prove I caused them. What I can do is show the decisions I made, the work I contributed, the records I have, and the limits of those records. Then somebody can judge the quality of the work without pretending we have stronger proof than we do.

INTERVIEWER: That sounds like a careful way to avoid answering. Were the results luck or not?

CONSULTANT: I do not know how much of any recorded outcome came from my work versus the client's execution, timing, market conditions, starting advantage, or luck. I can explain what I recommended and what the records say happened afterward, if the privacy and permission boundaries allow it. I cannot turn sequence into causation. If I told you, “This happened after my advice, therefore I created it,” I would be giving you a cleaner story than the evidence supports.

INTERVIEWER: Yet you market yourself with results.

CONSULTANT: I have described results, yes. The responsible version is specific about what kind of result it is. Is it a number the client self-reported? Is it an entry in an anonymized sheet? Is it independently verified? What period does it cover? How many clients are in the denominator? What is missing? In this case, the supplied outcomes are anonymized and there is no independent financial verification. That has to travel with the result every time, not appear in tiny language after the impressive part.

INTERVIEWER: Have you always done that?

CONSULTANT: I am not going to claim a perfect history when one of the concerns presented here is that outcomes may have been promised too aggressively. I can discuss my own mistakes, and that is exactly where the useful answer is. If I compressed a nuanced record into language that sounded like a likely future outcome, that was a mistake. The correction is not to say I had good intentions. The correction is to narrow the language, explain what is actually documented, and stop asking a prospect to infer typicality from a highlight.

INTERVIEWER: “If”? You are still hedging.

CONSULTANT: I am separating what is supplied from what is not. I have been told that prospects question whether outcomes were promised too aggressively. I have not been given a complete archive of every post, sales call, or proposal. So I cannot honestly confess to a specific sentence I have not seen, and I cannot honestly dismiss the concern either. I can say the standard I should be held to now: past outcomes are not a promise, recorded outcomes are not necessarily verified outcomes, and no prospect should have to guess which one I mean.

INTERVIEWER: What proof do you actually have?

CONSULTANT: An anonymized outcomes spreadsheet, refund-policy records, and permission to discuss my own mistakes. That is the supplied proof. No client identities. No independent financial verification. I am not going to make the spreadsheet sound like an audit. Anonymized means identities are withheld. It does not mean a third party verified the underlying numbers.

INTERVIEWER: Then the spreadsheet could be fiction.

CONSULTANT: That is a legitimate limitation. Without independent verification, an outside person cannot treat it as audited financial evidence. What I can do is make the record more interpretable. I can disclose how entries were collected, what fields exist, the time period, the denominator, which rows are included or excluded, whether the figures are self-reported, and where data is missing—assuming those details can be established from the actual records. If they cannot be established, I have to say that too. Transparency is not me holding up a spreadsheet on camera. It is giving you the context needed to know what the spreadsheet can and cannot support.

INTERVIEWER: Why anonymize it? Convenient that nobody can check with the clients.

CONSULTANT: I do not have permission to identify them. Privacy is not evidence of truth, but violating privacy would not make the underlying claim more responsible. It would just create a second problem. The right answer is to preserve the identity boundary and lower the claim to the strength of the evidence. If later a client independently agrees to an identifiable case and the underlying result can be verified, that is different evidence. I do not have that here.

INTERVIEWER: Can you at least say these results are typical?

CONSULTANT: No. Not from what has been supplied. To say typical, I would need a defensible cohort, a denominator, an outcome definition, a time window, inclusion and exclusion rules, and enough complete data to justify the statistic. I would also have to be clear about what “typical” means. Median? Most common? Average? Percentage above a threshold? Right now, I can say the sheet contains recorded outcomes if that is confirmed by inspecting it. I cannot say those outcomes represent what a new client should expect.

INTERVIEWER: So why should a prospect care about the results at all?

CONSULTANT: Because bounded evidence can still help somebody evaluate judgment. A result record can point to a case worth examining. Then the useful content is not, “Look how much money somebody made.” It is: what was the starting condition, what decision was made, what work did I contribute, what did the client do, what was observed, what limits remain, and what lesson transfers without promising the same outcome. Even if you never hire me, you should leave with a better way to make a decision. Otherwise the client result is just decoration around my reputation.

INTERVIEWER: Let us talk refunds. How many refunds have you had?

CONSULTANT: I have refund-policy records, but no counts or time period have been supplied in this conversation. I am not going to invent a number. A responsible answer would state the policy terms, the period being discussed, the number of relevant purchases, the number of refund requests, the number granted or denied, and any limitations in the records. Without those details in front of me, the answer is: I do not have a number I can responsibly give you here.

INTERVIEWER: That sounds terrible. Surely you know whether refunds are high or low.

CONSULTANT: I might have an impression, but an impression is not the record. And even a low refund rate would not prove clients succeeded or that every sales conversation was fair. People do not request refunds for all kinds of reasons. Some people are satisfied. Some do not know the policy. Some do not want the conflict. Some may not qualify. A refund record tells you about requests and decisions under a policy. It does not automatically tell you what every client experienced.

INTERVIEWER: Then what would publishing the refund policy accomplish?

CONSULTANT: It would help a prospect understand the decision they are making. Full terms, eligibility, exclusions, process, timing, and what happens in a dispute. If I publish only the comforting line, “We stand behind our work,” that does not create safety. If I publish a dramatic claim like “almost nobody asks for a refund” without the denominator and period, that is spectacle. The useful thing is the inconvenient detail. What does the policy actually cover, and what does it not cover?

INTERVIEWER: Did you overpromise?

CONSULTANT: The concern exists, so I should treat it seriously. But I do not have the complete evidence needed to give you a universal yes or no about every message. I can say what would count as overpromising: presenting a standout result as expected, implying I control variables I do not control, using a client's self-report as if it were independently verified, or making the refund policy sound broader than it is. If I find language like that in my own material, I should correct it in the same place with comparable visibility, not quietly update a footnote.

INTERVIEWER: You are asking people to trust the person accused of exaggerating to conduct the review of their own exaggeration.

CONSULTANT: That is a fair objection. Self-correction is not independent review. It is still better than no correction, but it should not be sold as external validation. I can publish the standard I am using, show how a statement changed, preserve the limitations, and make the underlying records available to an authorized reviewer if that becomes possible. Until independent verification exists, the claim remains limited. I do not get to award myself credibility for being transparent about evidence that is still self-reported.

INTERVIEWER: Is your age relevant at all?

CONSULTANT: It is relevant to my story. It is not proof of effectiveness. Being young does not make me lucky by definition, and it does not make me a prodigy by definition. If somebody thinks age increases the chance that a result was luck, I should answer with inspectable work, not a speech about doubters. Show the diagnosis. Show the choices. Show what changed when new information arrived. Show where the advice failed. A prospect should evaluate the quality and consistency of judgment, not decide that youth itself is evidence for or against me.

INTERVIEWER: But your growing audience is evidence that you know what you are doing.

CONSULTANT: It is evidence that the audience is growing, assuming that growth is documented. It may show that content is earning attention. It does not prove client outcomes, ethical selling, delivery quality, or typical results. Audience growth and client value are different records. Combining them makes a stronger story, but not stronger evidence.

INTERVIEWER: What is the mistake you are willing to admit?

CONSULTANT: I am allowed to discuss my own mistakes, but no specific mistake has been supplied for me to narrate here. I will not manufacture a vulnerable story because it would play well in an interview. The honest answer is that I need to choose a real, documented decision I got wrong, explain what I believed at the time, what signal I missed, what the consequence was, and what rule changed afterward. The value is in the changed judgment. If the story only makes me look brave for confessing, it is reputation theater.

INTERVIEWER: What about showing the spreadsheet on screen? Scroll through it. Let people see there are lots of rows.

CONSULTANT: Lots of rows are not context. A fast scroll creates the feeling of volume while making the details impossible to inspect. It also risks exposing identifying information. I would rather recreate the schema safely and walk through how the data was collected, which fields are complete, how outcomes are defined, and what cannot be concluded. If there is a distribution that can be calculated responsibly, show the distribution, including weak or negative entries if they exist. If the sheet cannot support that analysis, do not turn it into a visual prop.

INTERVIEWER: Would you publish the worst result?

CONSULTANT: If the record is real, relevant, privacy-safe, and its inclusion helps people understand the range, I should not hide it merely because it is inconvenient. But I also should not invent a “worst result” from incomplete data or expose an individual client. The right unit may be an aggregate or a de-identified account, depending on what the records and permissions support. The principle is not “publish something embarrassing.” It is “do not create a representative claim by removing the cases that weaken it.”

INTERVIEWER: What if your refund records and outcomes sheet disagree with the story you have been telling?

CONSULTANT: Then the story changes. The records outrank the brand narrative. I should identify the exact statement that no longer holds, correct it clearly, explain the evidence boundary, and keep the correction visible. Reputation built on avoiding a correction is already fragile. The point is not to perform moral purity. It is to make future decisions safer for the buyer.

INTERVIEWER: You make this sound like you deserve credit for basic honesty.

CONSULTANT: I do not deserve a medal for using accurate language. Transparency is not a heroic act. It is an operating behavior that becomes meaningful when it repeats, especially when restraint costs attention or makes the sales story less exciting. One interview does not prove I am trustworthy. A consistent pattern of accurate claims, visible corrections, clear policy terms, useful case walkthroughs, and refusal to overstate the evidence might help over time.

INTERVIEWER: What would you want a prospect to ask you before buying?

CONSULTANT: Ask me where each result came from. Ask whether it is self-reported or independently verified. Ask how many clients are represented and how many are missing. Ask what period the result covers. Ask what I actually did and what the client did. Ask what other explanations could account for the outcome. Ask what I am promising in the agreement. Ask how the refund policy works in practice. Ask what happens if the evidence is incomplete. And if my answer is a story that never reaches the question, do not reward me for sounding confident.

INTERVIEWER: What are you promising, then?

CONSULTANT: I can promise the terms and behaviors that are actually within my control only if those terms are defined in the engagement. I cannot promise a client outcome from the evidence supplied here. I can describe my process, my standards, what access or deliverables the agreement includes, how decisions will be documented, and how the refund policy operates. The buyer still has to decide whether that process and evidence justify the purchase.

INTERVIEWER: Does that not make your marketing weaker?

CONSULTANT: It makes the claim smaller. It can make the decision stronger. A buyer who understands the limits may decide not to buy. That is not automatically a marketing failure. If the only way the offer converts is by letting the prospect infer a result I cannot support, then conversion is hiding a trust problem.

INTERVIEWER: How do you rebuild a reputation once people think you got lucky and exaggerated?

CONSULTANT: Not with one takedown video, not by exposing clients, and not by staging a courtroom where I defeat every skeptic. You rebuild by showing the work repeatedly. Publish how you evaluate a case. Explain the mechanism and alternatives. State what is missing. Make the terms readable. Correct overstatement. Show a real mistake when you have one and explain the rule it changed. Update the evidence when the dataset changes. Let later claims remain consistent with earlier limits. Reputation is accumulated behavior, not a dramatic receipt dump.

INTERVIEWER: And if independent verification never arrives?

CONSULTANT: Then independent verification never becomes part of the claim. I can still be useful. I can teach from my decisions. I can show de-identified work where permission allows. I can report self-reported outcomes as self-reported. I can make my policies clear. I can keep a versioned record and invite scrutiny. But I cannot cross the line from “this is what was reported” to “this is proven and typical” because I am tired of the limitation.

INTERVIEWER: Final question. Why should anyone trust you now?

CONSULTANT: They should not decide from this interview alone. They should inspect whether my evidence matches my language, whether I answer the hard question directly, whether my terms are clear, whether corrections stay visible, whether my work is useful without the result claim, and whether that behavior holds over time. I am not entitled to trust because I disclosed a limitation. I am responsible for giving people enough information to decide whether trust is warranted.
