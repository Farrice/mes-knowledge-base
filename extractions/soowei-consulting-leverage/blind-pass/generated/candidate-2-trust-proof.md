## Earliest Missing Stage

**Earliest missing stage: Credible.**

The known skepticism is not primarily “I do not notice this person” or “I do not like this person.” Prospects are challenging whether the reported outcomes are believable, representative, and attributable to skill rather than luck. That is a credibility problem before it is a trust-safety problem.

The aggressive-promise concern also creates a later **Trustable** deficit: even if prospects believe the consultant has produced some results, they may not feel safe interpreting the marketing or entering the engagement. But trustable content cannot repair an earlier credibility gap by displaying more confidence, more lifestyle, or more favorable anecdotes. First show what is known, how it was produced, what is not known, and where the consultant's contribution stops. Then demonstrate consistent restraint over time.

Diagnosis basis: summarized objections supplied in the brief. Because no Buyer Mirror map, content inventory, or observed behavior was supplied, the diagnosis remains a bounded working diagnosis to review when direct buyer evidence becomes available.

## Required Buyer Belief and Proof

| Stage | Buyer belief required | Proof that can support it now | Proof still missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Admirable | “This consultant has a standard or capability worth paying attention to.” | A real achieved outcome in the consultant's own work, if documented; willingness to disclose uncertainty and mistakes | Specific owned achievements and their source records were not supplied |
| Likable | “I recognize the person behind the expertise and understand how they make decisions.” | Honest accounts of the consultant's own mistakes and changed judgment | Lived details, values, and audience response are not supplied; vulnerability must not be staged |
| Credible | “The consultant can explain the mechanism, show the work, and distinguish evidence from interpretation.” | Anonymized outcomes spreadsheet after a methods check; worked examples stripped of identity; decision walkthroughs; explicit limits | Independent financial verification, causal attribution, typicality evidence, complete cohort definitions, and client permission for identifiable cases |
| Trustable | “I can interpret the evidence safely, understand the promise, and expect the same restraint after I buy.” | Full refund-policy records with context; public claim standard; correction process; repeated limitation statements; consultant-owned mistake stories | Evidence of consistent sales-call wording, independently audited claims, direct buyer-safety signals, and permission for client testimony |

The immediate content job is not to prove that “the system works for everyone.” The job is to make the consultant's evidence legible: what the records contain, what process was used, what alternative explanations remain, and what is explicitly not promised.

## Sequenced Portfolio

Use the following ten-asset sequence only after each asset's source material passes the stated evidence gate. If a gate fails, hold the asset; do not replace the missing fact with a stronger story.

| # | Asset brief | Primary trust job | Evidence used | Transition and proof gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **“The result I am not willing to promise”** — a short statement separating recorded past outcomes from a prospect's future result, followed by the variables the consultant does and does not control | Credible | Consultant's current claim standard; supplied fact that no independent verification exists | Moves “sounds aggressive” toward “defines the limits.” Publish no outcome figure unless its source, period, and definition are documented |
| 2 | **“What is actually inside the outcomes sheet”** — a screen-led methods walkthrough using a recreated, privacy-safe schema rather than exposing client rows | Credible | Spreadsheet structure after review | Shows the evidence process, not a victory montage. Must state cohort, denominator, dates, included/excluded rows, self-report status, missing fields, and that the records are not independently verified |
| 3 | **“Distribution, not highlight reel”** — present the range of recorded outcomes, including null or negative rows if present, with no “average client” conclusion unless the dataset supports it | Credible | Checked spreadsheet calculations | Moves isolated anecdotes toward bounded pattern evidence. Requires reproducible calculations and an explicit note that recorded association is not causal proof; if the sheet cannot support a distribution, do not make this asset |
| 4 | **“One decision, shown end to end”** — walk through an anonymized consulting decision: starting condition, evidence considered, recommendation, observable next step, recorded outcome, and competing explanations | Credible | De-identified row plus underlying work product only if permission and privacy allow | Answers the luck objection with visible judgment, not age defensiveness. Must not imply that the outcome was caused solely by the consultant |
| 5 | **“Where my advice was wrong”** — describe one consultant-owned mistake, the signal missed, the correction, and the operating rule that changed | Likable | Permission to discuss the consultant's own mistakes | Adds human context without borrowing a client's identity or manufacturing vulnerability. Requires a real mistake and documentation sufficient to keep the account accurate |
| 6 | **“What age cannot prove”** — a concise piece refusing both age-as-disqualification and age-as-authority, then showing the decision standard prospects should use instead | Admirable | Consultant's own age and documented work standard | Converts the age debate into an inspectable standard: mechanism, evidence, limits, and consistency. No “youngest,” “fastest,” or exceptional-status claim without support |
| 7 | **“The refund policy, including the inconvenient parts”** — explain eligibility, exclusions, process, counts by a defined period, and what the records cannot reveal about satisfaction or outcomes | Trustable | Complete refund-policy records | Moves policy from reassurance language to decision safety. Must include denominator and time window; cannot equate low refunds with client success or fair selling without further evidence |
| 8 | **“How we write outcome language before it goes public”** — publish a claim ladder: verified record, self-reported record, interpretation, hypothesis, and prohibited promise, with examples rewritten conservatively | Trustable | Current internal claim rules and supplied evidence limits | Demonstrates restraint as a repeatable behavior. Examples must use redacted or hypothetical wording and may not invent actual results |
| 9 | **“Questions skeptical prospects should ask me”** — give prospects an evaluation checklist covering data source, denominator, typicality, consultant contribution, refund terms, and what happens when evidence is missing | Trustable | The known objections and established limits | Turns skepticism into informed choice rather than spectacle. The consultant must answer each question with a sourced fact, “not known,” or “not verified” |
| 10 | **“Evidence update: what changed, what did not”** — a recurring quarterly ledger of corrected fields, new denominators, limitations, claim changes, and unresolved gaps | Trustable | Versioned spreadsheet and policy records over time | Accumulates trust through consistent behavior. It is not a celebration post; any change must cite the underlying record and retain historical corrections |

### Sequence logic

1. Assets 1–4 repair the earliest credibility gap by replacing compressed result claims with methods, distributions, and visible decisions.
2. Asset 5 adds real humanity only after the consultant has shown evidentiary restraint; the mistake is useful because it changes a rule, not because confession attracts attention.
3. Asset 6 reframes age around a decision standard rather than performing defensiveness or precociousness.
4. Assets 7–10 build trustable behavior: full policy context, a public claim standard, buyer-protective questions, and recurring corrections.

The sequence does not require a client reveal, staged confrontation, public “receipts” spectacle, or a dramatic refund story. Transparency means giving the buyer enough context to interpret the evidence. Spectacle means using exposure, humiliation, cherry-picked screenshots, or emotional intensity to substitute for missing context.

## Claim Constraints

- Describe spreadsheet outcomes as anonymized records or self-reported client outcomes unless independent verification is later obtained.
- Never call the outcomes typical, average, representative, guaranteed, or expected without a defined cohort and defensible analysis supporting that exact term.
- Do not infer causation from before/after rows. State the consultant's documented action, the recorded outcome, and plausible contributing factors separately.
- Do not imply that age proves exceptional ability or that skepticism about age has been disproved by audience growth.
- Do not expose client names, identifying combinations, screenshots, or row-level details that can re-identify a client.
- Do not use a low refund count, if one exists, as proof of client success, ethical selling, or satisfaction. State the policy, period, denominator, and record limitations.
- Do not call anonymization independent verification.
- Do not convert a consultant-owned mistake into a client story without permission.
- Do not promise an outcome. A compliant frame is: this is what the record says, this is how the consultant contributed, these are the limits, and this is what a prospect must decide for themselves.
- Correct prior overstatement in the same channel and with comparable prominence when the underlying evidence warrants correction.
