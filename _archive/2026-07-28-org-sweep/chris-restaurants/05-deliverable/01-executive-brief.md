## A fact the menu work surfaced

The address at **201 N Maryland Avenue in Glendale** is not just where Panda Inn seats its Saturday-night regulars. One block from that dining room, in the food court of Glendale Galleria II, October 1983, Andrew Cherng opened the first Panda Express after a developer who had eaten at the Panda Inn two blocks away asked him to. Forty-three years and 2,600 stores later, the founding address of the world's largest Asian restaurant chain sits on the menu nowhere. Not on the website. Not on the Google profile. Not on the wall as you walk in.

The Glendale dining room is, quietly, the birthplace of Panda. It has been the birthplace for four decades. Its customers do not know. The full one-page brief on what to do with this is in `07-the-founding-myth-brief.md` if anything in the menu analysis makes the activation feel worth the print run.

## What Chris asked for, done the right way

Chris's ask was specific: average pricing for mains, apps, and sides against the surrounding market; what items are common across the competitive set; where the menu can be made more efficient. That work sits in three dedicated files in this folder. One per restaurant. Each with pricing bands computed against a 5-mile competitor set, common-item analysis, and specific efficiency recommendations defensible from public data alone.

The short version of that work, per restaurant:

**Wasabi at CityWalk** prices its mains and signature rolls in the **casual-mid Ventura Boulevard band** ($19.90 average signature roll; $20.33 average main) while rating **0.4 stars below the area median Japanese restaurant** (4.1 versus 4.50 across 40 deduped competitors within 5 miles). The menu runs a flat-priced sushi grid: every nigiri $9 regardless of fish, sweet egg costing what scallop costs, which has the kitchen subsidizing premium fish with cheap fish on every check. No sake program in an entertainment district where sake bombs are a tourist ritual. No anchor $32–$38 signature roll to make the $20 rolls feel like the value choice. The **Spicy Garlic Edamame** has already crossed into signature-dish status on Yelp (its own photo gallery) and is still being treated like an $8 side. (Source: `02-menu-intelligence-wasabi.md`, with raw menu at `_raw-data/wasabi-menu.md`.)

**Yakiya Pasadena** sits in the upper third of the Pasadena omakase/tasting-menu median at $128 per person, but the experience is being undercut by **KAVIAR at $109** (four miles west, four times the review count) and **Sushi Karen at $65–$96** (fourteen seats, 4.9 stars. The highest-rated Japanese restaurant in the 5-mile radius). The deeper finding is not about pricing. Cocktails are held at **$13–$15** in a concept where the Pasadena premium floor is $16–$22, and a verified party-of-four receipt from July 2024 shows a **6% beverage attach rate** against a 20–30% industry benchmark. That gap alone models to **$300,000–$700,000 per year** in unmonetized beverage revenue at this one location. A separate finding, harder to read in a menu audit and impossible to unsee once it surfaces, is described in the Yakiya file in full: the $128 A5 Miyazaki tasting is being cooked tableside by inexperienced waitstaff rather than a chef, documented by an eyewitness review with the full receipt. Overcooked tongue. Undercooked ribeye. No salt provided. That is a $40-per-pound raw-cost protein being burned in front of a paying customer. (Sources: `03-menu-intelligence-yakiya.md`, eyewitness review at Parkzer.com 2024-07-17, beverage mirror at yakiya.res-menu.net.)

**Panda Inn Glendale** occupies a clean slot the rest of the Glendale corridor has left open: heritage upscale-casual Chinese-American, cocktails, private dining, named-server service intensity, in a 190,000-person city whose only authentic-Sichuan sit-down competitor (Lao Sze Chuan) closed. The menu's **two-portion architecture** ($17/$33 on Mongolian Beef, $21/$40 on Honey Walnut Shrimp) is buried: walk-in diners learn it from the server, delivery-app customers see it as "Priced by add-ons." The **lunch-specials program** exists on a URL most customers never see (`pandainn.com/menu_type/lunch-specials/`) and is surfaced nowhere on Google, Uber Eats, Postmates, or the Google Business profile. There is no Peking Duck at the one sit-down Mandarin restaurant in central Glendale in 2026. The Sichuan adjacency sits wide open, and the founders are Sichuan-trained. (Source: `04-menu-intelligence-panda-inn.md`, pricing from Postmates and Uber Eats cross-references.)

## The three findings that travel together

Across the three restaurants, three patterns show up that no single-location menu audit would have caught.

**1. Each concept is a category monopolist that behaves like it has competition it doesn't have.** Wasabi is the only sushi restaurant inside a free-to-enter entertainment district that 9 million visitors enter every year, and CityWalk's own "Guide to Dining" blog post doesn't list it. Panda Inn is the only sit-down heritage Chinese restaurant within walking distance of both The Americana at Brand and the Glendale Galleria, and the "birthplace of Panda Express" line is nowhere on anything customer-facing. Yakiya is the only premium Japanese restaurant for 2.5 miles in any direction in East Pasadena — a market with the demographics to support it — while the discerning premium customer drives four miles to Old Pasadena out of habit. In each case the category is owned and the category ownership is not being pressed.

**2. The expensive things and the cheap things are wearing the same prices.** Wasabi's nigiri grid is flat at $9 across ten fish (inari and sweet egg cost what scallop costs). Yakiya's $78 shabu tasting sits between Osawa's $21 rib-eye cut and Wagyu Master's unlimited A5, with nothing in the beverage ladder between a $24 sake and a $130 bottle of Dassai. Panda Inn's large-portion family entrees are hidden behind a small-portion sticker price on delivery apps. Three different PPA problems, same shape: **the pricing architecture isn't working as an architecture, it's working as a single line.**

**3. Each restaurant has one piece of brand capital already sitting on the table, uncashed.** The Garlic Edamame at Wasabi. The bone marrow bibimbap at Yakiya. The founding story at Panda Inn. Reviewers are naming all three unprompted. The menus, the websites, and the host-stand stories are not.

## What this means for you

Chris asked the menu question. The menu question has answers. Three files of them, each one defensible line by line from public data. That's the work, and that's what this folder is. The pattern behind the answers is incidental — the kind of thing you only see when you look at three restaurants in the same portfolio in the same week — but the answers stand on their own without it.

A few things this folder is not. It is not a slide deck. It is not a pitch to replace anyone's team. It is not a proposal to bring AI to a company whose co-founder earned a PhD in electrical engineering and wrote custom POS analytics for Panda Inn in 1975 — a fact we came across while researching this and will not insult the reader by pretending we didn't know. **Peggy Cherng was running data-driven restaurant operations before most people selling "AI for restaurants" today were born.** Nothing in this folder is built on the premise that PRG needs to be taught about data.

What this folder **is**, beyond the menu work itself, is a specialist's read on the same four legacy concepts (Panda Inn, Yakiya, Wasabi, Hibachi-San) that sit inside the same portfolio as 1,650 Panda Express stores and cannot compete with Panda Express for the corporate CIO's quarterly attention. Not because the CIO is wrong. Because the arithmetic isn't close. One Panda Express store rolled out across 1,650 units moves a number the whole company feels. One experiment at Yakiya Pasadena, however good, does not. The most concrete example sits at Wasabi: the location runs Digital Dining, a legacy POS that no cloud AI vendor has a plug-and-play connector for, which means the corporate BI roadmap structurally cannot reach it this fiscal year — and that single fact is what "side-quest specialist" means in practice. That observation is in the folder for the reader who wants it. It is not the point of the folder.

The point of the folder is the menu work. The contextual files that follow earn their read on their own if the menu work makes them feel worth opening. The **founding myth brief** (`07-the-founding-myth-brief.md`) is one page on activating an asset Panda Inn already owns. The **90-day roadmap** (`08-90-day-ai-transformation-roadmap.md`) sketches what a structured engagement could look like, with the limits named first. The **opportunity prioritization matrix** (`09-opportunity-prioritization-matrix.md`) is a 90-second read on what to do first, second, and third, and why. None of those files are the deliverable. The deliverable is the menu intelligence. They're the receipts the menu work generated along the way.

The rest of it, starting with Wasabi on the next file, is the work Chris asked for. Executed at the level it deserved.
