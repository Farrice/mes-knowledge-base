# 13 — Chris Handoff Script

**Audience:** Chris. Written in his voice, not Farrice's. Plain, slightly casual, confident but not pushy.
**Purpose:** Make it effortless for Chris to walk this folder upward without feeling like he's making a sales pitch.
**Unresolved context:** Chris's role inside PRG is not confirmed. Variant A assumes he's PRG corporate (inside the building). Variant B assumes he's external (third-party manager). Farrice picks at Checkpoint 2 based on what he knows from conversation with Chris.

---

## Design principles (read first)

1. **Chris is the hero, not Farrice.** Every version of the handoff positions Chris as the operator who brought a solution to the table — not as someone forwarding a salesman's pitch. If Chris isn't positioned as the one who "found this," the frame collapses.
2. **Short. Forwardable. Screenshot-proof.** Every message below has to survive being forwarded, screenshotted, or read in 15 seconds. Length kills momentum.
3. **The deliverable does the defending.** Chris is not selling. The folder is selling. His job is to point at it and let it speak. Every line below frames the folder as the thing that matters, not the person who built it.
4. **"Would Chris text this to his group chat?"** If the voice feels consultant-polished, it's wrong. Rewrite until it reads like Chris's actual texting voice.
5. **No "Here's what..." openers.** Banned. Chris doesn't talk like that. Neither does the script.

---

# VARIANT A — Chris is PRG Corporate (Inside The Structure)

**Assumption:** Chris reports up through a Regional Director or directly to Gigi Cheung's team. The deliverable becomes his tool for showing initiative and getting noticed for running the legacy concepts well.

**The emotional frame for Chris:** *"I didn't hire a consultant. I asked a friend who works in AI a simple question, and he took it way further than I expected. I looked at what he built and thought — someone upstairs should see this."*

---

## A1 — The Internal Text (to his regional manager or Gigi's chief of staff)

**Context:** Chris is in a 1:1 with his boss, or sending a Slack DM between meetings. Informal. Two paragraphs max.

> "Quick thing. A friend of mine who does AI consulting was going to help me do menu analysis for Wasabi, Yakiya, and Panda Inn — just the standard competitive pricing stuff. He ended up going way deeper than I expected. Built a full folder. I think it's worth 15 minutes of your time, or forwarding to whoever runs concept strategy.
>
> Couple things stood out — he found about $300K–$700K a year of unmonetized beverage revenue at Yakiya based on our own receipts vs Pasadena benchmarks. He caught that Panda Inn Glendale is literally on the same corner as the birthplace of Panda Express and that story isn't anywhere on the website or menu. He also noticed Wasabi is on Digital Dining and has a specific plan for getting it onto the same analytical layer as the other concepts. Anyway — folder is here [LINK]. Happy to walk you through it if useful."

**Why this works:**
- Opens with the original task ("menu analysis") so Chris's boss doesn't think he went rogue.
- The "went way deeper than I expected" line gives Chris plausible deniability. He's not advocating — he's observing.
- Two specific, dollar-attributed findings in the second paragraph. Not a pitch, just facts.
- Closes with "forwarding to whoever runs concept strategy" — Chris offers the escalation path without taking responsibility for the call.
- "Happy to walk you through it if useful" preserves Chris's role as the internal expert without making it about Farrice.

---

## A2 — The In-Person Line (if he catches Gigi or a director in the hallway)

**Context:** Chris has 30 seconds. One sentence, one hook.

> "Hey Gigi — do you have 10 minutes this week? A friend of mine who does AI work put together something on Yakiya, Wasabi, and Panda Inn that I think you should see before I forward it up. He found a revenue thing at Yakiya I didn't know about."

**Why this works:**
- "Do you have 10 minutes this week?" asks for a tiny commitment, not a meeting. Nobody says no to 10 minutes.
- "Before I forward it up" is a political frame — it tells Gigi that Chris respects her chain of command AND that he has something worth her pulling rank on.
- "A revenue thing I didn't know about" is the curiosity hook. Chris is admitting he missed something, which disarms any defensiveness and creates space for Gigi to take the conversation seriously.

---

## A3 — The Email Version (if Chris has to write it up formally)

**Subject line:** *Yakiya / Wasabi / Panda Inn — outside analysis worth 15 min*

**Body:**

> Gigi,
>
> I asked a friend of mine — Farrice, he does AI consulting — to help me with the menu analysis you [or Regional Director X] asked me to look at for Wasabi, Yakiya, and Panda Inn. He did the analysis, then kept going. The folder he sent me has more in it than I expected, and three of the findings felt like things your team should see directly instead of me paraphrasing them:
>
> 1. Yakiya's beverage attach rate is 6%, against a Pasadena premium floor of 20%+. Based on our own receipts and 9 years of cover data, that's roughly $300K–$700K per year of unmonetized beverage revenue at one location. He has the math in the folder.
>
> 2. Panda Inn Glendale is on the corner of the food court that became the first Panda Express in 1983. That fact isn't on our website, our menu, or our Google profile. He outlined what activating it could look like — zero new menu cost.
>
> 3. He noticed Wasabi is on Digital Dining and has a specific scoped plan for the analytics bridge — three implementation paths, costed, with the procurement conversation as week one. It's the kind of single-location engineering work the corporate roadmap would never reach.
>
> Folder is here: [LINK]
>
> If any of it is useful, he's happy to walk you through what he found. If not, I wanted you to see it so nothing gets missed.
>
> Chris

**Why this works:**
- Subject line is neutral. Not a pitch, a piece of internal intelligence.
- "I asked a friend" keeps Chris in the active voice. He initiated this, not Farrice.
- Two specific findings in bullet form — scannable, dollar-attributed, defensible.
- "He has the math in the folder" invites Gigi to verify rather than trust.
- "If not, I wanted you to see it so nothing gets missed" is the CYA close. Chris is doing his job, not selling.
- Signed just "Chris" — no title, no urgency, no calendar request.

## A4 — The Slack / DM Version

**Context:** Internal comms channel, async, 2-3 lines max.

> "Hey — quick one. Asked a friend who does AI to help with the menu analysis for the three concepts and he over-delivered pretty hard. Worth a look if you have 10 min: [LINK] — the Yakiya beverage thing on page 3 surprised me."

**Why this works:**
- "Over-delivered pretty hard" is a Chris-voice phrase. Casual, accurate, not consultant-speak.
- The specific page reference ("Yakiya beverage thing on page 3") gives the reader a hook and a commitment device. Almost everyone who reads this will open to page 3 first.
- 10 minutes = the universally acceptable "no commitment" frame.

---

## A5 — The Follow-Up Text After The Meeting

**Context:** Chris's boss or Gigi has seen the folder and either said "interesting" or "let's talk." This is the next-day check-in text.

> "Appreciate you taking a look at the folder yesterday. If you want me to loop Farrice in directly, I can make the intro. Otherwise happy to let it sit and you can come back to me whenever."

**Why this works:**
- "Appreciate you taking a look" is a gratitude anchor, not a push.
- "Loop Farrice in directly" is the permission request — Chris is asking to escalate without asking to escalate.
- "Let it sit" is the pressure release. It tells leadership Chris isn't going to chase. That alone makes them more likely to respond — the absence of pressure is its own form of credibility.

# VARIANT B — Chris is External (Third-Party Manager)

**Assumption:** Chris is a store-manager-equivalent operating the three concepts under a contract or franchise-like arrangement, not directly inside PRG corporate. The deliverable becomes Chris's way of introducing Farrice as someone he trusts to people he knows.

**The emotional frame for Chris:** *"I've been quietly working with this guy on something relevant. It's not a pitch, it's a heads-up — I think you should meet him before anyone else does."*

---

## B1 — The Warm Introduction Text

**Context:** Chris texts the owner/executive he has a relationship with. Casual, personal voice.

> "Hey — want to run something by you. Been working with a guy named Farrice on a menu analysis project for Wasabi, Yakiya, and Panda Inn. He's an AI strategy guy, really sharp, and what he put together goes way beyond what I asked for. I think you should see it before I do anything else with it. Folder's here: [LINK]. No pressure, just wanted it in your hands first."

**Why this works:**
- "Want to run something by you" is relationship language. Chris is consulting, not presenting.
- "Been working with a guy named Farrice" establishes that Chris has vetted Farrice — there's already a relationship, which transfers trust.
- "You should see it before I do anything else with it" is a loyalty signal. Chris is saying "you get first look," which creates urgency without pressure.
- "No pressure, just wanted it in your hands first" is the soft close. It tells the reader the decision is theirs and Chris isn't going to chase.

---

## B2 — The "I've Been Thinking About This" Follow-Up

**Context:** A few days after Chris first sent the folder. He's following up without being pushy.

> "Been thinking more about that folder Farrice put together. The Yakiya beverage finding keeps sticking with me — 6% attach rate vs 20% industry floor on a $128 tasting. That's real money. Let me know if you want me to set up a quick call with him. Or if not, totally fine — I'll loop back when there's something new."

**Why this works:**
- "Been thinking more about..." is Chris's own reflection, not a nudge about whether they've read it.
- Restating the specific finding creates a re-hook without repeating the whole pitch.
- "That's real money" is how Chris actually talks. Concrete. Operator voice.
- "Or if not, totally fine" is the pressure release that makes this text feel like a friend, not a salesman.

---

## B3 — The Email Version (if formal introduction is needed)

**Subject line:** *Quick intro — AI strategy guy I've been working with*

**Body:**

> Hey,
>
> Wanted to give you a heads-up on something. A friend of mine named Farrice — AI strategy consultant, based in [city] — put together a piece of work on Wasabi, Yakiya, and Panda Inn that I think you should see directly.
>
> Context: I asked him to help me with a simple menu analysis project, the kind of thing I'd normally run myself. He ended up producing a full competitive intelligence and operational assessment across all three concepts. Three findings stood out:
>
> 1. Yakiya's beverage attach rate is running at 6% against a 20%+ Pasadena premium floor — roughly $300K–$700K per year of margin sitting in the dining room based on our own data.
> 2. Panda Inn Glendale sits directly adjacent to the birthplace of the original Panda Express store, and that story doesn't show up anywhere customer-facing. He built out what activating it could look like.
> 3. He noticed Wasabi is on Digital Dining — has a scoped plan for the analytics bridge nobody's ever built for that POS at a single-location level.
>
> The folder is here: [LINK]
>
> I'm not pitching anything — I just wanted to put it in front of you before anything else happens with it. If you want me to set up a conversation with Farrice, I'm happy to make the intro. If not, let me know your thoughts and I'll take it from there.
>
> Chris

**Why this works:**
- Subject line establishes this is a warm introduction, not a sales email.
- "I'm not pitching anything" is the disarmament line. Chris is explicitly disclaiming the sales frame.
- Two specific findings, dollar-attributed, with "based on our own data" for verification.
- "Before anything else happens with it" subtly implies the work has value elsewhere — creating implicit scarcity without saying it.
- Close offers two paths (meet or pass) without preference.

## B4 — The "Casual Mention" Version

**Context:** Chris sees the executive at an informal moment — dinner, event, phone call. He drops a single line.

> "By the way — I've been working with an AI strategy guy on something for the three concepts. He found some stuff I think you'd want to look at. I'll send the folder when we're done catching up."

**Why this works:**
- "By the way" signals low stakes, no agenda.
- "He found some stuff I think you'd want to look at" is the curiosity plant.
- "When we're done catching up" tells the reader this conversation isn't about the folder — Chris respects their time, which makes them more likely to want the folder.

# Shared Elements (Both Variants)

## The single line Chris memorizes

Regardless of variant, if Chris is caught off guard and asked "who is this guy?" — here is the single sentence he delivers:

> "Farrice is an AI strategy guy I trust. He specializes in the kind of operational work that doesn't make it onto a corporate roadmap because the ROI math is locked on higher-priority brands. I asked him for a menu analysis and he came back with the folder you're holding."

**Why this single sentence works:**
- "AI strategy guy I trust" is credibility transfer through relationship.
- "Doesn't make it onto a corporate roadmap because the ROI math is locked on higher-priority brands" is the WS2 unencumberance reframe, baked directly into Chris's mouth. If this sentence is the only thing Chris says about Farrice, it still pre-empts the 1975 objection entirely.
- "I asked him for a menu analysis and he came back with the folder you're holding" makes the deliverable the subject. Farrice becomes the answer to "who built this?" rather than the person being sold.

---

## What Chris should NOT say (anti-script)

These phrases kill the frame. Chris should avoid them in every variant:

- ❌ "He's looking for clients." (Turns Chris into a broker.)
- ❌ "He's really affordable." (Cheapens the work. Price belongs to the folder, not Chris's mouth.)
- ❌ "He could help PRG a lot." (Makes Farrice sound desperate and Chris sound naive.)
- ❌ "This will transform your business." (Transformation language is a consultant tell. PRG has calluses on it.)
- ❌ "You should definitely meet him." (Pressure. The folder generates the meeting; Chris doesn't have to argue for it.)
- ❌ Any version of "trust me" or "I promise." (If Chris has to promise, the folder isn't selling itself.)

---

## What Chris SHOULD say when asked follow-up questions

The three most likely follow-ups and how Chris handles each:

**"What's his pricing?"**
> "There's a line item in the folder — there's a formal $4,500 audit version of what he gave me, and a bigger 90-day engagement he outlined. I'd let him walk you through it directly if you're interested."

**"What's his background?"**
> "He runs an AI consulting practice. His thing is operational AI for restaurants that don't get corporate IT attention — he's specifically NOT a Panda Express guy. I'd rather let him explain it than try to summarize."

**"Have you used this stuff in your store?"**
> "He showed me a manager voice log tool that I've been testing on my phone for two weeks. It's actually useful. I can demo it when we meet."

*(Only use that third response if Chris has actually spent 14 days using the manager voice log demo. If he hasn't, substitute: "He offered to show me one of his tools live — we can set that up if you want to see it work before a bigger conversation.")*

---

## Delivery cadence (what Chris does and when)

Do not forward the folder to leadership on the same day Chris receives it. The sequence:

**Day 0 (receives folder from Farrice):**
Chris opens it, skims it, texts Farrice back: *"Just got this. Going to spend some time with it tonight."*

**Day 1:**
Chris reads the Executive Brief and skims two more documents. He spends 5 minutes with the manager voice log demo. Then he decides whether this is a Variant A or Variant B handoff based on his actual relationship with leadership.

**Day 2–3:**
Chris forwards one of the scripts above. He chooses the casual version for the relationship he has — DM for a Slack-native boss, email for a formal one, text for a personal relationship.

**Day 4–7:**
Chris checks the room. If he gets an acknowledgment ("I'll take a look"), he lets it sit. If he gets nothing, he doesn't follow up — yet.

**Day 7–10:**
If still nothing, Chris sends a single gentle nudge: *"Hey — did you get a chance to look at the folder I sent last week? No pressure, just wanted to check."* One time only. Never twice.

**Day 10+:**
If the room is warm, Chris offers the intro. If the room is cold, Chris lets it go for 30 days and then tries again with a new reason (maybe Farrice ships a second small asset — see follow-up sequence doc 15).

---

## The escape hatch for Chris

If at any point Chris feels awkward forwarding this — if it doesn't match his reading of his relationship with leadership, or if the timing feels wrong — he has one permitted move: *"Sit on it and wait for a natural moment."* That's it. He never feels obligated to push.

The deliverable has a long half-life. A Yakiya beverage finding that's true today will still be true in 60 days. Chris's relationship is more valuable than any single forwarding window.
