# 14 — Objection Prevention Tree

**Audience:** Farrice (internal) + the deliverable itself (pre-empt sentences baked into the Executive Brief, Capability One-Pager, and Pricing page).
**Purpose:** Dissolve the 7 objections most likely to surface at a PRG executive table — before they're spoken. Each objection has a reframe that respects the buyer's identity and a pre-empt sentence the deliverable drops in, so Chris never has to defend the pitch. The document defends itself.
**Core principle:** *"The objection is never what they say. It's what they're protecting underneath. Name the protection, and the objection dissolves."*

---

## How to use this document

Each objection below follows a five-part structure:

1. **The objection in the buyer's voice.** Not the consultant version. The actual sentence a PRG executive says when they're resisting.
2. **What they're really protecting.** The identity layer, the fear, the competing priority underneath.
3. **The reframe.** A specific sentence (or pattern) that acknowledges the underlying protection, then redirects.
4. **The pre-empt sentence.** The exact sentence that goes into the deliverable so the objection is dissolved before it's spoken. This is the most important output of this document.
5. **Where the pre-empt sentence lives.** Which artifact carries the sentence, and roughly where in that artifact.

## Objection 1 — The 1975 Objection (the hardest)

*(This one is non-negotiable. Every artifact touching positioning or pricing must carry this reframe. It's the single most important objection to dissolve because it's aimed directly at Peggy Cherng's lifework and any competent PRG executive will voice some version of it within 60 seconds of opening the folder.)*

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"We've been investing in restaurant technology since 1975. I have a CIO. I have an innovation kitchen. Peggy built our POS analytics when most of the people selling AI today were not born yet. What are you going to tell me that I don't already have on a roadmap somewhere?"*

**What they're really protecting:**
- **Peggy's lifework.** Her PhD work, her 1975 POS analytics, her Caltech board seat — all of it is tangled up in "we are a technology-led restaurant company." Any pitch that implies "let me introduce you to AI" is an identity assault.
- **The CIO's pride.** Someone at PRG is the CIO. They're good. They have a roadmap. They've already been asked "why haven't we done AI yet" by the board.
- **The family pride.** The Cherngs have built a legitimately engineering-literate company. Being talked down to by a generalist consultant is humiliating.

**The reframe:**
Unencumberance, not superiority. Do not claim to know more. Claim to be free of the constraint that keeps the internal team from getting to this work.

**The pre-empt sentence (memorize this — it appears in multiple artifacts):**

> *"You can do this. Your CIO can do this. Peggy built the original system in 1975 that taught every Asian restaurant in America how to run this way. The reason this folder exists isn't that we know more. It's that we're unencumbered by the arithmetic keeping your team locked on 1,650 Panda Express stores. The math is right — Express is where the ROI lives. But the four legacy concepts can't wait 18 months for the roadmap to loop back. This folder is the work your team would do if their priorities weren't already spoken for."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Executive Brief (01):** Second or third paragraph, immediately after the Panda Inn founding myth hook. Before any positioning language. Before any pricing.
- **Capability One-Pager:** Opening paragraph, verbatim or lightly adapted.
- **Chris Handoff Script (13):** Embedded in the "single line Chris memorizes" as: *"He specializes in the kind of operational work that doesn't make it onto a corporate roadmap because the ROI math is locked on higher-priority brands."*
- **Tier 3 pricing page (12):** Directly above the Tier 3 dollar figure as the justification anchor.

**Critical formatting rule:** The sentence that contains "You can do this. Your CIO can do this. Peggy built…" must be the FIRST positioning sentence the reader encounters in the deliverable. If any positioning language appears before this sentence, the reframe fails.

## Objection 2 — "Consultants Are a Waste of Money"

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"I've had consultants before. McKinsey, boutique restaurant guys, the whole circuit. They leave a 60-slide deck, bill six figures, we keep doing exactly what we were doing. Why should I think you're different?"*

**What they're really protecting:**
- Past scar tissue. Every PRG executive has lived through at least one engagement where a polished deck led nowhere.
- Budget anxiety. Consulting budgets at a $5B company are scrutinized precisely because the organization has been burned.
- Identity as a "doer." The operator who's survived decades of consultants views them with a specific kind of operational contempt. They don't hate consultants — they hate *not shipping*.

**The reframe:**
Lead with shipped work. The entire dossier is the proof. The first deliverable in the engagement is something installed, not something presented. Lead with the manager voice log demo — a working tool Chris has already been using — before any conversation about what Tier 2 looks like.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"Nothing in this folder is a slide. Every artifact is a working tool, a measurable finding, or a decision ready to be made. The first thing you see in any engagement is not a presentation. It's a tool running on somebody's phone. If I can't ship something usable in the first two weeks, we shouldn't start."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Executive Brief:** In the "how this works" section, framed as a constraint Farrice has placed on himself, not a promise.
- **Tier 2 pricing page:** Directly under the delivery timeline, as the explanation for why weeks 1–2 ship a deployed voice log system before any "strategy" conversation.
- **Follow-up sequence (15):** Day 3 message includes the phrase "the demo is the proof."

## Objection 3 — "Too Expensive Right Now, Maybe Next Quarter"

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"This is interesting, I'd love to revisit it. But the budget for this quarter is already committed. Let's circle back next quarter and see where we are."*

**What they're really protecting:**
- The polite no. 80% of the time, "next quarter" means never. The buyer is exiting the conversation with grace.
- Prioritization fatigue. Not actually a budget problem — the buyer is exhausted from saying yes to things that didn't work, and "next quarter" is how they preserve the option without committing.
- Real budget cycle reality. At PRG scale, Q2 budgets are locked by mid-Q1. If Farrice shows up in March, there may not be signature-capacity for a new line item until July.

**The reframe:**
Re-anchor the price against found money, not against consulting expense. The Yakiya beverage lever alone pays Tier 2's retainer inside 60 days. This isn't an expense line — it's a revenue unlock with an invoice attached. Budget categories don't apply the same way to found-money projects.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"Tier 2's fee is structured to pay back from Yakiya's beverage attach gap alone, inside the first 60 days of the engagement. That's not a forecast — it's arithmetic against receipts we already have. The question isn't 'can the budget absorb $24,000,' it's 'can we afford to leave $300K+ on the Yakiya dining room floor for another quarter.' If the answer to the second question is yes, the answer to the first question doesn't matter."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Tier 2 pricing page:** As the opening framing above the fee.
- **Executive Brief:** In the call-to-action paragraph, adapted to: *"The math here doesn't sit in a budget category. It sits in the Yakiya dining room right now, in the difference between a 6% beverage attach rate and a 20% one."*

## Objection 4 — "Send Me a Proposal" (the polite brush-off)

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"This is great. Send me a proposal and I'll look it over with my team."*

**What they're really protecting:**
- The graceful exit. 90% of "send me a proposal" is a polite decline. The buyer is offloading the decision to their calendar and expecting the proposal to die in an inbox.
- Their time. They don't want to have the deeper conversation in the moment — they want to control when (and if) it happens next.
- The org chart. "My team" means they want to delegate the engagement to a subordinate who will never advocate for it.

**The reframe (Nepq-style — refuse the brush-off without being rude):**
The deliverable Gigi is holding IS the proposal. It's already pre-built, pre-scoped, pre-costed. There's no proposal to send — the proposal is the folder, and it's already in her hands. The next step isn't a document, it's a decision about which Phase 1 experiment to run.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"The folder you're reading is the proposal. Everything a formal proposal would contain — scope, deliverables, timeline, fee, ROI math — is already on these pages. The only missing element is a decision about which Tier 2 experiment to start with, and we can make that in 30 minutes whenever your calendar has room. Nothing gets emailed to you afterward. The next touch is either a kickoff or a polite pass — both of which I'd rather have in a room than in an email thread."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Capability One-Pager:** In the "what happens next" section, as the explicit anti-proposal frame.
- **Executive Brief:** Final paragraph, adapted.
- **Chris Handoff Script:** Embedded in the follow-up guidance — if leadership responds with "send a proposal," Chris's response is *"He said the folder IS the proposal — do you want me to set up the 30-minute decision call?"*

## Objection 5 — "We Need Proof Before We Commit"

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"Before we commit to an engagement, I'd want to see case studies, references, proof of similar work with a comparable concept. What have you done in this space?"*

**What they're really protecting:**
- Risk aversion. At $5B scale, executives don't buy on vibes. They buy on proof of work.
- Their own reputation. Approving a new vendor with no track record is a career risk, even at low dollar amounts.
- The honest reality that Farrice may not have a case study with a comparable restaurant group yet.

**The reframe:**
The proof is the folder + the working demo, not a case study. A case study is evidence someone did the work for someone else. The folder is evidence Farrice is already doing the work for PRG — he just hasn't been paid for it yet. The manager voice log demo running on Chris's phone is live proof, not historical proof. Live proof is better.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"The proof of capability is the folder you're reading. The beverage finding at Yakiya, the founding-myth analysis at Panda Inn, the Wasabi throughput assessment — these are the exact deliverables of a Tier 2 engagement, produced before the engagement existed. The only thing a case study would add is a brand name on a testimonial page. What the folder already contains is the work itself, applied to your four concepts. The manager voice log demo on Chris's phone is the second piece of proof — a working production tool, deployed and running for 14 days before any contract was signed. If this isn't enough to justify a 30-minute conversation, the case study wouldn't be either."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Executive Brief:** The opening page, adapted into: *"Nothing in this folder is speculative. Every finding is built from receipts, reviews, or live data — the same methodology a Tier 2 engagement uses, applied here as proof of what the engagement would produce."*
- **Capability One-Pager:** Under the "evidence" section instead of a traditional case study section.

## Objection 6 — "Why AI? Why Not Just Hire Better Operators?"

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"The problem isn't technology. The problem is operator quality. If Yakiya had the right GM, the beverage attach rate would fix itself. We should be investing in people, not tools."*

**What they're really protecting:**
- The operator-first worldview. Most restaurant veterans believe technology follows people, and they're usually right. Any "AI is the answer" framing triggers this reflex.
- The unspoken truth that PRG HAS struggled to staff the legacy concepts with A-player GMs, because the A-players are all at Panda Express where the career ladder has 2,600 rungs instead of 4.
- The fear of looking lazy. Choosing a tool over a person can feel like abdication of operator responsibility.

**The reframe:**
AI doesn't replace the operator. It gives the operator back the hours they're drowning in right now. The Yakiya GM isn't failing to train servers on beverage attachment — they're failing because they have 47 other things on their plate and beverage program isn't in the top ten. The AI work surfaces beverage as a testable decision, gives the GM the script, and measures the result. The GM is still the operator. They just stop drowning in the administrative work that keeps them from being one.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"Nothing in this engagement replaces an operator. Every intervention is designed to give Chris and his GMs back the 8–12 hours a week they're currently losing to administrative work — review response, inventory variance triage, daily reporting, menu analysis — so they can spend that time on the part of the job that actually needs a human. The Yakiya beverage program isn't broken because the GM is bad. It's broken because no human being has time to run a two-week A/B test on server scripts while also making service at 6pm. We build the test. The GM runs it."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Executive Brief:** In the "what this isn't" section — Farrice explicitly naming what he's NOT doing as a trust-building move.
- **Tier 2 pricing page:** In the "what the weeks deliver" section, framed as "labor hours returned to the operator" instead of "AI automation."

## Objection 7 — "We Already Do This In-House"

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"We have a corporate data team. We have FP&A. We have Andrea's digital business group. Anything you're proposing to build, we can build internally."*

**What they're really protecting:**
- Loyalty to the internal team. Gigi isn't going to throw her own people under the bus.
- The sunk cost of internal capacity. PRG has spent years building these functions. Outsourcing feels like an admission they're insufficient.
- A real question about whether external capacity is redundant.

**The reframe:**
This is the WS2 reframe, sharpened. The internal team is not in-house for the legacy concepts — they're in-house for Panda Express, because that's where the scale ROI is. Not a critique of the team. A fact about where their hours go. The legacy concepts need capacity that's structurally uninterested in competing with Express priorities, which is only possible from outside the org chart.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"Your internal team is world-class. They can do almost any of the work in this folder. The honest test is the specific case: can they justify the engineering hours to build an ODBC bridge to Digital Dining at Wasabi this fiscal year? The arithmetic says no — one location running a legacy POS that no cloud vendor has a plug-and-play connector for, against a roadmap built around 1,650 Panda Express stores already on modern infrastructure. That's not a critique of your team. That's the math. The legacy concepts aren't in-house work because there isn't enough in-house capacity left over after Express gets its analyst hours. This isn't a gap in your team — it's a gap in the org chart that nobody at PRG was ever funded to fill. Tier 3 exists to fill it without creating a headcount request that would have to compete with Express priorities in next year's budget cycle."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Executive Brief:** In the "what this engagement is" section, as the differentiation from internal capacity.
- **Tier 3 pricing page:** Above the annual fee as the justification for "why external."
- **Chris Handoff Script:** Chris's memorized line already contains a version of this: *"doesn't make it onto a corporate roadmap because the ROI math is locked on higher-priority brands."*

## Bonus Objection — "Have You Spoken With Andrea's Team?"

*(The PRG-specific one WS2 flagged. Andrea Cherng as CBO will gatekeep anything touching brand or marketing. If her name comes up and Farrice hasn't pre-empted it, the deliverable gets routed to her team and slow-dies in a review cycle.)*

**The objection in the buyer's voice:**
> *"My daughter Andrea runs brand strategy for the legacy concepts. Have you spoken with her team? Anything touching brand expression goes through her."*

**What they're really protecting:**
- Family org chart reality. Andrea is the Cherng family's hand-picked next-generation steward for brand. Going around her is a political error.
- The integrity of the brand voice across concepts. Andrea lives the tension between concept differentiation and family stewardship — she doesn't want an outside consultant freestyling on it.

**The reframe:**
Position the work as explicitly upstream of brand. The engagement looks at operational decisions — pricing, menu mix, throughput, review response — and flags anything that touches brand expression for Andrea's team to decide on. Farrice is not a brand strategist and explicitly doesn't want to be one.

**The pre-empt sentence:**

> *"Nothing in this engagement modifies brand expression. Every finding is operational — pricing, menu mix, throughput, review response patterns, beverage program mechanics. Anything that touches the way a concept speaks to its customers gets flagged for Andrea Cherng's team to decide on. This work is designed to be a feeder into the CBO function, not a parallel track to it."*

**Where the pre-empt sentence lives:**
- **Executive Brief:** In the "what this isn't" section, as the brand boundary declaration.
- **Capability One-Pager:** Under scope exclusions.

## One Meta-Reframe

Across all 8 objections above, there is a single underlying pattern Farrice must internalize:

**Every objection a PRG executive raises is protecting an identity, not a decision.**

- Objection 1 protects Peggy's lifework.
- Objection 2 protects "I'm not the kind of person who gets fooled by consultants."
- Objection 3 protects "I'm a disciplined budget manager."
- Objection 4 protects "I control my calendar."
- Objection 5 protects "I don't approve vendors on vibes."
- Objection 6 protects "I believe in people over tools."
- Objection 7 protects "My team is good."
- Bonus protects "Family comes first in my org chart."

Every reframe above respects the underlying identity and gives the buyer a way to keep that identity intact while saying yes. **A buyer who has to abandon their identity to say yes will always say no.** The whole job of this document is to make yes compatible with their self-image.

Memorize that sentence.
