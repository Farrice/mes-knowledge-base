# The Founding Myth Brief
### Activating an asset Panda Inn Glendale already owns

**Subject:** 201 N Maryland Avenue, Glendale CA. And what happened one block away in October 1983
**Status:** A two-page brief on a story the restaurant has never told its own customers
**Cost to activate:** Under $500 across all five recommendations
**Timeline:** 30 days

---

## The fact

In 1982, Andrew Cherng opened the second Panda Inn in Glendale at 201 North Maryland Avenue. One year later, October 1983. The developer of Glendale Galleria II walked into the restaurant, ordered a meal, and asked Andrew if he could build a faster, takeout version of Panda Inn for the new food court he was building two blocks away. **That food court stand opened in October 1983 and became the first Panda Express.**

Forty-three years and 1,650 stores later, the restaurant where the conversation happened. The actual Glendale dining room where the developer ate Panda Inn cooking and decided the world needed a faster version. Is the same restaurant Chris's Saturday-night regulars walk into every week. **Customers don't know.** The plaque isn't on the wall. The line isn't on the menu. The Google Business Profile says "established 1982" without context. The website tells the corporate Panda Restaurant Group story but doesn't tell the room's story. The story isn't being suppressed. It's just not being said.

This brief is about saying it.

---

## Why it matters

Most restaurants would invent this story if they didn't have it. A founding-myth narrative for the world's largest Asian restaurant chain, set in an actual physical room you can sit in tonight, with the original family still operating the company, is the kind of brand asset that media companies, food writers, and tourism bureaus pay marketing budget to manufacture. Panda Inn Glendale doesn't have to manufacture it. It happened. It is a fact. It is at this address. It is documented in Wikipedia, in ABC7's "Original Panda Inn Reopens in Pasadena" piece, and in the Cherng family's own public statements about how the company began.

The story is also doing different work than a standard marketing claim does. It is doing **emotional work**. The reviews of Panda Inn Glendale already include several customers who frame their visit as a pilgrimage:

> *"We flew in from Tampa, Florida, for a wedding... Both being originally from California and huge fans of Panda Express, it felt like an honor to finally experience the Panda Inn."*

> *"Original panda express restaurant. Up side down noodle is a very interesting surprise."*

> *"We have enjoyed dining at Glendale Panda Inn for **decades** (save for those years during the remodel)."*

> *"Been coming here since the **late 90s** when the restaurant was in that small corner with a big parking lot."*

These are not marketing-induced reviews. These are organic. The pilgrim segment exists. The 50-year regular segment exists. They're the customers who have already done the cultural work of recognizing what the restaurant means. **Activation is about letting the next customer in on the story before they walk out.**

## Five activations, all zero or near-zero cost

### Activation 1 — A table card and menu insert

The simplest move. A small printed card, 4×6 inches, slipped onto the table or printed on the back of the dinner menu.

**Headline:** *Before there was Panda Express, there was Panda Inn.*

**Body (suggested copy):**
> Our flagship dining room has been at 201 North Maryland Avenue since 1982. One year after we opened, the developer of Glendale Galleria II ate here, loved the food, and asked our founder Andrew Cherng if he could build a faster version of Panda Inn for his new food court. The first Panda Express opened a half-mile from this room in October 1983. Today, more than 1,650 Panda Express stores serve guests across the world. They all started with a meal in this dining room. Welcome back.

**Cost:** ~$80 for a print run of 500 cards. ~$200 if printed on the back of new menus.
**Effort:** One afternoon. One vendor call.
**Lead time:** 1 week.

### Activation 2 — A wall plaque or framed photograph near the host stand

A single physical object that gives the room a heritage marker.

The plaque should be small, restrained, the kind of thing you see at historic restaurants in San Francisco and Boston. An understated reminder, not a billboard. A bronze or brushed-metal plate with the heading "Where Panda Began, 1982" and a single paragraph of text describing the founding. Mounted at eye level near the host stand or in the entry vestibule.

If a plaque feels too formal, a framed photograph from 1982 (the first day of opening, a photo of the original Galleria II food-court Panda Express, or a portrait of Andrew and Master Chef Ming-Tsai Cherng) can do the same emotional work.

**Cost:** $150–$400 depending on plaque material and finish.
**Effort:** One vendor (any local sign or trophy shop), one mounting.
**Lead time:** 2–3 weeks.

### Activation 3 — The Google Business Profile update

The Google Business Profile is currently the first impression for any customer searching "Panda Inn Glendale." The "From the business" section is short and corporate. This is the lowest-cost, highest-distribution single change in the entire activation list.

**Suggested update for "From the business" section:**
> Panda Inn was founded in 1973 in Pasadena by Andrew Cherng and his father, Master Chef Ming-Tsai Cherng. We opened our Glendale dining room in 1982. One year later, the developer of Glendale Galleria II asked us to create a faster version of our menu for his new food court. That became the first Panda Express. The Glendale Panda Inn is the original sit-down dining room that inspired what is now the world's largest Asian restaurant chain. Visit us for the heritage Mandarin and Sichuan dishes that started it all.

**Cost:** $0.
**Effort:** One Google Business Profile login. Thirty minutes to write and proofread.
**Lead time:** Immediate. Updates are live within hours.

### Activation 4 — The host-stand training script

The hostess (or any front-of-house team member) needs a single, memorized paragraph for the moment a guest asks "how long has this place been here?" or "what's the history?" The story should be told the same way every time, by every team member, with the same facts and the same cadence.

**Suggested script:**
> Actually, this dining room is where Panda Express started. The developer of the Glendale Galleria across the street ate at our Panda Inn in 1982 and asked Andrew Cherng if he could build a faster version of our menu for his new food court. The first Panda Express opened a year later, half a mile from where you're sitting. Today there are more than sixteen hundred Panda Express stores around the world. This is the room where the conversation happened.

**Cost:** One staff meeting. Maybe two. One to introduce the script, one to make sure it's stuck.
**Effort:** Thirty minutes of management time. Fifteen minutes of staff time.
**Lead time:** 1 week.

The script works because it doesn't sell anything. It tells a true story in three sentences and lets the guest do the emotional work of caring. The hostess isn't pitching. The guest is pulling.

### Activation 5 — Two menu items marked "since 1973"

A single visual treatment on the menu — a small star, a small note, a tiny calligraphic mark — next to two items that were on the original 1973 Panda Inn menu. Hot & Sour Soup is the obvious first choice (the most-mentioned item in reviews; the dish that defines a visit). One classic Mandarin entrée — Mongolian Beef, Kon Pao Chicken, or Mapo Tofu — is the second.

The note next to each item: *"On our menu since 1973."*

**Cost:** One menu reprint. ~$100–$300 depending on quantity.
**Effort:** One menu redesign, half a day for a layout person.
**Lead time:** 2 weeks.

This is the activation that converts the founding myth from a wall fact into an ordering fact. A guest who is choosing between Hot & Sour Soup and Egg Drop Soup, who sees the small "since 1973" mark next to the Hot & Sour Soup, will order the Hot & Sour Soup. Not because the marketing told them to. Because the dish is now the story they get to participate in.

## What none of this is

This brief is not a marketing proposal. It is not an attempt to "tell the brand story better" in the abstract sense that consultants use that phrase. It is a list of five specific physical and digital changes that activate a fact the restaurant already owns. The fact is true. The fact is documented in the public record. The fact is meaningful to the customer base in ways the customer base has already demonstrated unprompted in their reviews. **Activation is the work of telling people what is already true.**

It is also not, and this matters, a project that requires the corporate marketing team's permission to start. The Google Business Profile update can happen tonight. The host-stand script can be introduced at the next staff meeting. The table cards can be printed by Friday. The plaque takes longer because of vendor lead time. The menu reprint takes longer because of layout cycles. None of it touches the food. None of it changes the kitchen. None of it requires approval from anyone outside the four walls of the building, except the GBP update and the menu reprint, which are routine operational decisions.

## The press pitch (a sixth activation that earns its own paragraph)

If any of the above sticks, the next move is a single press pitch. One email, one LA food writer, one story:

> **Subject:** *The room where Panda Express was conceived is still serving Hot & Sour Soup at 201 N Maryland Ave. Most of its customers don't know.*

The body of the email writes itself. Local angle (Glendale). Heritage angle (the family is still running the company). Human-interest angle (the developer-meets-restaurateur conversation that built a $5B chain). Visual angle (the original 1982 dining room). The article hooks itself.

The pitch should go to **Eater LA, Pasadena Now, LA Times Food, LA Magazine, and the Glendale News-Press**, in roughly that order of priority. Andrea Cherng's CBO function at PRG corporate has the relationships to make the pitch land. This is the kind of asset she would naturally activate as a Chief Brand Officer if it crossed her desk. The pitch costs nothing. The press placement costs nothing. The compounding effect on Google Business reviews (a tier-1 LA food publication article generates 30–80 organic Google reviews on the article wave alone) is significant.

**Estimated visible result if even one of those publications picks it up:** 50–150 new Google reviews within 60 days, weighted toward 5-star, weighted toward customers who came in specifically because of the heritage story. Rating goes from 4.4 to 4.5 or 4.6. Review velocity doubles. The restaurant becomes a Google search result for "historic Chinese restaurant Los Angeles" and "where did Panda Express start". Search terms that currently have no winner in Google's eyes.

## What the founding myth has to do with the menu intelligence

The menu intelligence files in this folder are about pricing, items, and competitive position. This brief is about story. It belongs in the same folder because at a heritage restaurant, the menu and the story are the same product. Customers don't come back to Panda Inn Glendale because the Mongolian Beef is the best Mongolian Beef in the city. They come back because the Mongolian Beef is *theirs*. The 50-year regulars know this. The pilgrims know this. The new customer doesn't yet. But they could, and the cost of telling them is a printing run and a Google login.

The most efficient menu in the world is a menu that tells a true story the customer wants to be part of. **Panda Inn Glendale has the story. It is sitting on the table, uncashed.**
