# Two-Question Empathy Ladder (`/hughes-empathy-ladder`)

You are operating from Chase Hughes's claim that you can decode any human in two questions, no therapy degree required. The technique replaces emotional empathy (hard) with procedural empathy (easy) — and produces deeper recognition than either alone.

## When This Fires

Run this workflow whenever:
- Doing audience research where surface demographics aren't producing recognition language
- Constructing an ICP / consumer posture / persona — Hughes's ladder is what makes a profile feel *seen* rather than *described*
- Approaching a difficult conversation where surface judgment ("they're annoying / arrogant / desperate") is blocking your ability to connect
- Diagnosing what a hostile audience or customer is actually defending
- Writing copy that needs to land on a specific fear, not a generic pain point
- Parenting / mentoring conversations about reading other people

## Skill Acquisition

Load `genius.md` Pattern 1 (Two-Question Empathy Ladder) and the Hall of Fame Exemplar 1 (Bumper Sticker Empathy Drill) before producing.

## Execution

### Step 1 — Capture the Signal

Inventory the specific signals the subject emits. Be observational, not interpretive yet.
- Clothing / accessories / aesthetic
- Bio / profile language / titles they claim
- Content they post / share / engage with
- Behaviors observed in conversation
- What they over-explain
- What they refuse to discuss
- Communities they signal membership in

The more specific and concrete the signals, the more accurate the decode. Generic signals produce generic fears.

### Step 2 — Question 1: What Do They Want Me to Think About Them?

For each signal (or the dominant signal cluster), answer the literal question.

**Discipline**: this is *want*, not *need*. Not "they need attention" — that's interpretation. *They want you to think they're successful.* *They want you to think they're hard to impress.* *They want you to think they figured it out alone.*

The answer is usually a string of identity claims:
> "They want me to think they are: [trait 1], [trait 2], [trait 3]..."

These are **the identity surface**.

### Step 3 — Question 2: What Would They Be Afraid Of If That Were True?

Take each identity claim from Step 2 and run the inverse:
> "If [they want me to think X] is true... what does X protect them from?"

The answer is a fear. Not a vague fear — a *specific* fear with concrete content.

Worked examples:
- Wants me to think: "I am self-sufficient." → Fears: needing help and being told no. Fears asking and being ignored.
- Wants me to think: "I'm not impressed by your fancy thing." → Fears: being seen as someone who *was* impressed, who tried, who wanted in.
- Wants me to think: "I have many friends and active communities." → Fears: ending the day alone, having no one to call when something good happens.
- Wants me to think: "I'm an expert and authority." → Fears: being caught not knowing, being asked something basic in front of someone they respect.
- Wants me to think: "I don't care what people think." → Fears: caring deeply and having that care be unreciprocated.

### Step 4 — Synthesize the Fear Hypothesis

Write a single-sentence fear hypothesis that names the fear specifically:
> "[Subject] is most afraid of [specific scenario], which is why they signal [identity claim]."

This sentence is the operational decode. Everything downstream — copy, conversation, persona, ICP — runs off it.

### Step 5 — Generate Three Outputs from the Fear

**Output A — Recognition Language**: One sentence that, if said in their presence, would produce the small flinch of being seen. Not a sales line. Not flattery. A line that names the fear without judging it.
> "Most of the people who do what you do are exhausted from being the one everyone else relies on, and the irony is that admitting that is the one thing the role doesn't allow."

**Output B — The Trap**: One thing you might naively say or write that would activate their defense. The signal you should NOT echo.
> "If you tell them they're 'high-achieving,' they will close. The signal they emit is 'I am self-sufficient' — but the fear is being treated as a peer. Calling them high-achieving treats them as a tier, which feels like social processing, which threatens the fear."

**Output C — The Bridge**: The conversational/copy move that moves toward the fear without naming it.
> "Don't ask them what they need help with. Ask them what they used to be good at that they don't have time for anymore."

### Step 6 — Quality Check

- [ ] The fear hypothesis is specific enough to be wrong (not "they fear failure" but "they fear sending the email and getting no reply")
- [ ] The recognition language doesn't flatter and doesn't pity
- [ ] The trap is something you would have written by default if you hadn't run this exercise
- [ ] The bridge is a concrete move — copy, question, gesture — not a feeling

## Example Output

**Context**: Audience research for a coaching offer targeting mid-career corporate executives (VP+) who are quietly considering leaving for solo work.

**SIGNAL INVENTORY**:
- LinkedIn posts about "intentional leadership" and "high-performance teams"
- Reads books like *The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F\*ck* and *Atomic Habits*
- Wears unbranded but expensive minimalist clothing (Lululemon ABC pants, plain merino sweaters)
- Refuses to talk about their compensation
- Frequently mentions their direct reports in conversation as a way of locating themselves

**QUESTION 1 — What do they want me to think about them?**:
- They want me to think: I am calmly in control of a complex operation
- They want me to think: I have evolved past chasing status (the unbranded clothing IS the status)
- They want me to think: I'm thoughtful, well-read, intentional — not corporate-by-default

**QUESTION 2 — What would they fear if that were true?**:
- "Calmly in control" protects against: the fear that everyone reporting to them would be fine without them, that the role is the thing that's complex, not them
- "Evolved past chasing status" protects against: the fear that they ARE still chasing status, just at a higher tier where the status moves are more sophisticated
- "Thoughtful, well-read, intentional" protects against: the fear that the role has made them generic — that another VP could be swapped in tomorrow and no one would notice

**FEAR HYPOTHESIS**:
*This audience is most afraid of being structurally replaceable — of discovering that the elaborate edifice of their role would absorb a substitute without disturbance — which is why they signal "calmly in control of a complex operation."*

**OUTPUT A — Recognition Language**:
> "The hardest part isn't the workload. It's noticing that the operation runs whether or not you're the one running it."

**OUTPUT B — The Trap**:
> Don't write copy that calls them "high-performing executives" or "leaders making moves." That language treats them as a category, which mirrors the exact fear — that they ARE a category, swappable. It will produce a quiet click-away with no rebuttal.

**OUTPUT C — The Bridge**:
> The conversational opening: "What's the smallest part of your week that you'd take with you if you left?" — surfaces the irreplaceable craft underneath the role, which is what they're actually trying to protect when they signal control.

**What elevates this**: The fear isn't "burnout" (generic) or "midlife crisis" (lazy). It's structural replaceability. That fear has different copy, different offers, different recognition language — and it's the actual fear most of this audience cannot say out loud.
