# Resonance: Godin Tribal Frameworks

*Positioning strategy for the room, not the buyer — Seth Godin tribal frameworks applied to Resonance, Chicago. Use when shaping audience definition, status dynamics, change tension, and the non-obvious selectivity insight.*

---

## Minimum Viable Audience

The Resonance attendee is the Chicago single in their 30s who has quietly, without bitterness, concluded that dating apps and 11pm bars produce a kind of encounter they no longer want. Their life is mostly together — career, body, some self-awareness, maybe a therapist — and they've finally recognized that their own guardedness, not "the dating pool," is what's in the way. They know music is how they feel things they can't say out loud, and they're willing to look awkward in a room if the room has taste. They've been to enough generic singles events to know the cringe by smell, and they want a room that doesn't make them apologize for wanting a partner. They're not seeking healing, expansion, or conscious connection as the product — they're seeking another real human being, and they'll trade an afternoon of discomfort for a real shot at one.

**Andrea's gut-check:** *"If this doesn't land for someone who fits that description, it's wrong."*

## Tribal Worldview Lines

*Test: Would a real person say this to a friend? If not, it fails.*

### Marcus's Version

> "First room I ever walked into where I didn't have to be 'on.' I just moved. The music handled the rest."

*Why this works:* Marcus spends his life translating himself — for work, for white spaces, for dating. "Didn't have to be 'on'" is the highest compliment he can give a room. "I just moved" is a body-level report, not a feeling-statement. "The music handled the rest" credits the structure, not the organizer — which is how Marcus gives praise.

### Daniel's Version

> "Bro I was SO ready to make fun of it. Danced sober for three hours and met someone I actually want to see again. I don't have words, man."

*Why this works:* Daniel's identity is built on skepticism. Leading with "SO ready to make fun of it" is non-negotiable — his friends wouldn't believe him otherwise. "Danced sober for three hours" is the specific, unbelievable fact that makes the rest credible. "I don't have words, man" is Daniel's admission that something broke through his defenses, which is the only endorsement his friends will trust.

### Woman's Version

> "Finally — a thing for singles that doesn't feel like husband-hunting or wellness cosplay. Good music, actual daylight, men who were present. I felt like a person, not a target."

*Why this works:* She's been to the bad rooms. "Husband-hunting" and "wellness cosplay" are categories she uses to dismiss them. "Men who were present" is a meaningful distinction from every other singles event. "I felt like a person, not a target" captures the dual risk of those rooms — being hunted or being a shopper — and says this one solved both.

### Unified Version

> "People like us do things like this — we show up sober on a Saturday, let the music do the talking, and trust the floor more than the apps. Because leading with our heads got us everything except what we came for."

*Why this works:* It's specific enough to mean something (sober, Saturday, music, floor, apps) and true enough that all three versions of the attendee would nod if they heard someone else say it. "Leading with our heads got us everything except what we came for" is the shared insight — it names the cage without wellness language. "People like us" creates tribal boundaries: if you don't know what we mean, you're not us yet.

## Status Map

### For the Man

**What status is he TERRIFIED of losing:**
The status of self-sufficiency. His identity is built on being the guy who "has his shit together" — good job, good body, competent in social situations. Walking into a curated singles event threatens all of that because it says, "I can't do this part on my own." He's terrified of becoming "that guy" — the guy at the singles event. He can't BJJ his way through this room, and that terrifies the part of him that's built a life on being able to figure things out.

Specifically for Marcus: he's terrified of losing the status of the grounded, watchful man. Dancing feels like performing, and performing feels like losing the groundedness he's fought to cultivate as a Black man in white spaces.

Specifically for Daniel: he's terrified of encountering the one domain where his competence stack doesn't transfer. He's good at almost everything he tries. This is the exception, and exceptions feel like exposure.

**What status does he QUIETLY GAIN:**
The status of "he was there" — the courage badge of entering a room most men won't. Not alpha courage. Not performative courage. The courage of being seen trying at something you're not good at. That's a different, rarer status.

He also gains the status of being vetted by Andrea — implicit endorsement that he's serious, not creepy, not desperate. And the status of being the kind of man this room attracts: present, willing to be awkward, done with performance.

**What "looking foolish" means to him in this room:**
Standing at the edges with arms crossed, making the room carry his discomfort. Trying to "work the room" during the music — talking when everyone else is moving. Overcompensating with performative dance moves. The man who came to observe and judge, not to participate. The worst version: the guy who treats it like a nightclub without alcohol, scanning for targets.

**What "looking like someone who belongs" looks like:**
Letting the music move him however it moves him — skill is irrelevant. Noticing the music more than he's noticing who's watching. Dancing through his awkwardness instead of performing past it. The man who looks like he came for the music and stayed for the connection.

### For the Woman

**What status is she risking by being in a "singles event":**
The core risk: being seen as a woman who "has to" go to a singles event. If she were really desirable, wouldn't she meet someone naturally? That's the script she's fighting. She's also risking cringe-by-association — if the room is poorly executed, she's embarrassing by proximity. And the risk she knows from experience: predatory or desperate male energy that makes her feel like prey.

For professional women: the specific risk of being recognized by colleagues at a "singles event."

**What status does she gain by being in THIS room vs. any other singles event:**
The status of discernment: "Of all the ways to meet someone in Chicago, I found the one with taste." The status of being vetted and invited — "Andrea invited me" is a different statement than "I went to a singles event." The status of being in a room that doesn't read as "singles event" at all — she can describe it as "this music thing my friend told me about" if she wants.

Most importantly: the status of being around men who self-selected for courage and seriousness. She didn't have to wade through a bar full of randos or a dating app full of tourists. The room pre-filtered for her.

**What she needs to see in the men to feel this room is worth her time:**
She needs to see men who are present in their bodies, not scanning the room for targets. Men who moved through awkwardness authentically — not men who walked in confident, but men who became present by going through the discomfort. Men who seem like they'd be interesting humans regardless of whether she's single (the "would I want to know this person anyway?" test). Men who came for the music AND for connection — not just one or the other. Most critically: men who look like they showed up for themselves, not just to meet women. The man who dances like nobody's watching — because in this room, nobody is.

## The Tension of Change

The healthy tension is the gap between "I'm fine" and "fine isn't enough." It's not the fear of dying alone — that's manufactured urgency, and these people can smell it. It's the quieter, more specific fear of becoming the man who "has it all together" except for the one thing that makes it all matter.

**The cost of another year on the apps:** Not loneliness — numbness. Becoming so fluent in the Hinge script that genuine connection starts to feel like a foreign language. The 11:38pm bourbon scroll isn't a ritual, it's a resignation. And resignation is a kind of small death — the death of the part of him that still believes this matters.

**The cost of NOT learning to be present in a room:** He becomes the man who substitutes competence for connection. Who has a great career and an empty side of the bed and tells himself that's just being a grownup. Who gets really good at being almost happy.

**What he knows deep down that makes "not going" feel like a small death:** He knows that the apps aren't broken — the apps are doing exactly what they were designed to do. He knows that another year of "fine" means another year of the same conversations, the same almost-dates, the same 11:38pm resignation. He knows that the part of him that still wants something real is getting quieter, and he's not sure how many more years he can pretend that's okay.

**What makes "going" feel like a step toward the person he wants to become:** It's the first room that doesn't ask him to be someone he's not. It doesn't ask him to be extroverted, or a nightlife person, or "good at dating." It just asks him to show up and move. The music does the rest. The room protects him from the worst-case scenario (phones in a basket, no alcohol, Andrea at the door, the floor as protocol) while still requiring him to do the one thing that matters: be seen.

**In one line:** The tension isn't between going and being alone. It's between going and staying fine — and he's no longer sure which one scares him more.

## The Non-Obvious Insight

The amateur positioning strategist looks at this and says: *"Men are afraid to dance because they'll look foolish. Make the dancing feel safe — offer lessons, make it optional, have a social hour first."*

The Godin-trained thinker sees something entirely different:

**The dance floor is not a performance space. It's a filter.** And the filter doesn't test for dance skill. It tests for willingness to be seen trying.

The woman in this room isn't evaluating his dance moves. She's evaluating whether he's the kind of man who would enter a room where dance skills don't matter. The man who stands at the edge for 20 minutes, visibly uncomfortable, and then finally moves — that breakthrough moment IS the signal. It says more about his character than any Hinge prompt ever could.

The status gain isn't "I'm a good dancer." It's: **"I'm the kind of man who goes to rooms where my competence doesn't protect me, because the thing on the other side matters more than my ego."**

And the deeper layer the amateur misses: **You don't fix the gender ratio by marketing harder to women.** You fix it by making the room so right for the right men that the right women feel safe. A woman doesn't want "more men." She wants "the right kind of men" — men who passed the filter. Even 12 of those men is better than 40 of the wrong kind. The room's selectivity IS the signal that makes women say yes. Scarcity of the right thing is a feature, not a bug.
