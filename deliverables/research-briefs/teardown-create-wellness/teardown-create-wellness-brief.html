the only NSF certified creatine gummy in the world, in an accordion

Create Wellness holds the one receipt their entire category is accused of faking — an outside lab measured 4.59g against a 4.5g label while two competitors measured zero. Their homepage is running a summer sale. Public-surface teardown, every claim sourced, sent to the brand before publication.

01

the gap in one line

THE VERDICT

The most valuable sentence about this company was written by somebody else, in a lab report, and it is nowhere on their site.

An independent test put six creatine gummies through a lab. Four failed. Two had no detectable creatine at all. Create came back at 4.59 grams against a 4.5 gram label — over the label, in the category currently being accused of selling candy. Their quality page publishes impurity caps, heavy-metal limits, batch-level NSF Certified for Sport, and 18-month stability retests. Nobody publishes numbers like that unless they hold, because publishing them is how you get caught.

Then the homepage says "Creatine for Everybody" over "Save Big and Have the Hottest Summer Ever," and the category-of-one claim sits in a collapsed FAQ underneath a question about whether kids can take them.

✓

Science page, MEMORY chart — "Memory Improvement With Creatine" plotting adults 11–31 vs 66–76 — footnoted to Volek et al., "Performance and muscle fiber adaptations to creatine supplementation and heavy resistance training."

GAP — that footnote is a bench-press and muscle-fiber study sitting under a memory chart. The same citation also appears under the strength chart, where it belongs. On the one page carrying two real creatine researchers' names, the memory claim is sourced to a lifting paper. Cheapest fix in the teardown.

VERIFIED/pages/the-science · read directly ↗
✓

Quality page: creatinine ≤100 ppm, DCD ≤50 ppm, DHT ≤3 ppm, heavy metals ≤0.1 ppm, "100% of Create's purity and potency testing is third-party," "every batch is NSF Certified for Sport®," product retested at 18 months still meeting label.

PROVEN, and it is the asset. Creatine degrades to creatinine in water and heat — exactly what making a gummy involves — which is why most of the shelf fails. They also didn't round up: three gummies is 4.5g and they printed 4.5g.

VERIFIED/pages/our-quality-promise ↗
✓

Flavor in four of five, convenience in three. Not one gram, not one lab, not one test. Their own auto-generated summary agrees: "delicious flavors and highly convenient, travel-friendly packaging." One curated testimonial higher on the page says the missing headline out loud — "something this delicious is NSF certified and backed by real science."

THE OPENING

three angles I'd ship

Ranked. Angle 1 is the only one a competitor cannot copy this quarter.

01

Put the number in the hero.

Replace the seasonal sale line with the test result and its source. Four of six gummies failed; ours measured 4.59 against a 4.5 label. Copying this means paying for testing that might not go their way — and the category's failure is still in the news.

02

Take the FAQ answer out of the FAQ.

"The only NSF Certified for Sport creatine gummy in the world" is a category-of-one claim buried under a question about children. It belongs on the pack, the PDP above the fold, the Target shelf-talker, and line one of every retail sell sheet.

03

Fix the memory footnote before somebody screenshots it.

Then use the repair as content — Smith-Ryan or Candow on what creatine and cognition research actually shows, real citations under real charts. The brand caught being careful about its own footnotes owns a position nobody is competing for.

CAVEATS WORTH KEEPING

what this isn't

Public surface only. No access to internal strategy, and there may be reasons for all of it not visible from outside — the published post says so in its first three lines.

The hero being critiqued is a seasonal sale banner, named as a promo rather than treated as permanent positioning. The 100K review count is plausibly an all-channel total, said before the critique. The publication date of the third-party lab test is UNCONFIRMED and no date is asserted anywhere in the piece. A second round of testing commissioned by NOW Foods reported similar failures but its source returned 403, so it was deliberately excluded from the published version.

Not claimed anywhere: that their marketing caused any business outcome, anything about internal strategy, or that the electrolyte mix is or isn't NSF certified — the FAQ names the gummies only.

The founder's best rebuttal, answered in the piece rather than argued at them: "NSF Certified for Sport means nothing to a mom in a Target aisle." Real, and half right — the gummy failures reached mainstream health press, so "does this contain creatine" is now the mass buyer's question, not the lab nerd's.
