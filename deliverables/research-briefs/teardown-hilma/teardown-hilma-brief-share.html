the gap in one line

THE VERDICT

They ran the study nobody else runs, then wrote a headline their own footnote contradicts.

The category standard is to find research on an ingredient, buy that ingredient, and let the research follow it onto your label. Hilma took a finished product and put it in front of 101 people. Then they printed the part that makes it weaker — "Single arm observational study with 101 completed participants" — in small type, but they printed it. In a category where "clinically studied" usually means somebody read an abstract, that footnote is more honest than the entire shelf around it.

Directly above it, the heading says "Clinically Proven To Work Quickly." Single-arm means no control group. Nobody got a placebo, so nothing separates the formula from thirty minutes passing. It is a claim about causation resting on a design that cannot establish it — and their own footnote already says so, three lines lower.

THE OPENING

three angles I'd ship

Ranked. Angle 1 replaces a weaker claim with a stronger one for free.

01

Publish the study design as the pitch.

Retire "Clinically Proven." Run: we tested this product, not an ingredient, on 101 people; 94% felt better in 30 minutes; there was no placebo group, so this is what we measured, not what we proved; here's what we're running next. Stronger than the claim it replaces, and no competitor copies it because copying means admitting they never tested anything.

02

Move the moat to the homepage.

"Debloat + Digest" is the category's sentence. "We test our finished formulas on people and publish what we find, including the parts that make the numbers smaller" is theirs. Hero, PDP, packaging, retail sell sheet.

03

Turn the advisory board into arguments.

Six named doctors are currently six headshots. Dr. Close on why observational studies still matter when the alternative is nothing. Dr. Romm on where herbal evidence is genuinely strong and where it isn't. The brand that publishes its own limits owns the trust position nobody else will risk.

There's a footnote on Hilma's heartburn page that most brands in their category would have quietly deleted.

"Single arm observational study with 101 completed participants."

For eighteen years my job included being the last person somebody asked before they bought something. You learn fast that "does this work" and "how do you know" are two different questions, and only one of them has a good answer most of the time.

I'm only reading the public surface here. No access to Hilma's internal strategy, and there may be reasons for all of it I can't see from out here.

Hilma ran a study on the product they actually sell. That is rare. The normal move in supplements is to find research on an ingredient, buy the ingredient, and let somebody else's study ride along on your label. They tested the finished formula, on people, and published the number: 94% reported less upset stomach in 30 minutes.

Then they published the part that makes it weaker. Single arm. No placebo group. Nobody got a sugar pill, so nothing in the study separates the formula from time passing.

They printed that themselves. In small type, but they printed it.

Above it, the heading says "Clinically Proven To Work Quickly."

So the headline and the footnote are having an argument on the same screen, and the footnote is winning.

The fix costs nothing. "We tested this product on 101 people. 94% felt better in half an hour. There was no placebo group, so this is what we measured, not what we proved. Here's the study we're running next."

That paragraph is more convincing than the claim it replaces. It's also the one thing nobody in that category can copy, because copying it means admitting they never tested anything.

I see this outside supplements constantly. A team does the expensive, honest work, then describes it in the borrowed language of people who didn't. The strong version always sounds smaller than the puffed one. It just happens to survive contact with a skeptic.

The move, if you have real evidence: write the sentence that includes your own limitation. Then read it next to your competitor's claim and notice which one you'd believe.

Somebody at Hilma fought to run that study. The footnote is the proof they won.
