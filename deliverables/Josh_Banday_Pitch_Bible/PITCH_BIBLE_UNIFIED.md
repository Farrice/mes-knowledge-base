## ARTIST STATEMENT

Josh Banday is not an emerging writer hoping to break in. He is a **set-tested actor-auteur** — a series regular (*Upload*, *Not Dead Yet*) with Second City and Groundlings training, writing from a voice no one else in the room has: biracial, Black, Filipino, Mexican, Jewish, and Queer, raised in South Central LA.

He has 5 seasons of series regular experience. He has been inside the machine. He knows what works on set — not because he studied it, but because he lived it.

These three shows are not diverse versions of things that already exist. They are shows only Josh could write, because they come from intersections no one else stands at.

> **The Unifying Theme**: All three shows ask the same question from three different angles — *What happens when the role you're playing isn't the person you are?*

## CONCEPT 1: POTLUCK

### The Logline

**A queer, mixed-race music teacher at an underfunded South Central LA middle school accidentally becomes the community therapist — and he's *good* at it. One problem: he has zero credentials, no degree, and if anyone finds out he's been running unlicensed therapy sessions out of his classroom, he loses everything.**

### The Comp

> ***Abbott Elementary* meets *Somebody Somewhere* meets *Nurse Jackie* — if Ted Lasso grew up code-switching between quinceañeras and Shabbat dinners, and was also committing a felony.**

### The Pitch

MARCUS REYES-GOLDSTEIN (30s, Josh Banday) took a "temporary" music teaching gig three years ago after his comedy career stalled. He's the only teacher his students actually trust — because he's the only adult in their lives who doesn't pretend to have it together.

Then a parent broke down crying during a conference, and Marcus — trained in nothing but improv and active listening — said the right thing. Word spread. Now parents schedule "conferences" that are really therapy sessions. The school security guard comes for anger management. The PTA president uses Marcus's classroom as her personal counseling office. Marcus has become the neighborhood's most trusted mental health provider.

**The Secret**: Marcus has no psychology degree. No counseling certification. No license of any kind. He's a music teacher with a gift for listening and a Second City background in "yes, and." What started as one parent venting has become a full-blown unlicensed practice — and Marcus is in too deep to stop. If the school district, the parents, or the state licensing board finds out, he's not just fired. He's sued. Possibly charged.

**The Engine**: Every episode, Marcus helps someone — and every episode, the evidence of his unlicensed practice grows. A parent writes a Yelp review. A student mentions "Mr. G's therapy" in a school essay. The vice principal notices parents booking "conferences" at 7 PM on Saturdays. Marcus keeps putting out fires while starting new ones, because the community genuinely needs him — and he genuinely can't stop.

**The Double Life**: The comedy lives in the **con artist who isn't conning anyone**. Marcus isn't charging money. He's not lying about credentials — people just *assumed*, and he never corrected them. He's doing real good with fake authority, and the show asks: if it works, does the credential matter? (The answer, legally, is yes. And that's the ticking bomb.)

**Why Now**: Post-*Abbott Elementary*, audiences have proven they want workplace comedies set in underrepresented institutions. But where Abbott is a **love letter to teachers**, *Potluck* is a **love letter to the neighborhoods they serve** — with the added engine of a secret that could blow everything up. The therapy boom + the credentialism debate + the mental health crisis in underserved communities = a show that's both timely and timeless.

**The Heart**: Marcus is running from the fact that he belongs here. Every episode, while helping someone else, he accidentally heals a piece of himself. The comedy comes from the chaos. The heart comes from the staying. The tension comes from the secret.

### Series Engine — First 6 Episodes

| # | Episode | The Session | The Evidence Trail | Marcus's Hidden Wound |
|---|---------|------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | "Neutral Zone" | Two feuding parents both come to Marcus separately | A parent thanks "Dr. Goldstein" in the school newsletter | Marcus hasn't told his family he quit comedy |
| 2 | "Potluck" | Marcus mediates a neighborhood food turf war at the annual potluck | Someone tags "@MarcusTherapy" on Instagram | Marcus doesn't know which culture's food to bring — brings store-bought |
| 3 | "Intake" | A new parent demands a "proper intake form" — Marcus Googles one at 2 AM | The vice principal finds a Psychology Today printout in the copy room | Marcus realizes he's actually good at this, and it terrifies him |
| 4 | "Conference" | A parent-teacher conference becomes genuine group therapy | Marcus's mom shows up and outs his sexuality — to a room full of his "clients" | The dual exposure: his secret AND his identity, same room |
| 5 | "Recital" | A parent credits Marcus in her divorce filing as her "therapist" | A lawyer calls the school asking for "Dr. Reyes-Goldstein's" records | Marcus calls his old comedy connections — and confronts why he left |
| 6 | "Mold" | The PTA room gets condemned; the school psychologist (a real one) moves into Marcus's classroom | Marcus now shares space with the person who could expose him | Marcus has to decide: come clean, or double down |

### Why Buy This Show

- **Built-in serial engine**: The unlicensed therapy secret gives this show what *Abbott Elementary* doesn't have — a **season-long tension arc** underneath the episodic comedy. Every episode is funny AND raises the stakes.
- **Culturally electric premise**: The therapy credentialism debate is EVERYWHERE right now. This show lives in that conversation without being preachy.
- **Castable**: Josh Banday is attached as creator, writer, AND lead — a de-risked package.
- **Franchise potential**: The secret can sustain 3-4 seasons of escalation before the inevitable exposure — and the post-exposure seasons are equally rich.
- **Awards lane**: Heartfelt, specific, underrepresented community + queer lens + moral complexity = prestige comedy territory.

## CONCEPT 2: DAD'S PLUS-ONE

### The Logline

**A gorgeous A-list actor — the guy on every magazine cover and late-night couch — gets divorced and discovers he has absolutely no idea how to be a parent without his ex-wife running everything. His 9-year-old daughter, mortified, starts bringing him as HER plus-one to navigate the unhinged social hierarchy of elementary school parents.**

### The Comp

> ***Entourage* meets *Black-ish* meets *Catastrophe* — the parenting show where the most famous man in the room is the least competent person at drop-off.**

### The Pitch

DANIEL BANDAY (30s, Josh Banday) is one of the biggest movie stars on the planet. Mixed-race, jaw-droppingly handsome, freshly divorced — TMZ is camped outside his house. He walked a red carpet last week. He has 14 million Instagram followers.

He also doesn't know his daughter's teacher's name, has never packed a lunch, and found out yesterday that "picture day" requires a signed form.

Daniel was the **trophy parent** — the one who showed up to school events and made every mom swoon and every dad feel inadequate, while his ex-wife RACHEL handled every actual piece of parenting infrastructure behind the scenes. Now, with 50/50 custody, Daniel is dropped into the world she built — and he's a tourist in it.

His daughter CLEO (9, wise beyond her years, exhausted by her father's fame) has already figured out the system. She knows which parents to befriend for good playdates, which to avoid, and exactly how to manipulate the PTA gift basket exchange. Daniel is her project. And she's embarrassed — not because her dad is divorced, but because the man who can command $20 million a picture can't figure out the carpool app.

**The Comic Engine**: The juxtaposition is the show. Daniel walks into school drop-off and every parent *loses their mind* — moms flirt, dads compete, the principal clears her schedule. But the second the celebrity dazzle fades, Daniel is exposed as the most incompetent parent in the room. He's used to having assistants, handlers, and a wife who made the trains run on time. Now it's just him and a 9-year-old who's running the operation.

**The Deeper Layer**: Daniel's fame makes everything harder, not easier. He can't be anonymous at the PTA meeting. His divorce is public. Other parents either worship him or resent him. And Cleo has to navigate being "the movie star's kid" while also being the only one keeping her father alive in the parent economy. The comedy lives in the **collision between red-carpet glamour and lunchbox logistics**. The heart lives in Daniel discovering that the role he's worst at — dad — is the only one that actually matters.

**Why Now**: In the age of celebrity-worship culture and parasocial relationships, this show asks: what happens when the most desired man in America is also the most useless parent in the carpool lane? It's *Notting Hill* in reverse — the famous person isn't the fantasy, they're the disaster.

**The Heart**: Daniel thinks fame makes him a good dad ("I can give her anything"). Cleo knows presence makes a good dad ("You can't even give me a ride"). The show is about Daniel learning that the smallest role of his life is the biggest one.

### Series Engine — First 6 Episodes

| # | Episode | The A-List Collision | The Real Story |
|---|---------|---------------------|---------------|
| 1 | "Drop-Off" | Daniel arrives at school in a Tesla with tinted windows. Paparazzi follow. Cleo is mortified. He doesn't know any parent's name. | Daniel confronts that he outsourced all connection to Rachel |
| 2 | "The Playdate" | A wine-mom invites Cleo over — but really wants Daniel at her house. Cleo sees through it immediately. | Daniel realizes his fame makes Cleo's friendships transactional |
| 3 | "Snack Dad" | Daniel has his assistant order high-end snacks for class. The other kids want Hot Cheetos. | Daniel tries to buy competence — Cleo tells him competence can't be bought |
| 4 | "The App" | Daniel downloads the parent group chat. 847 unread messages, half of which are parents trying to get his autograph | The loneliness of being the most visible single dad who's invisible as a parent |
| 5 | "Culture Day" | Cleo's school has "Heritage Day." Daniel could send a famous chef. Cleo wants him to cook. He can't. | Daniel asks his own mom for help and has to confront his roots vs. his image |
| 6 | "The Plus-One" | Cleo invites Daniel to her best friend's birthday — not as a celebrity guest, but as "just my dad." Daniel has to leave the fame at the door. | Role reversal: the most famous man in the room has to learn how to just be a person |

### Why Buy This Show

- **The juxtaposition IS the hook**: A-list fame vs. F-list parenting. Every scene writes itself. This is the pitch you can explain in one sentence and the room laughs.
- **Star vehicle with built-in casting heat**: Josh Banday playing a version of Hollywood's "It" actor — with his real-life set experience informing every scene — is a magnetic package.
- **Universal hook, specific voice**: Every parent knows the divorced-parent struggle. But the A-list lens makes it aspirational AND relatable.
- **Built-in tenderness engine**: The father-daughter dynamic guarantees emotional payoff every episode without being saccharine, because Cleo's deadpan keeps the movie star grounded.
- **Breakout child actor role**: Cleo is the kind of role that launches careers — Marsai Martin in *Black-ish*, Aubrey Anderson-Emmons in *Modern Family*.
- **Franchise-ready**: The Hollywood world gives this show access to cameos, industry satire, and a scope that expands naturally season over season.

## CONCEPT 3: GUEST STAR

### The Logline

**An actor who's been a guest star on every hit show for 15 years — but never the lead — finally snaps and decides to sabotage his own career to force Hollywood to either promote him or let him go. It backfires: his sabotage keeps accidentally making him more famous.**

### The Comp

> ***Atlanta* meets *The Comeback* — a satirical comedy about the algorithm of fame from the POV of the guy the algorithm forgot.**

### The Pitch

JOSH MARIN (30s, Josh Banday) has the most recognizable face in television — and the least recognizable name. He's been "that guy" on 47 different shows. The cop's partner. The funny coworker. The patient in the hospital scene. He's worked with every A-lister, been on every set, knows every secret — and nobody knows who he is.

After 15 years, Josh decides: **no more**. He's going to be so bad, so difficult, so uncastable that Hollywood has to make a choice: star him or fire him.

**The Engine**: Every episode, Josh takes a guest role with a plan to tank it. But his self-destruction keeps creating viral moments. His meltdown on a prestige drama becomes a meme. His refusal to learn lines on a sitcom gets praised as "fearless improv." His sabotage of a streaming show's finale accidentally fixes the ending. **The harder he tries to fail, the more the industry rewards him** — but always as the guest, never the lead.

**The Satirical Layer**: Each episode parodies a different genre/show type (prestige drama, network procedural, reality competition, streaming limited series), giving Josh an anthology-within-a-series structure that lets him showcase range while commenting on the entire television landscape.

**Why Now**: This is *The Studio*, *Hacks*, and *Atlanta*'s love child — a show about the **machinery of fame** told from the perspective of someone trapped in its gears. In an era where "that guy" character actors are finally getting their cultural moment (thanks to social media and podcasts like "Character Actors Acting"), this show rides a real wave.

**The Heart**: Josh thinks he wants to be famous. What he actually wants is to be *seen* — by the industry, by his family, by himself. The comedy-with-a-heart is the gap between the performance and the person.

### Series Engine — First 6 Episodes

| # | Episode | The Gig (Genre Parody) | The Sabotage | What Actually Happens |
|---|---------|----------------------|-------------|----------------------|
| 1 | "Day Player" | Prestige drama (Succession-type) | Josh improvises his 2-line part into a monologue | The monologue goes viral. He's invited back. |
| 2 | "Cop's Partner" | Network procedural | Josh refuses to die in the scripted death scene | The showrunner rewrites the finale around him |
| 3 | "The Table Read" | Streaming comedy | Josh roasts the script at the table read | The creator thinks he's a genius and offers him a recurring role |
| 4 | "Reality" | Reality competition show (as a celebrity guest judge) | Josh tells every contestant the truth | He becomes the breakout fan favorite |
| 5 | "Craft Services" | Big-budget action movie | Josh bonds with the crew instead of the stars | A documentary crew starts following him; he still can't get his name right |
| 6 | "The Lead" | Josh finally gets offered a lead role | Josh sabotages himself out of fear | He has to face the question: did he ever *actually* want this? |

### Why Buy This Show

- **Meta without being navel-gazing**: Unlike *The Studio* or *Episodes*, this comes from the *bottom* of Hollywood's hierarchy — funnier, more relatable, and wildly more diverse.
- **Anthology engine**: Each episode parodies a different genre, giving the show built-in variety and viral clip potential while maintaining serialized character arcs.
- **Josh Banday IS the show**: The ultimate actor-auteur vehicle. Josh's real résumé IS the character's résumé. The authenticity is undeniable.
- **IP expansion**: The "Guest Star" brand extends naturally to a podcast, social content, and cross-platform storytelling.

### MARCUS REYES-GOLDSTEIN — *POTLUCK*

**Fanciful-Real Calibration**
- **Fanciful**: A music teacher with zero psychology credentials becomes the most trusted therapist in South Central LA — using nothing but improv training and active listening.
- **Real**: A queer, mixed-race man who abandoned his comedy dream is terrified he'll also abandon the community that now depends on him.

**Identity Core**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Full name** | Marcus Alejandro Reyes-Goldstein |
| **Age** | 33 at series start |
| **Physical** | Slight build, always slightly underdressed for his own authority. Carries himself like someone perpetually trying to take up less space — until he's in session, when he leans forward, opens his hands, and becomes twice his size. Wears the same three button-downs on rotation. |
| **Occupation** | Music teacher (officially). Unlicensed community therapist (actually). Failed stand-up comedian (secretly). |

**Emotional Architecture**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **What he loves** | (1) The specific sound of a classroom going quiet when a kid actually *gets* a song. (2) His abuela's voice singing "Cielito Lindo" while doing dishes. (3) The moment in improv when someone says something true by accident. |
| **What he fears** | Surface: being exposed as a fraud. Deeper: that he abandoned comedy not because he wasn't good enough, but because he was afraid of being *seen* — and that the therapy practice is just another way to hide behind other people's problems. |
| **Neuroses** | Rehearses conversations in the shower. Can't leave a room without checking that he said goodbye to everyone. Organizes sheet music alphabetically but his apartment is a disaster. |
| **Anger triggers** | When someone dismisses a kid's problem as "not real." When his mother tries to set him up in front of people. When credential gatekeepers dismiss community knowledge. |
| **Secret shame** | He didn't quit comedy because he failed. He quit because he got a callback for a Netflix special and ghosted, because performing his own story felt like selling it. |
| **Defining loss** | His grandfather (Jewish side) died during COVID, alone in a nursing home. Marcus couldn't visit. He became a therapist because no one was there for his grandfather. |

**Voice Fingerprint**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Vocabulary** | Streets-smart, culturally fluent across four traditions. Uses Spanish, Tagalog, Yiddish, and AAVE interchangeably — the ultimate code-switcher. |
| **Speech rhythm** | Starts sentences slow, builds speed when excited, cuts himself off before the punchline — a comedian who's learned to stop performing. |
| **Verbal tics** | "No but actually though" (emphasis). "I hear that" (therapist mode). "Wait — say that part again" (improv training). |
| **Silence behavior** | Fidgets with a guitar pick he keeps in his pocket. When truly uncomfortable, hums three bars of a song he won't finish. |

**The Literal Wallet**
- A laminated fake "therapy license" his best friend made as a joke
- A crumpled set list from his last open mic (3 years ago)
- A picture of his grandfather wearing a yarmulke at his quinceañera
- A Psychology Today article titled "When Peer Support Becomes Practice" — highlighted, annotated, dog-eared

**Connelly 6-Layer Build**

| Layer | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Name** | Marcus Alejandro Reyes-Goldstein — every name is a cultural claim, and together they sound like an identity crisis set to music |
| **Outsider lens** | Insider access to four communities, outsider status in all of them |
| **Telling detail** | He keeps the classroom temperature at exactly 68° — the temperature his grandfather kept the house |
| **Nod economy** | When parents arrive for "therapy," Marcus gives a small nod and says "I saved you the good chair" |
| **Ambient conflict** | The copy machine in the teacher's lounge has a "Dr. G's office hours" flyer that Marcus didn't put there |
| **Geography trigger** | The supply closet where Marcus hides to call his grandmother when sessions get too heavy — it smells like old paper and Pine-Sol |

**Transformation Arc**
- **STATE A**: A man hiding from his own ambition behind other people's problems.
- **STATE B**: A man who chooses to earn the authority he's been borrowing.

**Voice Isolation Test**

> **Ordering food**: "Can I get the — actually, you know what, what do *you* think is good here? No but actually though. What do you order when nobody's watching?"

> **Describing someone he loves**: "My grandpa used to wear a yarmulke to my tía's house for Christmas. Just walked in. Didn't explain. Nobody asked him to explain once. I think about that every day."

> **Receiving bad news**: *[Long pause. Fidgets with the guitar pick. Hums three bars of something. Then:]* "Okay. Okay, so what do we — I hear that. I do. I hear that."

### JOSH MARIN — *GUEST STAR*

**Fanciful-Real Calibration**
- **Fanciful**: A man tries to sabotage his career and the harder he fails, the more famous he becomes — but always as the guest, never the lead.
- **Real**: A man who's been performing so long he's forgotten whether he's acting or living.

**Identity Core**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Full name** | Joshua Reyes Marin |
| **Age** | 37 at series start |
| **Physical** | The face everybody recognizes and nobody can name. Handsome in a way casting directors call "non-threatening leading man energy" — which means: never the lead. Dresses slightly too well for someone nobody's heard of. |
| **Occupation** | Professional guest star. 47 shows, 0 leads. His agents describe him as "incredibly versatile," which is industry code for "we don't know what to do with him." |

**Emotional Architecture**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **What he loves** | (1) The moment between "quiet on set" and "action" — the last two seconds where he exists as himself. (2) Craft services coffee — not because it's good, but because it means he's on a set, which means he exists. (3) His mother's face when her friends recognize him on TV. |
| **What he fears** | Surface: aging out of "that guy" range without ever becoming "the guy." Deeper: that he's spent 15 years being other people and has no idea who he is when the camera isn't running. |
| **Secret shame** | He turned down a lead role 8 years ago because he was terrified of carrying a show. The show became a hit. The other actor won an Emmy. |
| **Defining loss** | His college roommate — equally talented — quit acting at 25 to become a teacher. He now has a family, a house, a life with a name. Josh has a résumé with 47 names that aren't his. |

**Voice Fingerprint**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Vocabulary** | Performer-sharp — always in rhythm, always landing a beat. Uses industry jargon casually but catches himself and translates. |
| **Speech rhythm** | Stand-up comedian cadence — setups and punchlines even in casual conversation. But when genuinely vulnerable, the rhythm breaks and he speaks in fragments. |
| **Verbal tics** | "Here's the thing" (performative wisdom). "I've done 47 shows" (credential drop when insecure). "Am I wrong?" (needs external validation, always). |
| **Silence behavior** | Fills it. Josh doesn't do silence. When forced into it, he narrates: "This is the part where I say something profound. I don't have it." |

**The Literal Wallet**
- 14 call sheets from 14 different shows — organized chronologically, like a scrapbook of a career nobody else remembers
- A folded IMDb printout where a commenter wrote: "This guy is in EVERYTHING. What's his name?"
- His SAG card, laminated, carried daily — the only credential that proves he exists
- A Post-it note from a showrunner: "You were the best part of this show" — the show was cancelled after one season

**Connelly 6-Layer Build**

| Layer | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Name** | Josh Marin — designed to be hard to remember. At parties, people hear "Josh" and stop listening before "Marin." |
| **Outsider lens** | Industry insider, fame outsider. He's been on every set and knows every secret — but none of that knowledge helps because he's invisible |
| **Telling detail** | Josh keeps a suit bag in his car at all times — pressed, camera-ready. Not because he has auditions, but because he refuses to look like he's given up |
| **Nod economy** | When crew members recognize him, Josh gives a two-finger salute to the temple. Their shared acknowledgment that the real work of TV is done by the people nobody sees. |
| **Ambient conflict** | His apartment wall has framed headshots from every decade — 20s, 30s. Each says "JOSH MARIN" in a different font. The fonts got smaller as the headshots got bigger. |
| **Geography trigger** | Sound Stage 12 at Warner Bros — the first stage he ever worked on. He books jobs on that lot whenever possible. He tells himself it's convenient. It's actually a pilgrimage. |

**Transformation Arc**
- **STATE A**: A man who defines himself by the career that refuses to define him.
- **STATE B**: A man who discovers that being seen isn't about the screen — it's about being known by the people who matter off-camera.

**Voice Isolation Test**

## ADVERSARIAL STRESS TEST

> **Purpose**: Surface every objection a network buyer, studio exec, or fellowship reader would raise — then arm Josh with counter-arguments before he walks into the room.

---

### POTLUCK — 5 Attack Vectors

**"This is just Abbott Elementary"**
> **Counter**: Abbott is a workplace comedy that happens to be in a school. *Potluck* is a **community comedy** that happens to include a school. Marcus's classroom is the setting, not the subject. The show's engine is the neighborhood. Abbott never leaves the building. Potluck barely stays in it.
>
> **Killer Line**: "Abbott is a love letter to teachers. Potluck is a love letter to the block they go home to."

**"The therapy secret feels like a legal liability"**
> **Counter**: The show doesn't glorify unlicensed practice — it **interrogates it**. The question — *if it works, does the credential matter?* — is one of the most debated questions in modern mental health. This isn't *Breaking Bad* celebrating crime; it's *Nurse Jackie* asking where the line is.
>
> **Killer Line**: "The show doesn't take a side. The show takes the question seriously. That's what makes it prestige."

**"A music teacher protagonist feels limited"**
> **Counter**: Marcus isn't a music teacher show — he's a **community center operator who technically teaches music**. The therapy secret alone provides 4 seasons of escalation.
>
> **Killer Line**: "The music classroom is the Cheers bar. It's where everyone goes. But the stories come from the neighborhood outside."

**"Queer protagonist — is there audience?"**
> **Counter**: Marcus's queerness isn't the premise — it's one ingredient in a five-dimensional identity. *Somebody Somewhere* proved that a queer protagonist in a naturalized setting works. Marcus is queer the same way your neighbor is queer — you know, it comes up, and also 47 other things are happening.

**"Josh Banday as lead — is he bankable?"**
> **Counter**: This is the exact moment in Josh's career where the answer becomes yes. Creator, writer, AND star = a de-risked package.
>
> **Killer Line**: "You're not betting on an unknown. You're getting a four-season series regular who also writes and produces. Quinta Brunson had less on her résumé when she pitched Abbott."

---

### DAD'S PLUS-ONE — 5 Attack Vectors

**"We've seen the hot divorced dad show"**
> **Counter**: None of those shows had this juxtaposition. Daniel isn't a regular hot guy — he's an A-list movie star with 14 million followers who can't figure out the carpool app. The inversion has never been done.
>
> **Killer Line**: "Every divorced-dad show makes the dad a schlub. This one makes the dad the biggest movie star on Earth — and that makes him *worse* at parenting, not better."

**"Is it aspirational or relatable?"**
> **Counter**: It's both, and that's the engine. *Schitt's Creek* proved that aspirational-meets-incompetent is a goldmine.
>
> **Killer Line**: "It's Schitt's Creek at drop-off. You envy his life, you pity his parenting."

**"The kid being smarter than the parent — isn't that a trope?"**
> **Counter**: Cleo isn't precocious — she's **competent out of necessity**. That frustration makes her a real character, not a trope.
>
> **Killer Line**: "Cleo isn't a precocious kid. She's an unpaid assistant who happens to be nine."

**"Celebrity satire — competing with The Studio?"**
> **Counter**: *Dad's Plus-One* isn't a Hollywood satire — it's a **parenting comedy where the parent happens to be famous**. 80% of its time is in the parent economy: school, playdates, birthday parties.
>
> **Killer Line**: "The show isn't set in Hollywood. It's set at a school in the Valley. Daniel goes home to Hollywood — the show stays at the pickup line."

**"Where's the serialized arc beyond 'dad gets better'?"**
> **Counter**: Season 2: career-defining role requiring 4 months on location. Season 3: Cleo's identity crisis. Season 4: Rachel wants full custody back.

### GUEST STAR — 5 Attack Vectors

**"Inside-Hollywood shows don't perform"**
> **Counter**: *Atlanta* (4 Emmys), *Hacks* (Emmy winner, 3 seasons), *Barry* (HBO's biggest comedy hit of the 2020s). The lane is hot. But all those shows observe Hollywood from the top. *Guest Star* observes it from the **bottom**.
>
> **Killer Line**: "Atlanta is Hollywood from the outside. The Studio is Hollywood from the top. Guest Star is Hollywood from the bottom. Nobody's told that story."

**"The sabotage-backfires engine seems unsustainable"**
> **Counter**: Sabotage is the Season 1 hook, not the series engine. Season 2: Josh gets fame and discovers it's worse. Season 3: Josh finds his actual calling and starts over.
>
> **Killer Line**: "Season 1 is about trying to fail. Season 2 is about succeeding and wishing you hadn't. Season 3 is about finding the thing that was right the whole time."

**"The anthology structure sounds expensive"**
> **Counter**: Each episode's "show-within-a-show" only needs 30% of screen time. *Barry* did this with plays. *Hacks* does it with standup sets.

> **Killer Line**: "It's not an anthology. It's one show with a rotating B-story that gives you clip content for free."

> **Killer Line**: "The best comedies of the last decade are all someone playing themselves. Josh has the résumé to make this character undeniable."
