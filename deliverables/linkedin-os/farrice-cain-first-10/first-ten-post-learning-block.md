## P01. The sale I talked them out of

**Bucket:** Authority
**North star:** Buyer trust is built when judgment outranks the transaction.
**Hypothesis:** The counter-side story makes the new supplement positioning credible faster than a credential list.
**Primary metric:** Qualified profile views.
**Gate A:** anchor=The Vitamin Shoppe · artifacts=Proof Library, Experience canon, Voice Card · stakes=the customer's money and trust · mechanism=no-stake judgment changes the recommendation

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** The best sales I made at The Vitamin Shoppe<br><br>were the ones I talked people out of.
2. My name tag said Health Enthusiast. Half the job was telling somebody to put it back.
3. A $60 test booster taught me more about marketing than any copywriting course did.

### Draft

The best sales I made at The Vitamin Shoppe

were the ones I talked people out of.

Guys would walk in already sold. They had the magazine open on their phone, the product name memorized, and enough cash to make the whole conversation easy.

Then we would talk.

Sometimes the $60 test booster was sitting on top of four hours of sleep and a diet with barely enough protein to support the goal. Sometimes the expensive product was worth it because the formula was open, the dose held up, and the cheaper version was mostly label.

Plenty of days the honest answer was creatine. Some days it was food. A few times it was, “Put it back.”

That counter taught me the difference between knowing a product and knowing the person deciding whether to buy it.

The label knows what is in the bottle. The ad knows what the company wants to say. The buyer is carrying a different question.

“Is this worth my money?”

“Will I still be taking it a month from now?”

“Is this going to do anything I can notice?”

I spent the next 18 years hearing versions of those questions as a trainer.

That is the part I bring to supplement creative now. I am not trying to make every product sound exciting. I am trying to find the true thing a skeptical buyer can hear without rolling his eyes.

Sometimes that means a harder sell.

Sometimes it still means put it back.

## P02. Transparent Labs owns the wrong word

**Bucket:** Growth
**North star:** Product truth should lead before category adjectives.
**Hypothesis:** A named-brand teardown with a counterintuitive proof gap will generate non-follower profile visits.
**Primary metric:** Non-follower impressions and profile views.
**Gate A:** anchor=Transparent Labs · artifacts=homepage, Bulk Pre-Workout page, testing page, 20 reviews · stakes=an expensive open formula marketed with free adjectives · mechanism=proof hierarchy hides the uncopyable asset

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** Transparent Labs prints every dose.<br><br>Its homepage sells the word everyone owns.
2. The company is paying for a formula. Its customers are buying a flavor.
3. Transparent Labs owns the strongest word in supplements. It is not “clean.”

### Draft

Transparent Labs prints every dose.

Its homepage sells the word everyone owns.

I captured the public surface on July 27. Bulk Pre-Workout listed eight grams of citrulline malate, four grams of beta-alanine, 2,500 milligrams of betaine, 300 milligrams of Alpha-GPC, and 200 milligrams of caffeine.

No proprietary blend. No place to quietly shrink the formula later.

That is expensive honesty.

The homepage led with “clean ingredients,” “transparent labels,” and a “higher standard.” All true. All available to any competitor with a copywriter and an afternoon.

Then I read 20 recent reviews on the whey isolate. Customers talked about chocolate ice cream, taste, mixing, digestion, and recovery.

Not one mentioned a dose.

The company is paying for a formula. The customer is buying a flavor.

There is nothing wrong with flavor. It helps people keep using the product. But it cannot explain why this formula deserves to win against something cheaper that tastes just as good.

Transparent Labs already owns the answer. The word is full.

Full label. Full dose. Every number exposed before the customer spends a dollar.

“Clean” is a mood the whole shelf can borrow. “Full” costs money every time the tub is made.

That difference should own the first screen.

## P03. Twenty hooks, one belief

**Bucket:** Authority
**North star:** Production volume cannot settle an unmade campaign decision.
**Hypothesis:** A compact self-audit will earn saves from growth and creative operators.
**Primary metric:** Saves.
**Gate A:** anchor=Meta Ad Library · artifacts=ICP Truth Map, current teardown corpus, offer canon · stakes=another paid creative round that produces no learning · mechanism=surface variation disguises belief repetition

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** Twenty new hooks can hide one unchanged belief.
2. Your creative team may be shipping faster and learning less.
3. Open your last 20 ads. Count beliefs, not files.

### Draft

Twenty new hooks can hide one unchanged belief.

Open your last creative round in Meta’s Ad Library. Ignore the colors, creators, aspect ratios, and first lines for a minute.

Write down the one thing each ad needs the buyer to believe before the product makes sense.

If all 20 answers are “this gives you more energy,” you did not test 20 angles. You tested one angle in 20 outfits.

That distinction matters because the dashboard cannot tell you whether the underlying decision was weak. It can only report what happened after you made it.

Before the next round, answer four questions:

1. Name the buyer's current belief.
2. Describe the change required before the product becomes the obvious choice.
3. Choose the product truth strong enough to carry that change.
4. Decide which result would earn the idea another dollar.

Now the hooks have jobs.

One might challenge the buyer’s explanation for the problem. Another might change the comparison set. A third could make the proof visible. A fourth could name the moment the product earns its place in the routine.

Those are different hypotheses. New wording alone is not.

The team can still make 20 ads. It should simply know whether it is producing more files or testing more beliefs.

## P04. Momentous buried a census

**Bucket:** Growth
**North star:** The strongest proof asset should own the first screen.
**Hypothesis:** The “32 of 32” contrast will produce profile clicks because the proof is both specific and immediately legible.
**Primary metric:** Profile views per 1,000 impressions.
**Gate A:** anchor=Momentous · artifacts=homepage, creatine page, standards page, ten reviews · stakes=category-leading proof reduced to manufacturing language · mechanism=proof placed below generic convenience claims

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** Momentous has proof every brand wants.<br><br>Its best line sits on an interior page.
2. Thirty-two NFL teams trust Momentous. The homepage sells mixability.
3. A brand can prove its product is safe and still fail to make it worth wanting.

### Draft

Momentous has proof every brand wants.

Its best line sits on an interior page.

When I captured its public creatine pages on July 27, Momentous named six systematic reviews and meta-analyses, the testing house, and the month of the test. The standards page said the company spends up to 2% of revenue on independent testing and certification.

Then came the line nobody else in the category can copy:

All 32 NFL teams and more than 200 collegiate and professional teams trust the brand.

Thirty-two out of thirty-two. That is closer to a census than a testimonial.

It lived on an interior standards page.

The creatine hero focused on quality, manufacturing, and mixability. The three bullets under the price kept talking about the factory.

The customer reviews followed the message. Tasteless. Dissolves well. Easy in coffee. Convenient for travel.

All useful. None of it explains why this brand deserves the premium better than 32 out of 32 does.

Proof has an order.

The first proof should make the product worth wanting. The rest should make the choice feel safe.

Momentous had those jobs reversed. The certifications were doing the persuasion while the league-wide trust signal waited inside the site.

The repair does not require a new claim. Move the sentence the company already earned.

Lead with the locker rooms. Let the testing explain why they stayed.

Source boundary: public pages captured July 27, 2026. I do not know the company’s internal strategy or performance data.

## P05. When legal cuts the strongest line

**Bucket:** Authority
**North star:** Claim-safe creative can stay decisive.
**Hypothesis:** Reframing legal review as an angle-design problem will resonate with founders, growth leads, and evidence guardians.
**Primary metric:** Saves and qualified comments.
**Gate A:** anchor=FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance · artifacts=official guidance, claim-safety canon, teardown corpus · stakes=a campaign becomes generic after one risky line is removed · mechanism=the health claim was carrying both persuasion and positioning

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** Legal cut the strongest line.<br><br>Then the whole campaign went flat.
2. One deleted health claim should not collapse the whole campaign.
3. If the ad becomes generic after legal review, the claim was doing two jobs.

### Draft

Legal cut the strongest line.

Then the whole campaign went flat.

A team writes the loud version first. The product does too much, too fast, with more certainty than the evidence can defend.

Then the qualified reviewer removes the line the evidence cannot carry.

The next draft gets softer everywhere. “Supports.” “Promotes.” “May help.” The words are safer, but the ad no longer has a reason to exist.

That usually means the health claim was carrying two jobs:

It described an outcome, and it supplied the entire campaign idea.

Those jobs can be separated.

The angle can live in the buyer’s occasion, the trade they are making, the routine the product must enter, the comparison they already use, or the proof they can inspect.

A creatine gummy does not need a miracle promise when an outside test shows the labeled dose survived the format. A fish oil does not need to promise a cured joint when the buyer can scan the lab result for the exact batch in the kitchen.

The claim still needs evidence and qualified review. The campaign needs a belief worth testing.

When those are separate, legal can narrow a sentence without flattening the whole idea.

I read for signal, not law. The brand’s qualified reviewer keeps the pen.

## P06. Create measured over the label

**Bucket:** Growth
**North star:** Measured product truth beats a seasonal adjective.
**Hypothesis:** The 4.59g result will stop attention and the proof-placement critique will create authority.
**Primary metric:** Non-follower impressions and profile views.
**Gate A:** anchor=Create Wellness · artifacts=Create pages, outside lab report, five reviews · stakes=four of six tested gummies failed while the winner buried the result · mechanism=the category’s failure makes exact dose the mainstream question

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** An outside lab tested six creatine gummies.<br><br>Four failed. Create measured over the label.
2. Two creatine gummies measured zero. Create came back over the label.
3. Create Wellness owns the best sentence in creatine gummies. Somebody else wrote it in a lab report.

### Draft

An outside lab tested six creatine gummies.

Four failed. Create measured over the label.

Two products had no detectable creatine at all.

Create came back over the label in a format where heat and water can degrade creatine before the customer ever opens the bag.

Its own quality page went further when I captured it on August 7. Every batch was third-party tested. The gummies were pulled and retested at 18 months. The core gummies were described as the only NSF Certified for Sport creatine gummies in the world.

That last line sat in the final FAQ answer under a question about children.

The homepage led with “Creatine for Everybody” and a seasonal sale.

Then I read five recent customer reviews. Four talked about flavor. Several mentioned convenience. None mentioned a gram, a lab, or a test.

Flavor helps the habit. Convenience helps the product travel. The measured dose is why the buyer should trust the format in the first place.

Create already paid for the hardest version of the argument. It simply made the buyer dig for it.

Put 4.59 against 4.5 on the first screen. Name the source. Explain why the format makes that number hard to hold.

The category’s failure created the question. Create owns the answer.

## P08. The formula removed the bottles

**Bucket:** Authority
**North star:** One lead belief must organize a multi-benefit product.
**Hypothesis:** A current three-brand pattern will earn saves from founders planning an all-in-one launch.
**Primary metric:** Saves and shares.
**Gate A:** anchor=DIZZY, Isagenix WITHIN, Attaboy · artifacts=three official product pages checked 2026-08-29 · stakes=an all-in-one formula makes the campaign sound like the whole stack talking at once · mechanism=benefit compression without message hierarchy preserves buyer decisions

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** The formula removed the bottles.<br><br>The page kept every decision.
2. Three new all-in-one supplements solved the stack and inherited the stack’s messaging problem.
3. “Five benefits in one scoop” still leaves the buyer choosing which benefit matters.

### Draft

The formula removed the bottles.

The page kept every decision.

DIZZY calls Daily Flow a 12-in-one formula. Isagenix describes WITHIN as a five-in-one system for gut health, immune health, recovery, and overall wellness. Attaboy puts strength, focus, energy, recovery, and hydration into one scoop.

All three are making a real product decision: simplify the routine.

The message still asks the buyer to choose among every job the old routine performed.

Energy. Focus. Recovery. Hydration. Gut health. Immunity. Performance. One page can support all of them. It cannot lead with all of them at the same volume.

The first campaign decision is not which benefit to delete.

It is which buyer, occasion, and belief gets the first screen.

The remaining benefits become support.

For the woman replacing five separate products, simplification may lead while creatine and electrolytes explain why the trade is credible. For the operator running on caffeine, steady energy might lead while hydration and recovery make the routine feel complete. A gut-health launch may lead with one daily ritual and let the five systems prove depth underneath it.

Same formula. Different argument.

The buyer can learn the full stack after the first click. Before that, one promise has to earn the click.

The product team already did the hard work of deciding what belongs in the scoop. The campaign team still has to decide what the scoop means first.

Sources: official DIZZY, Isagenix WITHIN, and Attaboy product pages checked August 29, 2026. Product efficacy and buyer preference are not claimed.

## P09. Puori made proof scannable

**Bucket:** Growth
**North star:** Verification becomes persuasive when the buyer can hold it.
**Hypothesis:** The exact-batch scan and Costco comparison create a concrete tension that earns profile clicks.
**Primary metric:** Profile views per 1,000 impressions.
**Gate A:** anchor=Puori · artifacts=homepage, O3 page, Clean Label page, 15 reviews · stakes=batch-level testing fails to change the comparison set · mechanism=verification stays abstract until attached to one person and one tub

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** Puori lets you scan the result for your exact tub.<br><br>A customer still compared it with Costco.
2. Puori tests every batch against more than 200 contaminants. A paying customer still could not tell why it was better.
3. The strongest proof in fish oil was sitting behind a QR code while the customer bought on taste.

### Draft

Puori lets you scan the result for your exact tub.

A customer still compared it with Costco.

When I captured the public surface on July 27, Puori said it tested every batch of every product against more than 200 contaminants and published the results behind a QR code on the package.

Not a category average or a sample standing in for the whole product line.

Your batch.

Then I read 15 recent reviews on the O3 fish oil. Eight talked about the absence of fishy taste. One customer wrote that the product was fine, then said she was not sure it was better than Costco’s or whether she needed it anymore.

That is a paying customer holding some of the strongest verification in the category and comparing it to a warehouse club.

Every dollar of testing failed to reach her decision.

Two hundred contaminants is an impressive system. It is also difficult to feel.

The QR scan changes that. A buyer scans the tub in her kitchen, enters its batch number, and loads the result on her own phone.

That is the ad.

Show the scan. Show what the report answers. Explain which question this batch-level proof settles that a generic “third-party tested” badge cannot.

The testing is already real. The marketing job is to make the buyer hold it before she compares the price.

## P10. I was buying confidence

**Bucket:** Personal
**North star:** Buyer desire deserves truth, not ridicule.
**Hypothesis:** A buyer-origin story will deepen recognition without turning into a sales pitch or family memoir.
**Primary metric:** Depth-rate comments and profile views.
**Gate A:** anchor=Farrice’s magazine-era supplement buying · artifacts=Proof Library, locked About narrative, Voice Card · stakes=money spent on hope and a slowly corroding belief · mechanism=marketing borrows the buyer’s desired identity

### Hook candidates

1. **Selected:** I bought what the supplement magazines sold me.<br><br>Most of the time I was buying confidence.
2. The ad and the article used to be the same page. I believed both.
3. My shelf of half-used supplements was really a shelf of unfinished promises.

### Draft

I bought what the supplement magazines sold me.

Most of the time I was buying confidence.

Pre-workouts. Fat burners. Test boosters. Whatever the guy in the ad said separated the serious people from everybody else.

Back then the ad and the article were often the same page. I did not know where the education stopped and the sale started.

I just knew how I wanted to feel.

More disciplined. More certain. Like the next tub might turn me into the person who finally had all of this handled.

Years of that. A few things worked. Most did nothing. The half-used tubs stayed in the cabinet long after the promise wore off.

Working behind a supplement counter changed the way I saw the sale. Coaching changed it again.

The person asking about a product is rarely asking only about the product. He is asking whether the goal is still possible, whether he wasted the last six months, and whether this thing can help him trust himself again.

That desire is not stupid. It is exactly why the marketing has to tell the truth.

I do not hate a strong claim. I hate a borrowed one. I hate when a brand uses the buyer’s hope to make a sentence bigger than the evidence underneath it.

Good supplement marketing should help the right person make a cleaner decision.

Sometimes the answer is buy it.

Sometimes the kindest answer is still put it back.

## Block review rule

After all ten posts are published by Farrice and the ledger contains real results:

1. Run `review` against `posts.csv`.
2. Compare follower efficiency, profile-view rate, depth rate, qualified DMs, leads, and revenue.
3. Identify the shared topic, hook, and format pattern among the top three.
4. Preserve nine variables and change one weak variable for block two.
5. Do not infer causality from ten posts. Treat the pattern as the next hypothesis.
