**Current front door:** $500 prepaid · five finished static ads + one production-ready video concept · 72 hours after complete accepted inputs

**Audit owners:** Jeremy Haynes Cold Traffic Offer Mastery, with Claim-Safe Health Marketing as a bounded gate

## Verdict

**Keep the Control-Beater. Replace the ladder behind it.**

The pilot's components answer real buyer constraints: repetitive creative, unclear test logic, claim-review rework, slow production handoffs, and the risk of hiring an unproven outsider. The architecture is strong enough for a real payment test.

The current nine-stage GEO/content ladder is the misalignment. A buyer who purchases six claim-bounded creative tests does not naturally ascend into ghostwriting, AEO/GEO, or a Source-to-Search Trust Layer. Those may be valid separate services, but they do not absorb the next problem created by the Control-Beater.

The correct ladder stays on one commercial spine:

> Better creative decisions → production-usable tests → returned learning → repeated creative improvement.

Do not improve the offer again before exposure. Correct the ladder, preserve the claim boundary, and seek the first cleared $500 payment.

## Umbrella narrative map

### ICP definition

The fast-yes buyer is a founder or lean growth/creative lead at an already-selling, usually $1M–$15M supplement, performance-nutrition, recovery, or wellness brand that already runs paid social, has production capacity, and lacks a senior creative-strategy owner.

### Grounding status

`GROUNDED-WITH-GAPS`

The map uses two established local research surfaces, public-source category observations, and prospect/offer work. It does not include ten real sales calls or a statistically representative buyer-interview corpus. Inferred founder language remains inference.

### Problems

1. **Creative fatigue becomes ad-library sameness.** The team needs a new round, but “more ads” often means cosmetic variations on one old argument.
2. **The missing work is upstream of writing.** Nobody has clearly decided which buyer belief to move, which proof can carry it, and what each asset should teach.
3. **Claims become either dangerous or dull.** Aggressive language creates substantiation and review risk; compliance-only rewrites can flatten the argument until nothing remains worth testing.
4. **Production does not automatically create learning.** Files ship without a hypothesis map, comparable test question, or clean results-return loop.
5. **An unknown outside strategist is a trust risk.** Farrice has relevant customer depth and method proof, but no supplement-client performance case study for this exact package.

### Circumstances

1. The brand already has a product, an incumbent ad, paid-social activity, and someone who can make or launch creative.
2. A launch, reformulation, scaling push, or fatigue problem creates the deadline.
3. A small team may have a media buyer, designer, editor, agency, or freelancers without one person owning the creative hypothesis.
4. Product-specific substantiation and final claim approval remain with the brand and its qualified reviewer.
5. The buyer is wary of agencies, generic AI volume, expensive decks, and outsiders who need weeks of discovery before producing something usable.

### Desired outcomes

1. Six assets that make materially different, defensible creative bets instead of six wrappers around one message.
2. A production-ready handoff that does not require another strategy meeting.
3. Clear claim zones showing what is approved, conditional, held, or outside scope.
4. A test map that tells the media owner what each asset is intended to teach.
5. A small, fast paid engagement that proves working fit before a larger commitment.
6. A reliable next-round process that turns returned results into better hypotheses instead of more guesswork.

### Umbrella narrative

You do not need six more versions of the same supplement ad. You already have people who can change a hook, swap a background, resize a layout, or ask an AI model for more options. The expensive gap appears one step earlier: deciding what the next creative round should actually test.

That decision is harder in supplements and performance products because the strongest-sounding claim is not automatically a claim the brand can defend. The founder, growth lead, media buyer, creative producer, and reviewer may each own a piece, but nobody owns the whole movement from buyer language to product truth to a distinct, production-usable test. The result is familiar: more files, slower reviews, less confidence, and an account that learns almost nothing.

You may already have tried an agency, freelancer, internal brainstorm, positioning exercise, or AI-volume workflow. The work often fails in one of two ways. It remains strategically interesting but never becomes launchable creative, or it becomes launchable creative without a clear belief, proof direction, or learning question. In a regulated category, late claim edits can erase the argument entirely.

What you actually want is a small set of creative bets you can defend and learn from. Each asset should move a different buyer belief, use proof the brand can substantiate, fit the production reality, and tell the media owner what the test is supposed to reveal. The handoff should be usable without another meeting.

Leaving that decision unowned costs another production cycle, another review loop, and another spend window used to test decoration instead of strategy. The Control-Beater is the first bounded intervention: six claim-aware creative outputs in 72 hours after complete inputs, with the hypothesis and test logic attached. It makes the next round worth running without promising a winner.

### 30-second cold read

> You do not need six more versions of the same supplement ad. You need a creative round where each asset makes a different, defensible bet, and your team can tell what it learned.

**Cold-read status:** `Untested with buyers`. Structurally legible; human recognition still required.

## Audience-state classification

The buyer is **in-market for new paid-social creative** but **needs-convinced about Farrice and the strategic layer**.

That means the offer should not educate them about why ads matter. It should make four things obvious quickly:

1. This is not commodity production or six cosmetic variants.
2. Farrice understands the end customer and the claim environment.
3. The engagement is small, fast, specific, and prepaid.
4. The work produces a usable learning event without promising performance.

The buyer-facing order should be:

> Recognizable creative-round failure → cost of another same-message cycle → desired learning state → Control-Beater bridge → exact scope and boundary.

## Scar-tissue assessment

No radioactive done-for-you component is present if the buyer already has production and launch owners. The offer becomes radioactive when it is sold to a brand that cannot provide an incumbent, substantiation, approval ownership, or production capacity; Farrice would then be forced to invent missing truth or absorb unpriced execution.

The most sensitive scar is the buyer's distrust of another outsider selling “strategy.” A small prepaid scope, inspectable method, exact handoff, clear exclusions, honestly labeled adjacent proof, and one bounded make-right answer that distrust better than a larger explanation.

## Bridge articulation verdict

The internal method is strong, but the public name can lead with the bridge before the two cliffs are clear. “Control-Beater” sounds like an outcome promise unless immediately qualified.

Use this articulation:

> Your next creative round should test different buyer beliefs instead of six versions of the same ad. The Control-Beater turns one incumbent, approved product truth, and buyer evidence into five finished statics and one production-ready video concept, each tied to a distinct hypothesis, in 72 hours after complete inputs. Built to test against your control; never promised to beat it.

Do not lead with compliance. Claim discipline is a reason the work survives review; it is not the primary job the buyer is purchasing.

## Next-problem audit

The first post-purchase problem concerns the creative test itself:

> Who will produce, approve, launch, compare, and interpret these tests? What happens next when the result returns?

The current pilot partially absorbs this through production specifications, approval ownership, the test map, and one correction memo. The next rung must absorb **returned learning and the second creative cycle**.

### Contextual editions, not core components

These additions should appear only when the buyer's situation earns them:

- **Launch Edition:** a fixed launch date and one priority SKU.
- **Reformulation Edition:** old and new proof/claim language must be reconciled.
- **Creator Brief Edition:** creators need explicit prohibited-language examples and reviewer routing.
- **Claim-Review Recovery Edition:** the existing review process returned the message so thin that no useful test remains.
- **Multi-SKU Edition:** one learning must be translated carefully across products rather than copied wholesale.

Do not put every edition into the opening stack. The buyer should see the steak, not the truffle-butter inventory.

### Services removed from this ladder

Ghostwriting, SEO/AEO/GEO, Source-to-Search Trust Layer work, and general offer architecture are **not** downstream Control-Beater rungs. Preserve them as separate capabilities or separate buyer-job ladders. They may be quoted when a real client problem calls for them; they should not appear as the inevitable ascension path from paid-social creative testing.

### Capture rule

For every qualified exposure, record the verbatim response as one of:

- objection: explicit reason for `NO`;
- delay: explicit reason for `LATER`;
- disqualification: buyer or input does not fit;
- acceptance signal: asks about fit, scope, price, payment, or next step.

Do not add a new core component from one comment. Run `/jh-objection-mine` after ten substantive qualified conversations; use frequency to decide core versus edition.

## Claim-safety boundary

The service may promise controllable actions and outputs: exact deliverables, conditional turnaround, editorial claim classification, source tracing, production specifications, and the bounded make-right.

It may not promise:

- legal approval or certification;
- guaranteed Meta or platform acceptance;
- a control win, ROAS, CPA, CTR, revenue, conversion, or health outcome;
- that ingredient evidence proves the finished product produces the same result;
- that a disclaimer cures a misleading express or implied main impression.

The brand supplies product-specific substantiation and final approval ownership. Farrice may classify, question, narrow, and rewrite; the brand's qualified reviewer approves the final claim and net impression.
