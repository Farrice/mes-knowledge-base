# Character Bibles: Three Leads
### Eric Roth Psychology Wallets × Michael Connelly 6-Layer Builds

> **Method**: Each character gets a full Roth Psychology Wallet (fanciful-real calibration, emotional architecture, voice fingerprint, literal wallet, transformation arc) PLUS a Connelly 6-Layer Build (name, outsider lens, telling detail, nod economy, ambient conflict, geography trigger).

---

## MARCUS REYES-GOLDSTEIN — *POTLUCK*

### Fanciful-Real Calibration
- **Fanciful**: A music teacher with zero psychology credentials becomes the most trusted therapist in South Central LA — using nothing but improv training and active listening.
- **Real**: A queer, mixed-race man who abandoned his comedy dream is terrified he'll also abandon the community that now depends on him.

### Identity Core

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Full name** | Marcus Alejandro Reyes-Goldstein |
| **Age** | 33 at series start |
| **Physical description** | Slight build, always slightly underdressed for his own authority. Carries himself like someone perpetually trying to take up less space — until he's in session, when he leans forward, opens his hands, and becomes twice his size. Wears the same three button-downs on rotation. |
| **Occupation** | Music teacher (officially). Unlicensed community therapist (actually). Failed stand-up comedian (secretly). |

### Emotional Architecture

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **What he loves** | (1) The specific sound of a classroom going quiet when a kid actually *gets* a song. (2) His abuela's voice singing "Cielito Lindo" while doing dishes — the Spanish she chose to sing in even though she spoke English. (3) The moment in improv when someone says something true by accident. |
| **What he fears** | Surface: being exposed as a fraud — the therapy secret coming out. Deeper: that he abandoned comedy not because he wasn't good enough, but because he was afraid of being *seen* — and that the therapy practice is just another way to hide behind other people's problems. |
| **Neuroses** | Rehearses conversations in the shower. Can't leave a room without checking that he said goodbye to everyone. Organizes sheet music alphabetically but his apartment is a disaster — control only exists at school. |
| **Anger triggers** | When someone dismisses a kid's problem as "not real." When his mother tries to set him up with "a nice boy" in front of people. When credential gatekeepers dismiss community knowledge. |
| **Giddiness** | When a parent who came in hostile leaves laughing. When a kid writes their first original song. When he improvises a response in a session and it *lands* — the comedian in him comes alive. |
| **Secret shame** | He didn't quit comedy because he failed. He quit because he got a callback for a Netflix special and ghosted, because performing his own story (queer, mixed, South Central) felt like selling it. |
| **Defining loss** | His grandfather (Jewish side) died during COVID, alone in a nursing home. Marcus couldn't visit. He became a therapist because no one was there for his grandfather, and he swore he'd be there for everyone else. |

### Voice Fingerprint

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Vocabulary level** | Streets-smart, culturally fluent across four traditions. Uses Spanish, Tagalog, Yiddish, and AAVE interchangeably depending on who he's talking to — the ultimate code-switcher. |
| **Speech rhythm** | Starts sentences slow, builds speed when excited, cuts himself off before the punchline — a comedian who's learned to stop performing. |
| **Verbal tics** | "No but actually though" (genuine emphasis). "I hear that" (therapist mode). "Wait — say that part again" (the improv training). |
| **Subtext default** | In sessions: says what the person needs to hear. In personal life: deflects everything with humor, never answers a direct question about himself. |
| **Silence behavior** | Fidgets with a guitar pick he keeps in his pocket. When truly uncomfortable, hums — always the same three bars of a song he won't finish. |

### The Literal Wallet
- A laminated fake "therapy license" his best friend made as a joke — which Marcus can't bring himself to throw away
- A crumpled set list from his last open mic (3 years ago)
- A picture of his grandfather wearing a yarmulke at his quinceañera
- A Psychology Today article titled "When Peer Support Becomes Practice" — highlighted, annotated, dog-eared

### Connelly 6-Layer Build

| Layer | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Name** | Marcus Alejandro Reyes-Goldstein — every name is a cultural claim, and together they sound like an identity crisis set to music |
| **Outsider lens** | Insider access to four communities, outsider status in all of them. Sees every group's blind spots because he's never fully comfortable in any one |
| **Telling detail** | He keeps the classroom temperature at exactly 68° — the temperature his grandfather kept the house. It's the only environment he controls completely |
| **Nod economy** | When parents arrive for "therapy," Marcus gives a small nod and says "I saved you the good chair" — never acknowledges what they're there for explicitly |
| **Ambient conflict** | The copy machine in the teacher's lounge has a "Dr. G's office hours" flyer taped to it that Marcus didn't put there and can't remove without raising questions |
| **Geography trigger** | The supply closet where Marcus stores music stands — it's also where he hides to call his grandmother when sessions get too heavy. The closet smells like old paper and Pine-Sol, and it's become his confessional. |

### Transformation Arc
**STATE A**: A man hiding from his own ambition behind other people's problems.
**STATE B**: A man who chooses to earn the authority he's been borrowing.

1. **Inciting shift**: A parent calls Marcus "the only person who ever really listened to me" — and Marcus realizes he's never let anyone listen to *him*
2. **False solution**: Marcus starts reading psychology textbooks, trying to earn credentials retroactively — treating the symptom, not the wound
3. **Mirror moment**: A session with a kid reveals that Marcus uses the same avoidance patterns he diagnoses in his "clients" — the kid says, "You always ask how we feel but you never say how YOU feel"
4. **Commitment**: Marcus signs up for an actual counseling program — part-time, nights, while still teaching — not to cover his tracks, but because the work matters enough to do it right
5. **Integration**: Marcus tells the first parent the truth. She says, "I knew. I didn't care. You helped."

### Voice Isolation Test

**Ordering food**: "Can I get the — actually, you know what, what do *you* think is good here? No but actually though. What do you order when nobody's watching?"

**Describing someone he loves**: "My grandpa used to wear a yarmulke to my tía's house for Christmas. Just walked in. Didn't explain. Nobody asked him to explain once. I think about that every day — having that kind of... quiet certainty about who you are."

**Receiving bad news**: *[Long pause. Fidgets with the guitar pick in his pocket. Hums three bars of something. Then:]* "Okay. Okay, so what do we — I hear that. I do. I hear that."

## DANIEL BANDAY — *DAD'S PLUS-ONE*

### Fanciful-Real Calibration
- **Fanciful**: The biggest movie star on the planet can't figure out the carpool app.
- **Real**: A man who confused providing with parenting, and a daughter who loves him too much to tell him how much that hurt.

### Identity Core

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Full name** | Daniel Alejandro Banday |
| **Age** | 36 at series start |
| **Physical description** | Devastatingly handsome in the way that makes normal men hostile and normal women stupid. Dresses in $400 plain white t-shirts and looks like a cologne ad even at 6 AM school drop-off. But his body language at school is *wrong* — he stands like he's waiting for someone to tell him where to go. On set: magnetic. At school: lost. |
| **Occupation** | A-list actor. Currently between projects ("I'm being selective") which is code for "my divorce is eating my life." |

### Emotional Architecture

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **What he loves** | (1) The specific weight of Cleo's head on his shoulder when she falls asleep watching movies. (2) The feeling right before a take when the director says "action" and the world narrows to one task. (3) His mother's lumpia — the one thing from his Filipino side he never lets catering replicate because "they always get it wrong." |
| **What he fears** | Surface: that Cleo will see him the way the tabloids see his divorce — as a failure. Deeper: that he's only good at one thing (performing) and that everything else in his life — his marriage, his parenting, his friendships — was always handled by someone else, and he never noticed. |
| **Neuroses** | Checks his phone for TMZ alerts before checking his texts. Can't enter any room without scanning for cameras. Rehearses "casual" — practices sounding unrehearsed in the mirror before school events. |
| **Anger triggers** | When someone treats Cleo differently because of who he is. When his ex-wife says "I told you" about logistical failures. When someone assumes he's a good dad because he's a good actor. |
| **Giddiness** | When Cleo laughs at something he says — not his movie-star humor, but the actual stupid dad jokes nobody else hears. When he does one small domestic thing right (packed a lunch that Cleo actually ate) and Cleo gives him a genuine look of surprise. |
| **Secret shame** | He once missed Cleo's first day of kindergarten for a press junket. Rachel covered for him. Cleo still doesn't know. Daniel thinks about it every single drop-off. |
| **Defining loss** | His marriage — not because he loved Rachel romantically (they'd been performing for years), but because losing Rachel meant losing the infrastructure that made him look like a whole person. Without her, he's a movie star who can't make mac and cheese. |

### Voice Fingerprint

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Vocabulary level** | Surprisingly street when relaxed (South Central comes back). In public, code-switches to "press junket Daniel" — articulate, self-deprecating, charming. The gap between the two is where the comedy lives. |
| **Speech rhythm** | Movie-star cadence: measured, designed for impact, with pauses engineered for laughs. But when panicked (school situations), the cadence breaks — words pile up, he over-explains, he sounds like a man who's never had to explain himself. |
| **Verbal tics** | "No, totally" (agreeing to things he doesn't understand). "I got this — I absolutely got this" (he does not got this). "Can we circle back on that?" (using meeting language at the parent sign-up table). |
| **Subtext default** | Performs competence even when failing. Won't ask for help until the building is on fire. Then overcompensates by thanking everyone too much. |
| **Silence behavior** | When overwhelmed, puts on sunglasses indoors — a literal mask. Cleo has learned to take them off his face when he does this. |

### The Literal Wallet
- A call sheet from his last film — he hasn't cleaned out his wallet since production wrapped
- Cleo's school picture from last year (Rachel gave it to him; he didn't know picture day happened)
- A business card from a family therapist Rachel recommended — creased but never called
- A handwritten note from Cleo that says "you're my favorite (don't tell mom)" in crayon

### Connelly 6-Layer Build

| Layer | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Name** | Daniel Banday — sounds like a movie star, which is the point. At school, the other parents call him "Cleo's dad" and he finds it disorienting — no one's called him by his role instead of his name since before he was famous |
| **Outsider lens** | Insider in Hollywood, outsider in parenthood. Sees the school parent economy with the bewildered eyes of someone who's used to a world where assistants handle logistics |
| **Telling detail** | Daniel's car has two child seats — he bought a backup because he couldn't figure out how to transfer the one Rachel had. Both are installed wrong. |
| **Nod economy** | When Cleo fixes something for him (a form, a schedule, a social situation), Daniel gives her a thumbs-up that started ironic and has become genuine gratitude |
| **Ambient conflict** | The other dads at school simultaneously want to be him and resent him. They invite him to poker night hoping for industry connections. He goes hoping they'll teach him about school fundraisers. Both sides are disappointed. |
| **Geography trigger** | The school parking lot — Daniel parks at the far end, partially to avoid paparazzi, partially because the long walk gives him time to rehearse what a "normal dad" would say. Cleo times the walk. It's getting shorter. |

### Transformation Arc
**STATE A**: A man who believes being famous means being successful at everything.
**STATE B**: A man who discovers that the performance nobody claps for — showing up, packing the lunch, waiting in the carpool line — is the only role that matters.

1. **Inciting shift**: Daniel arrives at Back-to-School Night and doesn't know what grade Cleo is in
2. **False solution**: Daniel hires a "parent consultant" (yes, this is a real thing in LA) to manage his school obligations — outsourcing parenting to an expert
3. **Mirror moment**: Cleo tells Daniel she lied to her friends about what he does for a living because "they only want to be my friend because of you"
4. **Commitment**: Daniel fires the consultant and starts showing up without a plan — raw, terrible, embarrassing, and present
5. **Integration**: Daniel packs Cleo's lunch for the first time. It's wrong. She eats the whole thing.

### Voice Isolation Test

**Ordering food**: "Can I get the — actually, what's good here? I usually have my assistant order, which I realize sounds insane when I say it out loud. No, I'm serious. What would *you* get? I'm being selective. I'm between meals."

**Describing someone he loves**: "Cleo does this thing where she puts her hand on my arm when I'm being an idiot. She doesn't say anything. Just... *[touches his own arm]*. And I know she learned that from Rachel, because Rachel used to do the exact same thing. And the part that kills me is — Rachel did it because she loved me, and Cleo does it because she's *managing* me."

**Receiving bad news**: "Okay. No, totally. I'm — yeah. I got this. I absolutely — *[puts sunglasses on]*. Sorry. I got this. Can we circle back?"

## JOSH MARIN — *GUEST STAR*

### Fanciful-Real Calibration
- **Fanciful**: A man tries to sabotage his career and the harder he fails, the more famous he becomes — but always as the guest, never the lead.
- **Real**: A man who's been performing so long he's forgotten whether he's acting or living, and a career that gave him everything except the ability to be seen.

### Identity Core

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Full name** | Joshua Reyes Marin |
| **Age** | 37 at series start |
| **Physical description** | The face everybody recognizes and nobody can name. Handsome in a way that casting directors call "non-threatening leading man energy" which means: never the lead. Always the best friend, the partner, the guy who dies in act two. Dresses slightly too well for someone nobody's heard of — his wardrobe is his résumé. |
| **Occupation** | Professional guest star. 47 shows, 0 leads. His agents describe him as "incredibly versatile," which is industry code for "we don't know what to do with him." |

### Emotional Architecture

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **What he loves** | (1) The moment between "quiet on set" and "action" — the last two seconds where he exists as himself before becoming someone else. (2) Craft services coffee — not because it's good, but because it means he's on a set, which means he exists. (3) His mother's face when her friends recognize him on TV — the only audience that's ever made him feel like a star. |
| **What he fears** | Surface: aging out of "that guy" range without ever becoming "the guy." Deeper: that he's spent 15 years being other people and has no idea who he is when the camera isn't running. |
| **Neuroses** | Watches himself in every show he's on — not for vanity, but to check if he was good. Keeps a running mental list of every actor who was less talented than him and became a star. Can't not perform — even ordering coffee becomes a bit. |
| **Anger triggers** | When someone calls him by a character name instead of his name. When a lead actor gets praise for work Josh made possible. When someone says "you look familiar" and can't place him. |
| **Giddiness** | When he nails an improv that makes the whole crew laugh. When a director says "do whatever you want with this" — four seconds of creative freedom. When a kid recognizes him from a show and knows his character's name (even though they never know his). |
| **Secret shame** | He turned down a lead role 8 years ago because he was terrified of carrying a show. He's told everyone the role "wasn't right for him." The show became a hit. The other actor won an Emmy. |
| **Defining loss** | His college roommate — equally talented, equally driven — quit acting at 25 to become a teacher. He now has a family, a house, a life with a name. Josh has a résumé with 47 names that aren't his. |

### Voice Fingerprint

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Vocabulary level** | Performer-sharp — always in rhythm, always landing a beat. Uses industry jargon casually but also catches himself and translates, because he spends half his life around non-industry people who matter more to him than he admits. |
| **Speech rhythm** | Stand-up comedian cadence — setups and punchlines even in casual conversation. But when genuinely vulnerable, the rhythm breaks and he speaks in fragments, like a man who didn't rehearse this part. |
| **Verbal tics** | "Here's the thing" (performative wisdom). "I've done 47 shows" (credential drop when insecure). "Am I wrong?" (needs external validation, always). |
| **Subtext default** | Everything is a performance. His "real" conversations are indistinguishable from his "acting" — the show's central question is whether there's a difference. |
| **Silence behavior** | Fills it. Josh doesn't do silence. When forced into it — in a doctor's waiting room, in an awkward pause — he narrates what's happening as if he's in a scene. "This is the part where I say something profound. I don't have it." |

### The Literal Wallet
- 14 call sheets from 14 different shows — he keeps them all, organized chronologically, like a scrapbook of a career nobody else remembers
- A folded printout of a IMDb page where someone in the comments wrote: "This guy is in EVERYTHING. What's his name?"
- His SAG card, laminated, carried daily — the only credential that proves he exists
- A Post-it note from a showrunner that says "You were the best part of this show" — the show was cancelled after one season

### Connelly 6-Layer Build

| Layer | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Name** | Josh Marin — a name designed to be hard to remember, which is the cruelest detail of all. When he's introduced at parties, people hear "Josh" and stop listening before "Marin." |
| **Outsider lens** | Industry insider, fame outsider. He's been on every set in Hollywood and knows every secret — who's difficult, who's kind, who's sleeping with whom — but none of that knowledge helps because he's invisible |
| **Telling detail** | Josh keeps a suit bag in his car at all times — pressed, ready, camera-ready. Not because he has auditions, but because he refuses to be seen looking like he's given up |
| **Nod economy** | When crew members recognize him from previous shows, Josh gives them a small salute — two fingers to the temple. It's their shared acknowledgment that the real work of TV is done by the people nobody sees. Including him. |
| **Ambient conflict** | His apartment wall has framed headshots from every decade of his career — 20s, 30s, soon 40s. Each one says "JOSH MARIN" in a different font. The fonts got smaller as the headshots got bigger. |
| **Geography trigger** | Sound Stage 12 at Warner Bros — the first stage he ever worked on, as a day player on a procedural that was cancelled mid-season. He books himself jobs on that lot whenever possible. He tells himself it's convenient. It's actually a pilgrimage. |

### Transformation Arc
**STATE A**: A man who defines himself by the career that refuses to define him.
**STATE B**: A man who discovers that being seen isn't about the screen — it's about being known by the people who matter off-camera.

1. **Inciting shift**: Josh's mother introduces him at a family dinner and says, "This is my son Josh, he's on television" — and nobody at the table can name a show
2. **False solution**: The sabotage campaign — Josh tries to force the industry to promote him by making himself impossible to ignore
3. **Mirror moment**: A young actor on set asks Josh for career advice and Josh realizes everything he'd say is a warning, not a recommendation
4. **Commitment**: Josh stops trying to be famous and starts trying to be *specific* — writing his own material for the first time, creating the thing no one gave him permission to create
5. **Integration**: Josh walks past Sound Stage 12 and doesn't stop. He's working on his own project now. On a different lot. Under his own name.

### Voice Isolation Test

**Ordering food**: "I'll have the — you know what, surprise me. I'm very versatile. I once played a guy who only ate raw food for six episodes. The show got cancelled but I lost eleven pounds. Anyway. Chef's choice. I trust you. I've trusted worse."

**Describing someone he loves**: "My mom watches every show I'm on. Every single one. Even the procedurals where I'm the victim — dead by the first commercial break. She records them. She shows her friends. Her friends say 'oh, your son!' and my mom just *lights up*. She doesn't care that nobody knows my name. She knows it. She says it all the time."

**Receiving bad news**: "Here's the thing — *[performative pause, like he's about to do a bit]*. No, actually, I don't have a thing. I don't have a line for this. *[Silence. For the first time, doesn't fill it.]*"

---

*Three characters, each worth caring about. Each living the "Only Josh" principle from the inside.*
