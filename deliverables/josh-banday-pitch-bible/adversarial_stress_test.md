# Adversarial Stress-Test: Three Josh Banday Show Concepts
### Dan Koe Concerns Methodology × Buyer Psychology

> **Purpose**: Surface every objection a network buyer, studio exec, or fellowship reader would raise — then arm Josh with counter-arguments before he walks into the room.

---

## CONCEPT 1: *POTLUCK*

### Attack Vector 1: "This is just Abbott Elementary"
**The Objection**: "We already have Abbott. How is this different enough to justify buying a second school-set comedy?"

**The Counter**: Abbott is a workplace comedy that happens to be in a school. *Potluck* is a **community comedy** that happens to include a school. Marcus's classroom is the setting, not the subject. The show's engine is the neighborhood — the overlapping families, the cultural friction, the block party politics. Abbott never leaves the building. Potluck barely stays in it.

**The Killer Line**: "Abbott is a love letter to teachers. Potluck is a love letter to the block they go home to."

---

### Attack Vector 2: "The therapy secret feels like a legal liability for us"
**The Objection**: "Are we glorifying unlicensed therapy? Will there be blowback from the mental health community?"

**The Counter**: The show doesn't glorify unlicensed practice — it **interrogates it**. Marcus is terrified. The evidence trail escalates every episode. The question the show asks — *if it works, does the credential matter?* — is one of the most debated questions in modern mental health (life coaches vs. therapists, peer counseling, community health workers). This isn't *Breaking Bad* celebrating crime; it's *Nurse Jackie* asking where the line is between helping and harm. The credentialism debate IS the zeitgeist.

**The Killer Line**: "The show doesn't take a side. The show takes the question seriously. That's what makes it prestige."

---

### Attack Vector 3: "A music teacher protagonist feels limited"
**The Objection**: "Music teacher is a narrow world. Where's the story engine after season 1?"

**The Counter**: Marcus isn't a music teacher show — he's a **community center operator who technically teaches music**. The engine is the neighborhood, which is inexhaustible. Season 1 is Marcus building his accidental practice. Season 2 is Marcus trying to legitimize it (night classes, online certification). Season 3 is the exposure — does the community defend him or abandon him? Season 4 is the rebuild. The therapy secret alone provides 4 seasons of escalation.

**The Killer Line**: "The music classroom is the Cheers bar. It's where everyone goes. But the stories come from the neighborhood outside."

---

### Attack Vector 4: "Queer protagonist in a community show — is there audience?"
**The Objection**: "Queer-led comedies have a mixed commercial track record. Is there a broad enough audience?"

**The Counter**: Marcus's queerness isn't the premise — it's one ingredient in a five-dimensional identity (Black, Filipino, Mexican, Jewish, queer). The show is primarily a community comedy; Marcus's sexuality is treated the way it's treated in real South Central — it's known, it's occasionally complicated, it's not the headline. *Somebody Somewhere* proved that a queer protagonist in a heartland setting works when the queerness is naturalized, not spotlighted. This show does the same in South Central.

**The Killer Line**: "Marcus is queer the same way your neighbor is queer — you know, it comes up, and also 47 other things are happening."

---

### Attack Vector 5: "Josh Banday as lead — is he bankable enough?"
**The Objection**: "He's a series regular, not a headliner. Can he open a show?"

**The Counter**: This is the exact moment in Josh's career where the answer becomes yes. He has 5 seasons of series regular experience (*Upload*, *Not Dead Yet*). He has Second City and Groundlings training. He's the creator, writer, AND star — which means the show is a de-risked package: you're not buying a script and hoping to cast it. You're buying a finished creative entity. Quinta Brunson wasn't bankable before *Abbott*. Issa Rae wasn't bankable before *Insecure*. The show *makes* the star bankable — and Josh has more on-set experience than either of them had at the same career stage.

**The Killer Line**: "You're not betting on an unknown. You're getting a four-season series regular who also writes and produces. Quinta Brunson had less on her résumé when she pitched Abbott."

## CONCEPT 2: *DAD'S PLUS-ONE*

### Attack Vector 1: "We've seen the hot divorced dad show"
**The Objection**: "Single dad, attractive, figuring it out — didn't we already do this with *About a Boy*, *My Two Dads*, every rom-com?"

**The Counter**: None of those shows had the **juxtaposition** this has. Daniel isn't a regular hot guy — he's an A-list movie star with 14 million Instagram followers who literally cannot figure out the carpool app. The comedy isn't "handsome guy is bad at parenting" — it's "the most powerful man in any room he enters is the least powerful person at his daughter's school." That inversion has never been done. The closest comp is *The Game Plan*, but that's a kids' movie. This is a half-hour prestige comedy about how fame is inversely correlated with real-world competence.

**The Killer Line**: "Every divorced-dad show makes the dad a schlub. This one makes the dad the biggest movie star on Earth — and that makes him *worse* at parenting, not better."

---

### Attack Vector 2: "Is it aspirational or relatable? It can't be both."
**The Objection**: "If he's A-list, the audience can't relate. If he's relatable, he's not A-list. Pick a lane."

**The Counter**: This IS both, and that's the engine. The audience aspires to his fame and relates to his incompetence. Every parent has felt out of their depth at school. Every parent has wanted to text their ex and say "how do I do this?" The A-list layer doesn't block relatability — it *amplifies* the comedy of the incompetence. It's funnier that a guy who negotiates $20M deals can't negotiate the snack sign-up sheet. *Schitt's Creek* proved that aspirational-meets-incompetent is a goldmine — rich people who can't do normal things. This is the same engine, but with a father-daughter heart underneath.

**The Killer Line**: "It's Schitt's Creek at drop-off. You envy his life, you pity his parenting."

---

### Attack Vector 3: "The kid being smarter than the parent — isn't that a trope?"
**The Objection**: "The precocious kid trope is exhausted. Audiences see it coming."

**The Counter**: Cleo isn't precocious — she's **competent out of necessity**. She's not smarter than Daniel because she's a genius; she's smarter because her mom trained her and her dad never showed up for the operational side. Cleo's competence is tinged with resentment: she didn't ask to run her father's life. The comedy isn't "adorable kid outsmarts dad" — it's "exhausted kid manages a celebrity who should be managing her." That frustration makes Cleo a real character, not a trope.

**The Killer Line**: "Cleo isn't a precocious kid. She's an unpaid assistant who happens to be nine."

---

### Attack Vector 4: "Celebrity satire — are we competing with The Studio?"
**The Objection**: "Apple just launched The Studio. FX has Always Sunny (Hollywood-adjacent). Is the Hollywood satire space overcrowded?"

**The Counter**: *Dad's Plus-One* isn't a Hollywood satire — it's a **parenting comedy where the parent happens to be famous**. The show spends 80% of its time in the parent economy: school, playdates, birthday parties, drop-off. Daniel's fame is the complication, not the subject. The Studio is about the industry. This show is about the family. They don't compete — they complement.

**The Killer Line**: "The show isn't set in Hollywood. It's set at a school in the Valley. Daniel goes home to Hollywood — the show stays at the pickup line."

---

### Attack Vector 5: "Where's the serialized arc beyond 'dad gets better at parenting'?"
**The Objection**: "If he just gradually improves, that's a sitcom that runs out of gas."

**The Counter**: The serialized arc isn't "Daniel gets better." It's "Daniel has to choose between his career and his daughter — and keeps trying to have both." Season 1: Daniel adjusts. Season 2: Daniel gets a career-defining role that requires 4 months on location — does he leave Cleo? Season 3: Cleo is old enough to start her own identity crisis (does she want to be famous? does she resent her dad's fame?). Season 4: Rachel wants full custody back — now Daniel has to prove in court that he's a real parent. The fame isn't just comedy — it's the obstacle that makes every parenting decision a crisis.

**The Killer Line**: "Every episode is funny. Every season is a choice between the career that made him and the kid who needs him."

## CONCEPT 3: *GUEST STAR*

### Attack Vector 1: "Inside-Hollywood shows don't perform"
**The Objection**: "Episodes flopped. The Comeback was a cult show. Hollywood-set comedies are niche."

**The Counter**: *Episodes* failed because it was about two British writers — nobody cared. *The Comeback* was ahead of its time by a decade. Since then: *Atlanta* (4 Emmys, massive cultural impact), *Hacks* (Emmy winner, 3 seasons), *The Studio* (Apple's flagship comedy), *Barry* (HBO's biggest comedy hit of the 2020s). The Hollywood-set comedy isn't niche anymore — it's one of the hottest lanes in television. But all of those shows observe Hollywood from the top. *Guest Star* observes it from the **bottom** — the day players, the recurring guests, the "that guy" actors who make the industry run but never get the credit. That's a fresh perspective no one has done.

**The Killer Line**: "Atlanta is Hollywood from the outside. The Studio is Hollywood from the top. Guest Star is Hollywood from the bottom. Nobody's told that story."

---

### Attack Vector 2: "The sabotage-backfires engine seems unsustainable"
**The Objection**: "How many times can his sabotage accidentally work before it becomes repetitive?"

**The Counter**: The sabotage engine is the Season 1 hook, not the series engine. Season 1: Josh tries to fail, keeps succeeding. Season 2: Josh gets what he wanted (real fame) and discovers it's worse — now he's too famous to be a guest star and not skilled enough to carry a lead. Season 3: Josh finds his actual calling (directing? producing? writing?) and has to start over in a new lane. The sabotage is the inciting concept. The series is about a man discovering what he actually wants versus what he thought he wanted.

**The Killer Line**: "Season 1 is about trying to fail. Season 2 is about succeeding and wishing you hadn't. Season 3 is about finding the thing that was right the whole time."

---

### Attack Vector 3: "The anthology-within-a-series structure sounds expensive"
**The Objection**: "A new genre parody every episode? That's a production nightmare."

**The Counter**: It's actually production-efficient. Each episode's "show-within-a-show" only needs 30% of screen time — the rest is Josh's real life (his apartment, his agent's office, his family). The genre parodies can be done with minimal sets and maximum writing: a few scenes on a fake procedural set, a table read in a conference room, a reality show stage that's just a platform with lights. *Barry* did this with plays. *Hacks* does it with standup sets. The production cost is in the writing, not the building.

**The Killer Line**: "It's not an anthology. It's one show with a rotating B-story that gives you clip content for free."

---

### Attack Vector 4: "Is this too meta? Too inside-baseball?"
**The Objection**: "Regular audiences won't get the industry references."

**The Counter**: The audience doesn't need to know Hollywood to understand "a guy who's invisible at work tries to make himself seen." That's a universal experience. The Hollywood setting is the wrapping paper — the gift is the identity crisis. *Office Space* worked because everyone has a job, not because everyone works at Initech. *Guest Star* works because everyone has felt overlooked, undervalued, or defined by a role they've outgrown.

**The Killer Line**: "You don't need to know Hollywood to know what it feels like when everyone recognizes your face but nobody knows your name."

---

### Attack Vector 5: "Josh Banday essentially playing himself — is that a limitation?"
**The Objection**: "If the character is transparently Josh, does it limit the show's scope?"

**The Counter**: Issa Rae played Issa Dee. Larry David plays Larry David. Quinta Brunson plays a version of herself. Pamela Adlon played a version of herself. The "creator plays a fictionalized self" model is the **most proven format in prestige comedy** right now. It's not a limitation — it's the format audiences and buyers trust most. And Josh's real résumé (series regular on Upload, guest spots across TV, improv training) gives the character instant authenticity that a purely fictional character would have to earn.

**The Killer Line**: "The best comedies of the last decade are all someone playing themselves. Josh has the résumé to make this character undeniable."
