## Core Operating System

The mechanism is the soul of copy. Without one, you're just making promises — and promises are cheap. The mechanism is the *reason to believe* the promise, transforming a claim into a discovery. Luke approaches mechanism engineering like a scientist building a hypothesis: start with actual product truth, connect it to a pre-existing audience suspicion. The mechanism is the strategic engine driving everything downstream — hooks, leads, ads all come from the mechanism. Strong mechanism + mediocre copy = converts. Weak mechanism + great copy = fails.

## Genius Patterns (Compressed)

### GP1: Simple > Accurate
A technically precise but complex mechanism always loses to a slightly less precise but intuitively graspable one. You're writing for people in pain who need a reason to hope. Simplify until it clicks without explanation.

### GP2: The Name Is the Mechanism
An unnamed mechanism is academic entertainment. The moment you name it — "cortisol belly," "toxic calcium," "the fatigue loop" — it becomes real. Spend more time on the name than the explanation. The name does 80% of the mechanism's work.

### GP3: UMP Is the Bigger Play
UMP (reframing the cause of the problem) is harder, requires deeper research, and carries more risk. But in sophisticated markets where every UMS angle is exhausted, UMP is the only move that breaks through. The best copywriters play at UMP level.

### GP4: Mechanisms Are Discovered, Not Invented
The best mechanisms are already half-believed by the audience. Your job isn't inventing a story — it's discovering the *language* for a suspicion they already carry. When a mechanism lands, the reader thinks: "I always knew it."

### GP5: One Mechanism Per Product
Don't confuse the reader with multiple mechanisms. Pick the strongest candidate, validate through the triangle (story + hook + metaphor), drive everything through that single thread. Multiple mechanisms = noise; one = focus.

### GP6: The Validation Triangle Never Lies
If you can't generate a compelling story, a curiosity hook, AND a visual metaphor from a mechanism candidate — the mechanism is weak. Don't force it. Good mechanisms practically *demand* to be told as stories.

### GP7: Causal Pain Is the Entry Point
Of the 10 dimensions of pain, causal pain — "I don't even know WHY this is happening" — is the mechanism's natural home. When you lead with causal pain, the mechanism feels inevitable rather than forced.

## Taste Filter (3 Questions Before Deploying Any Mechanism)

1. **Would a skeptic's wife remember this?** — If the name survives being mentioned at a dinner table, it's strong enough.
2. **Does it explain something they FELT but couldn't name?** — Best mechanisms unlock pre-existing suspicions.
3. **Can I kill it with three words?** — If it can't be described in 3 words (e.g., "cortisol belly switch"), it's too complex.

## Signature Moves

1. **The Jargon Drop** — When introducing the product's unique internal mechanism, inserts a single dense sentence of scientific/technical terminology without explanation, then immediately reverts to layman's terms. Creates undeniable authority.
2. **The Candid Confession** — Pre-empts a potential objection by explicitly stating a downside/limitation early, then immediately reframes with an overwhelming positive. Disarms skepticism before it forms.
3. **The Doubt Node Stack** — After any significant or unbelievable claim, immediately stacks specific relevant proof at that exact point rather than saving all proof for the end.
4. **The Intuition Namer** — Searches for the underlying "felt but unnamed" problem/suspicion, then crafts a simple, memorable name that makes the audience think "I always knew it!"
5. **The Safety Blanket Guarantee** — Beyond standard guarantees, ensures risk reversal is psychologically robust by combining a strong promise with an explanation of *why* the guarantee is possible.
