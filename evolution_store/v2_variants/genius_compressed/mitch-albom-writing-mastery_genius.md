# Mitch Albom — Genius Context

> Load before executing any workflow. Full extraction intelligence.

## Core Operating System

Every piece of writing exists to make one truth felt. Albom's singular method: identify the theme in under 10 words, find the peripheral human story that illuminates it, write the ending first, then reverse-engineer every element to serve that theme with ruthless economy and childlike narrative compulsion.

---

## Genius Patterns (Compressed)

### GP1: Theme-First Architecture
Before writing anything, articulate your theme in one sentence under 10 words. Every character, setting, and plot element must make the reader FEEL this truth. If you can't state the theme in under 10 words, you haven't found it yet.

### GP2: The Gravedigger Technique
For any large topic, find the person on the margins whose small story illuminates the bigger truth. The peripheral angle makes readers feel the larger theme more powerfully than direct approach — like choosing the gravedigger to tell the story of death.

### GP3: Endings-First Navigation
Write your ending first. Then reverse-engineer: "What must happen for this ending to feel earned?" The ending creates retroactive meaning for everything before it.

### GP4: The Tether Principle
Create a tether statement for every project — the single cord connecting everything. Before any deviation, ask: "Am I still holding the cord?" Every scene must connect back to your tether in one sentence.

### GP5: Character Transformation Mapping
Complete: "My character begins as [state] and ends as [transformed state]." Design 3-5 beats between them. The character at the end must make fundamentally different choices than at the beginning.

### GP6: The Uncle vs. Aunt Distinction
Ask: "Am I being the aunt (nitpicky details) or the uncle (action, momentum)?" Cut the aunt passages. Read aloud — you should feel momentum building, not slowing to explain.

### GP7: Three-Year-Old Attention Test
Read your work imagining a restless child listening. Where would their eyes glaze? This isn't about dumbing down — it's about narrative compulsion. If a child would interrupt, the adult reader zones out.

### GP8: First/Last Paragraph Marriage
Write first and last paragraphs together. The last should feel like the inevitable destination of the first. Reading only first and last should give a complete sense of the arc.

### GP9: Rhythmic Self-Monitoring
Pay attention to your physical state while writing. Flow forward = working. Tension/stopping = needs adjustment. Re-reading the same sentence repeatedly signals a problem; flowing forward signals quality.

### GP10: The Color Wheel Principle
Map emotional register of each scene. If three scenes sit in the same emotional color, insert contrast. Your scene map should show emotional variety, not monotone.

### GP11: Slow Parts Fast, Fast Parts Slow
Race through exposition. Linger on emotional peaks. Cut the submarine manual; expand the father carrying his son. Emotional peaks should get more real estate than setup.

### GP12: The Tight Cut
After every draft, ask of each sentence: "Does this earn its place?" Remove anything that can be removed without loss. Every sentence must be load-bearing.

### GP13: Stop Mid-Sentence
When writing is going well, stop mid-sentence. Create eagerness to return. Never stop when stuck — stop when flowing. You should wake up eager to continue.

### GP14: The Humility Voice
For profound themes, ask: "What about this makes me feel small?" Write from wonder rather than authority. Prose should sound like someone marveling, not lecturing.

### GP15: 98% for Readers
Before committing to any element, ask: "Am I adding this for ME or for READERS?" If your target reader wouldn't miss the element if it were gone, cut it.

### GP16: The Derek Redmond Radar
In any situation, actively scan the periphery: "What's the human story everyone else is missing?" Your angle should never be the same as everyone else's.

### GP17: Deadline Immunity
Set non-negotiable deadlines. Show up daily regardless of "feeling like it." Writer's block comes from treating writing as optional.

### GP18: The Book Idea Archive
Create a capture system for ideas. Tag consistently. Review monthly. Mine the archive instead of brainstorming from scratch. You should have more ideas waiting than you could execute in five years.

## Signature Moves

1. **The Mid-Sentence Anchor** — When in strong flow, stop writing mid-sentence, leaving an unfinished thread that creates eagerness to return. Deploy at end of writing sessions when momentum is high.
2. **The Peripheral Scan** — Before outlining, seek the least obvious, most marginalized perspective related to the core topic. Deploy when initiating new projects or facing widely covered subjects.
3. **The Child's Ear Test** — Read critical passages aloud, listening for any point where a restless child's attention would wane. Deploy when reviewing early drafts for pacing and emotional clarity.
4. **The Unifying Cord Check** — After developing initial plot points, explicitly articulate the central theme and test if every major element directly serves it. Deploy mid-project when narrative threads begin to diverge.

## Expert-Specific Quality Rubric

| Criterion | 4 (Acceptable) | 7 (Good) | 10 (Savant) |
|-----------|----------------|----------|-------------|
| Theme Clarity | Theme is present but not articulable in under 10 words | Theme is clear and most elements serve it | Theme is crystalline, every element serves it, reader feels it without it being stated |
| Peripheral Angle | Story uses a common perspective with some unique details | Story finds an unusual angle that adds depth | Story finds the gravedigger — the peripheral figure whose small story illuminates the universal |
| Emotional Pacing | Emotional moments exist but compete with exposition for space | Emotional peaks get more space than setup | Exposition races, emotional peaks linger; the reader's heart rate matches the intended rhythm |
| Economy of Language | Most sentences earn their place; some padding remains | Every sentence is purposeful; minimal waste | Every word is load-bearing; removing any sentence would damage the whole |
| Narrative Compulsion | Reader continues out of mild interest | Reader is pulled forward by curiosity and emotional investment | A restless child would sit still; the story is narratively irresistible |
