# The Two Funnels: Why AI Made Your Discovery Cheap and Your Trust Expensive
*Engine: /ce-write (Chase Hughes PCP × Roth + Cole + Dan Wang). Category word: "discount." Ask deferred + self-chosen.*

A client read her own work back last month, looked up, and asked who actually wrote it.

She runs content for three wellness brands. She didn't have a clean answer, and the pause told me more than any number on her dashboard. The trust had been thinning for months before that meeting, across forty posts that were 85% her and 15% nobody, that smooth, faintly-too-polished voice every brand now wears. Her content had gotten cheaper to make. That was the whole problem. When words cost almost nothing, sounding like everyone else costs more than it ever has.

Most founders in wellness read that situation as an efficiency upgrade. They saw the cost of a post drop to nothing and made more of them. What they were actually doing was selling something at a discount, and they had the price exactly backwards.

You are running two funnels now, and AI is doing the opposite thing to each.

At the top, discovery, the machines moved in. Someone asks ChatGPT which magnesium to take, and the answer gets written before they ever reach your site. A 5W study run from January to April this year put 3,800 supplement questions through ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google's AI answers. Four brands took more than 47% of the citations it tracked. AG1, reportedly the largest greens brand by revenue, came in third. What every winner shared was the same trait: they had published proof a machine could read.

This month gave us the cleaner version of the same lesson. On the 19th, 5W ran the same test on pharma. U.S. pharma spent an estimated eight billion dollars on direct-to-consumer ads in 2024, the biggest ad category in the country. Asked which brands to trust, the engines named Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk for reasons that had almost nothing to do with who spent the most. Eight billion dollars, and the budget didn't move the answer. Up here, a machine reads your substance, not your spend.

At the bottom, the moment someone trusts you with their body and their money, the physics flip. Trust got expensive. Your buyer arrives having read AI-written everything, and she can smell it by the second line. Ogilvy named the underlying shift in January and called it the authenticity recession: as scalable perfection floods the world, a visible human becomes the rare thing. The half of your funnel that punishes polish at the top now charges a premium for a face at the bottom.

You can hear the same note one level up, on this very platform. For about eighteen months the carousel was LinkedIn's cheat code, swipeable and easy to template, so everyone built them and then bought tools to build them faster. This June the people who track the algorithm started watching carousels slide while plain text posts that pick a real fight climbed past them. Nothing about the carousel got worse. It got common, and common is invisible. The feed is doing to formats exactly what AI is doing to content and the agencies, pricing down whatever a machine can mass-produce and pricing up whatever it can't. Digiday put the survivor in one word this year: taste, the part of the work that has to be earned one good call at a time, while 55 to 70% of agency hours get eaten by the execution and coordination the models now handle for free.

Here is where the discount gets paid. Founders watched content get cheap and bought more of it. Then, to save the hours, they quietly automated the one thing AI cannot fake: visible proof that a real person stands behind the claim. They poured effort into the half AI already flattened and sold off the half that was appreciating. The brands pulling ahead ran it backwards. They let the machines flood the top, where being legible is the whole game, and spent the saved hours making the bottom unmistakably human: a named coach who answers her own messages, a face on camera, a claim with somebody's actual reputation riding on it.

What that buyer is paying full price for, underneath all of it, is the oldest thing there is. She has read a hundred captions written by nobody, and she is starving to be seen by someone. A real person who has been where she's going. Automate the bottom of your funnel and you don't just lose a sale. You make your brand unseeable at the exact moment a human was reaching for one. The cheaper your content gets, the more your humanity is worth.

If you have spent any time inside this category, you already know your edge was never volume. It was the call you could make before the data confirmed it, the angle you could smell. A machine can copy the output of that taste forever and never once produce the source. That source is you, the one input no competitor can prompt. Stop racing the machine on speed it will always win, and put the part of you it can't reach exactly where the money changes hands.

Run the audit on your own brand this week, or a client's. It takes about twenty minutes and it ends an argument you've been having with yourself.

### The Funnel Bifurcation Audit

**Part 1. Top of funnel: can a machine read you?**
Open ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI mode. Paste your real category questions, one at a time:
- "What's the best [your category], and why?"
- "Is [your product type] worth it? Which brands are trusted?"
- "Compare the top [category] brands."
- "What does the research say about [your key ingredient or mechanism]?"

For each answer, mark: Do you appear at all? Is what it says accurate? Does it cite your real proof, or someone else's opinion of you?

*If you're absent, the problem is rarely the product. Your proof is trapped in a format a machine can't parse: a PDF, an image, a paragraph of brand voice. Publish your dosing, mechanism, and study data as clean, quotable text. Boring to a designer, catnip to a model.*

**Part 2. Bottom of funnel: can a stranger see a human?**
Walk your own buyer's path, first click to checkout. Count the places a stranger could prove a real person stands behind the claim:
- A named person, not "our team," answering questions
- A face on camera making the actual promise
- A practitioner with a reputation you could verify
- Specific testimonials, not "Amazing product!!"
- A claim a regulator could trace to one human

*Tally both halves. Low at the top means the machines doing the recommending can't see you. Low at the bottom means you get discovered and then disqualified at the door. Almost everyone is lopsided, and the lopsidedness is a strategy you've been running by accident.*

I spent eighteen years moving real bodies through real change, and the thing that moved them was never the most polished plan. It was the sense that a real person had them, a process they could feel working. Brands run on the same fuel. Legible enough for a machine to recommend at the top, unmistakably human at the moment someone decides to trust you.

You priced your humanity as the cheap part. Put your face on the next one, and price it as the scarce thing it became.
