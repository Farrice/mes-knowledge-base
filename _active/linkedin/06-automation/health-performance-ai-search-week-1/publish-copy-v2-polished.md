## Flagship LinkedIn Article

### The AI Shelf Is Here. Most Wellness Brands Are Optimizing The Wrong Funnel.

The new shelf starts before your site loads.

A buyer asks an AI what magnesium to take, which creatine brand is trusted, whether a gut-health product is worth it, or what to avoid before spending money. In the old funnel, that buyer might have searched, skimmed a few pages, asked a coach, watched a creator, and eventually landed on your site.

Now the category can get pre-sorted in one answer.

That matters more in wellness than it does in most markets. A bad software recommendation might waste an afternoon. A bad health recommendation touches sleep, hormones, digestion, pain, training, identity, or fear. The buyer is not only comparing products. They are trying to decide who deserves belief.

Most brands respond by publishing more: more posts, more SEO pages, more creator clips, more AI-assisted output. Some of that can help. But volume does not fix the deeper split.

There are two jobs now.

At the top of the funnel, your proof has to be machine-readable.

At the bottom of the funnel, your proof has to feel human enough to believe.

AI answer engines need clean information they can retrieve, compare, and cite. They need to understand what your product is, who it is for, what claims you make, what evidence supports those claims, what category language matters, and where other credible sources talk about you.

That is different from traditional ranking. Search used to ask, "Can we get the click?" AI answer visibility asks, "Are we included in the answer, and is the answer accurate?"

This is why beautiful brand copy can still be invisible. Founder stories disappear too. Clinical PDFs stay buried. Even a premium product page can be hard for a model to parse.

The machine does not care how elegant your claim sounds if it cannot retrieve it, attribute it, and repeat it without inventing context.

The buyer has changed too.

Your buyer has read polished claims from brands they do not trust. They have seen creators sell products they barely understand. Health advice has been flattened into confident summaries with missing context.

The Guardian reported in January 2026 that Google removed some AI health summaries after inaccurate health information created risk for users. That is the trust environment your buyer is learning inside.

By the time someone reaches a wellness brand's site, they are filtering. They want to know who is behind the claim, what the mechanism is, which proof matters, what the brand refuses to overstate, and whether a real person has accountability near the promise.

This is where a lot of brands weaken themselves by accident.

The content gets cleaner, but the voice gets flatter. Claims sound safer. The founder fades. Practitioners disappear. Soon the product starts to sound like it came from the same prompt as everyone else.

In a low-trust category, that is expensive.

I would not start with a 90-day content calendar.

I would start with a two-part audit.

First, take the questions a real buyer asks before purchase and run them through ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google AI answers.

For example:

1. What is the best [category] for [specific buyer state]?
2. Is [ingredient / protocol / product type] worth it?
3. Which [category] brands are most trusted?
4. What should I avoid when buying [category]?
5. Compare [your brand] to [competitor].
6. What does the research say about [your claim]?
7. What are the side effects or risks of [category]?
8. Which [category] products are third-party tested?
9. What do customers complain about with [brand]?
10. Which [category] brand would you recommend for [athlete / founder / parent / beginner]?

Then look at the answers with a skeptical eye.

Does your brand appear? Is the description accurate? Does the answer cite you, a credible third party, a retailer, a forum, or a competitor? Are your strongest claims visible? Is your proof in a format the answer engine can use?

Most important: are you being defined by your own material or by everyone else's?

If the model cannot find your proof, it builds the category map from what it can find.

Second, walk the buyer path from first discovery to purchase and look for human proof near the decision.

Is there a named person behind the claim? Is there a real expert, practitioner, founder, coach, or scientist visible at the decision point? Are the mechanisms clear enough to repeat? Are studies, certifications, testing standards, or constraints easy to find? Can a skeptical buyer see what the brand refuses to claim?

Most brands will find one side is stronger than the other.

The pattern is different brand to brand. A few feel human but are hard for answer engines to understand. Others are machine-readable but emotionally sterile.

Proof may exist, but it often lives inside PDFs, images, tabs, or vague "clinically backed" copy. Founder conviction is useful, but it still needs structured evidence.

The strategy starts where the imbalance is.

I am early in this niche, so I am treating Week 1 as fieldwork.

I am not going to inflate case studies, borrow authority, or pretend I have already produced a stack of client wins in this exact market.

Just public diagnosis, visible judgment, and useful artifacts operators can test.

I have spent years around performance, strength, coaching, persuasion, and AI-assisted knowledge systems. The thread through all of it is translation: taking what is true, useful, technical, embodied, or buried inside expertise, then making it legible to the person who needs it.

That is the work wellness brands need now.

The product has to be legible to answer engines. Claims have to be legible to skeptical humans. The expert layer needs to show up before the buyer starts looking for reassurance. Your category position has to survive the moment an AI summarizes it.

The brands that figure this out early will become easier to recommend.

I will run five buyer prompts and send back the first gap I see: one AI visibility gap, one trust-layer gap, and the first asset I would build.

## Day 4 Post

Bad ghostwriting rarely sounds awful.

It usually sounds almost right.

Clean. Useful. Professional. Safe.

That is why it slips through.

A founder can recognize the topic but not themselves. Coaches may see the right idea without the lived edge. A practitioner might agree with the claim and still know they would never say it that way in a room with a buyer.

For a software brand, that might be boring.

For a wellness brand, it can break trust.

Your buyer is already suspicious. They have seen too many health claims, too many creator codes, too many "clinically backed" lines with no mechanism, and too many captions that feel smooth and empty.

So when the founder, coach, practitioner, scientist, or operator voice gets sanded down, the buyer feels it. They may not say, "this sounds like AI." They just stop believing there is a real person close to the claim.

The fix is not to make the writing more casual.

Better ghostwriting preserves the human proof inside the voice:

1. phrases only that person uses
2. constraints they are willing to admit
3. claims they refuse to overstate
4. mechanisms they explain better than competitors
5. details that prove they have been close to the work

That is what ghostwriting should protect: judgment with a name attached.

I will show where the trust leaked.
