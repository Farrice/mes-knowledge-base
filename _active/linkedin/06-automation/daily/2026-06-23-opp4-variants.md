## VARIANT 1: Confessional / Scene-first — DECISION POINT PENDING

The first time I got it wrong, I was teaching someone how to hold a kettlebell and there was this split second — my hand was on theirs, I felt the tension go slack, and I knew: the cue worked, but the body didn't trust it yet. So I gave it again. And they stayed tense.

That's the moment taste started. In the half-second between giving the cue and watching the body refuse it.

It took maybe three more months of being wrong out loud in front of someone checking it the next day before I wasn't guessing anymore. Before I made calls that didn't need a second opinion because I'd already made the wrong one enough times to feel the difference.

This is why I'm skeptical when anyone tells you judgment scales.

Judgment scales when you have enough eyes checking your work to know when you're wrong the same afternoon. It scales when being wrong is humiliating. It doesn't scale through a prompt. It scales through reps, under observation.

Dana doesn't have a creativity problem. She has a judgment-scarcity problem, and there's no tool that builds it faster than a room that notices when you're wrong.

## VARIANT 2: Observational / Pattern-spotting (REVISED STRUCTURE — PROSE-DOCTOR FIXES APPLIED)

Two different job descriptions for the same role, shipping on LinkedIn three weeks apart.

One says: "Looking for a creative strategist who can develop 12 variants per week, ship at scale, leverage AI-assisted workflows."

The other says: "The competitive advantage in the age of AI is judgment. The spine to know what lands and the authority to say so. Can't be manufactured at scale. Earned — one good call at a time in front of people who'd notice if you got it wrong."

The first is a posting. The second is an audit. They're describing the same job two different ways, and nobody in recruiting has to pick.

I worked the version of the job where consequence is immediate — bodies don't forgive the wrong call, and there's no algorithm to backfill judgment. Every call made under that constraint taught me something. Not because I'm special, but because I was wrong enough times to feel the difference between pretending and knowing.

When Dana ships at the speed the calendar demands, she's caught between those same two descriptions. The one she's hired for (12 variants) and the one the market actually rewards (one call, made by someone who owns the outcome).

That gap is a judgment vacuum. It's also where taste accumulates.
