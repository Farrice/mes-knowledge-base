## POST 1: The Arrival Post
**Pillar 3 (The Body Doesn't Read Your Deck).** Draws from angle lines 11, 12, 13 (Pillar 3), reframed as the lane-shift I-story. Announces the new lane without launch-announcement energy.

---

For eighteen years, my job was a body in front of me.

A guy who couldn't sleep. A woman whose knee kept giving out. The lifter who read every label in the store and still felt nothing change.

I learned the difference between what a product claims and what actually moves in a person. "Supports recovery" is a phrase. I watched what recovery looks like when it finally shows up: the session that stops hurting, the morning someone wakes before the alarm and forgets they used to dread it.

Then I noticed the thing that pulled me sideways.

The brands making those products couldn't say that part. Their decks were full of proof. Their homepages sounded like every other homepage. The one thing no competitor could copy, someone who knows what the ingredient does inside a real body, never made it into the words.

So I changed lanes.

I help science-led supplement and performance brands decide what to say before they pay to say it. Positioning, proof, the buyer's real reason to care. Ten business days, settled before the launch spend leaves the building.

The body trained me to listen for the one thing a person actually needs to hear and can act on. Same instinct, pointed at a market instead of a client.

I still picture a body in front of me. Now it belongs to the stranger in the supplement aisle who turns your bottle over and looks for one reason to trust it.

## POST 2: The Category Tension Post
**Pillar 4 (The Sea of Same).** Built from angle lines 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, as lived homepage-sweep observation. Honest: a sweep Farrice can genuinely run; no client, no fabricated brand.

---

Last week I opened nine supplement homepages in nine tabs and tried to tell them apart.

Clinically dosed. Science-backed. Third-party tested. Clean label. Backed by research.

By the fourth tab I'd lost track of which brand I was looking at. Not because they were bad. Because they'd all reached for the same five words, and the words had quietly stopped meaning anything.

"Science-backed" used to be a moat. Somewhere in the last few years it turned into wallpaper. Technically present, completely invisible.

The part that gets me is that most of these brands really are different. The formulation logic is genuinely theirs. So is the sourcing, and the reason the brand exists at all. The differentiation is sitting right there in the product.

It just never makes it into the sentence.

That's the gap I keep seeing. The science is proprietary. The language is borrowed. A buyer scrolling nine tabs can't feel a difference the words refuse to carry.

Your buyer isn't skeptical of your science. They're bored of your sentence. They read it four times today already, on four sites that weren't yours.

The brand that wins the tab is the one that says the single true thing the other eight can't. Everyone else is still typing "clinically dosed" into the void.

## POST 3: The AI-Shadow Post
**Pillar 5 (The Machine Is Reading Your Label).** Built from angle lines 20, 21, 22, 24 (the two-readers idea). Softened per grounding rule: Farrice runs the query himself, no client, no "founder went quiet" scene. Closes to comment-gated Stress Test.

---

Last night I typed a plain question into ChatGPT: what's the best recovery supplement for someone training hard in their forties?

It named three brands. Described each one. Weighed the tradeoffs like a knowledgeable friend would.

I ran it a few more ways. Mostly the same three names came back. A lot of good brands never got mentioned at all, not because their product is worse, but because the machine had nothing clear to say about them.

Your label has two readers now. One has a body. One is a language model. Most brands wrote everything for the first and never once checked what the second was telling people.

And the second reader goes first. Before a buyer ever reaches your site, they've often already asked an AI what to take, and it has already answered. If your positioning is soft, the machine fills the gap with a guess. Usually a wrong one. Often a competitor's name.

You can optimize the funnel all you want. The front door is a conversation happening about you, without you in the room.

I built a short diagnostic for this exact blind spot. Twelve questions that show where your message goes soft, on the shelf and inside the machine.

Comment PROOF and I'll send it to you.
