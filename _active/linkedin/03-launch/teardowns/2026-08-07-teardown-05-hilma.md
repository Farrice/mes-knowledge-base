## 1. CREDIT WHERE IT'S REAL

Hilma ran a study on the thing they actually sell.

Almost nobody does this. The category standard is to find research on an ingredient, buy that ingredient, and let the research follow the ingredient onto your label. Hilma took a finished product and put it in front of people: *"We ran our own clinical study on the efficacy of Occasional Heartburn + Indigestion Relief."* Then a second one on Tension Relief.

They also printed the part that makes it weaker. Under the 94% headline, in small type: *"**Single arm observational study with 101 completed participants.**"* Under the 79% on Tension Relief: seventy completed participants, same design, same disclosure.

They said the word "observational" out loud. In a category where "clinically studied" usually means somebody read an abstract about an ingredient, that footnote is more honest than the entire shelf around it.

The rest holds up too. Clean Label Project™ Certified, named. *"Four Rounds Of Testing."* A scientific advisory board with real people attached to it, including a Yale-trained MD and herbalist and a board-certified gastroenterologist.

---

## 2. CLAIM → PROOF → GAP

**Claim A.** Product page heading: *"Clinically Proven To Work Quickly."* Directly below, the data: *"94% of participants saw a decrease in feelings of upset stomach after 30 minutes.**"* with the asterisks resolving to *"Single arm observational study with 101 completed participants."*

- **Proven:** 94 of roughly 101 people reported feeling better. That happened and they measured it.
- **Gap:** single-arm means no control group. Nobody got a placebo, so nothing separates the formula from thirty minutes passing, from the act of taking something, from a meal settling. "Clinically Proven" is a claim about causation resting on a design that cannot establish it. Their own footnote already says so, three lines lower, in smaller type. **The headline is arguing with the footnote and the footnote is right.**

**Claim B.** Badge row: *"Herbal antacid · Clinically studied · Drug-free."* Benefits: *"RELIEVES HEARTBURN · RELIEVES ACID INDIGESTION."* The panel on the same page is a **Supplement Facts** panel.

- **Proven:** it is a dietary supplement, correctly labeled as one, with the disclaimer present.
- **Gap:** "antacid" is a drug word. It names an OTC drug category with its own FDA monograph, and "relieves heartburn" is the phrasing that category uses. Every one of those claims carries an asterisk landing on *"not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease."* The asterisk is doing structural work in a place where a word choice could do it instead. I'm not a lawyer and this is not a legal read. It is the sentence a regulator's eye stops on, and they can rewrite it in an afternoon.

**Claim C.** Homepage hero: *"Debloat + Digest."* Sub: *"Everything you need to tackle bloat, ease digestion, + get regular—naturally."*

- **Proven:** accurate description of the line.
- **Gap:** interchangeable with every gut-health brand launched since 2020. Nothing in the hero says the one thing no competitor can say, which is that they tested this product on people and published the number and the study design. The homepage sells the category. The science page holds the moat.

**Claim D.** Ingredient claims, each with its own asterisk: chamomile *"promotes digestive comfort,"* artichoke leaf *"relieves occasional heartburn,"* marshmallow root *"soothes the intestinal tract,"* ginger *"promotes intestinal motility,"* licorice *"promotes integrity of mucosal lining."*

- **Proven:** standard structure/function phrasing, correctly hedged, and the site says *"We only use ingredients that have been deeply researched in clinical settings with double blind, placebo controlled studies."*
- **Gap:** the double-blind placebo-controlled research is at the ingredient level. Their own study, on their own product, is the single-arm one. The strong design belongs to somebody else's molecules and the weak design belongs to their formula, and the page presents both as one body of evidence.

## 5. THREE ANGLES I'D SHIP

**Angle 1 — Publish the study design as the pitch.** Retire "Clinically Proven." Run the honest version: we tested this product, not an ingredient, on 101 people. 94% felt better in 30 minutes. There was no placebo group, so this is what we measured, not what we proved. Here's what we're running next. That paragraph is stronger than the claim it replaces, it is unfalsifiable in the good way, and no competitor will copy it because copying it means admitting they never tested anything.

**Angle 2 — Move the moat to the homepage.** "Debloat + Digest" is the category's sentence. "We test our finished formulas on people and publish what we find, including the parts that make the numbers smaller" is theirs. Hero, PDP, packaging, retail sell sheet.

**Angle 3 — Turn the advisory board into arguments.** Six named doctors are currently six headshots. Dr. Close on why observational studies still matter when the alternative is nothing. Dr. Romm on where herbal evidence is genuinely strong and where it isn't. The brand that publishes its own limits owns the trust position in a category where nobody else will risk it.

---

## 6. THE COMPLIANCE LINE

Two things to separate, because they carry different weight.

The word "antacid" next to a Supplement Facts panel is the higher-exposure one. Drug-category language on a dietary supplement is a well-worn path to a letter, and the disclaimer asterisk does not neutralize a claim that reads as treatment of a condition. "Soothes after a heavy meal" does the same commercial job and sits inside the fence.

"Clinically Proven" is the second. An uncontrolled observational study supports "in our study, 94% reported," not "proven." The fix costs nothing and makes the claim more defensible, not less persuasive. Their footnote already contains the honest version.

Everything else here is well-built. Clean Label Project certification is named, the disclaimer runs where it should, and the ingredient claims are hedged correctly. The exposure is in two headlines, not in the product.

Single-arm was what we could afford, and running it at all put us ahead of everyone."* — Correct, and the piece says so in §1 and in the published version.
