## The Retrieval Asset

### Answer Page: "Keep Your Strength If Your Appetite Has Changed"
**Published:** June 23, 2026 | **Last Updated:** June 23, 2026 | **Next Review:** December 2026

---

#### The Situation

If you've started appetite-suppressant medication or noticed changes to your hunger and energy, you might be asking:
- How do I maintain my muscle and strength?
- What's the best way to fuel my workouts if I'm not hungry?
- Which supplements actually matter right now?

**This page answers those questions without pretending to know how medication works. It's just fitness basics, applied to a new situation.**

---

#### What's Actually Happening

Appetite-suppressant medications change how your body signals hunger. That's the intended effect. But it can make it harder to eat enough protein and calories for the workouts you want to do.

**The good news:** The same fitness fundamentals that work for everyone else work for you. You just might need to think about them differently.

---

#### How to Protect Your Strength

**1. Prioritize Protein**
You need protein to build and keep muscle. Most people need 0.7–1g per pound of body weight per day. If you're not hungry, hitting that number is harder.

**How to make it easier:**
- Protein powder in smoothies (no chewing required if you're not hungry)
- Collagen in coffee
- Greek yogurt or cottage cheese (small volume, high protein)
- Liquid nutrition (bone broth, protein shakes) counts

*Source: This is standard sports nutrition guidance, true regardless of appetite level.*

---

**2. Stay Hydrated**
Appetite suppression can mask thirst signals. Dehydration affects workout performance and recovery.

**How to make it easier:**
- Electrolyte drinks (sodium + potassium help with hydration and workout endurance)
- Set phone reminders if you're not thirsty
- Track water intake (aim for at least half your body weight in ounces)

*Source: Basic exercise physiology; applies to all athletes.*

---

**3. Keep Training, Adjust Intensity If Needed**
Don't stop exercising. But if you're eating less, you might need to adjust:
- Lower-rep strength training (burns fewer calories, maintains muscle)
- Shorter, more frequent sessions (easier to recover from on lower intake)
- Listen to energy (skip that third cardio session if you're genuinely depleted)

*Source: Strength and conditioning best practices.*

---

**4. Supplement Thoughtfully**
Not everything you bought before matters now. Here's what actually helps:

| Supplement | Why It Matters | How to Use |
|------------|---|---|
| **Protein powder** | Hits protein target without appetite friction | 1–3 servings/day as needed |
| **Electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium)** | Supports hydration and workout endurance | 500–1000mg sodium per workout |
| **Creatine** | Supports strength and muscle preservation | 5g daily (no loading) |
| **Caffeine** | May help with energy if appetite is suppressing carbs | 100–200mg before training |

**Supplements that probably don't help right now:**
- Fat-burners (your medication already suppresses appetite)
- Weight-loss blends (see above)
- High-calorie "gainers" (defeats the purpose if you're not hungry)
- Multivitamins alone (whole food first, supplement gaps only)

*Source: These recommendations are based on supplement efficacy research and adjusted for the specific context of appetite suppression.*

---

**5. Watch Your Micronutrients**
Lower food intake can mean lower micronutrient intake. If you're eating less, consider:
- Iron (especially if you're training hard and eat less red meat)
- B vitamins (easier to miss on lower calories)
- Vitamin D (always, but especially if energy is lower)

*Source: Nutrition science; true for anyone in caloric deficit.*

---

#### When to Talk to a Doctor

This page is about fitness and supplements. It is NOT medical advice. Talk to your doctor if:
- You're experiencing dizziness or fatigue that doesn't improve with hydration and nutrition
- Your appetite suppression is severe or concerning
- You're taking other medications and wondering about interactions

**Your medication is between you and your doctor. This page just helps you train safely around it.**

---

#### The Bottom Line

Your strength routine doesn't have to change just because your appetite did. The same principles work: adequate protein, hydration, smart training, and targeted supplements. You're not fighting the medication—you're just working with a different fuel situation.

**Start here. Track what works for you. Adjust if needed.**

## The Agent-Readable Primitive

### Purpose
Enable supplement brands to claim fitness support for people experiencing appetite changes, without claiming medication effects or clinical outcomes.

### Inputs
- Brand: supplement or fitness brand
- Category: protein, electrolytes, amino acids, or multi
- Claim: what the supplement does for the buyer
- Buyer situation: appetite suppression, energy changes, or similar

### Source of Truth
- **Medical boundary:** Medication outcomes are off-limits; fitness support outcomes are on-limit
- **Evidence requirement:** Claim must have published research (not just anecdote)
- **Wording rule:** Never invoke medication, treatment, or clinical outcome in messaging

### Decision Rules

**Rule 1: Medical Boundary Check**
If the claim uses ANY of these words, it's a medical claim and therefore OFF-LIMIT:
- Treat / treatment / therapeutic
- Medication / drug interaction
- Weight loss / fat loss / metabolism change (specific to medication)
- Appetite (specific to medication effect)
- Dosing / prescription language
- Clinical / evidence-based (when meaning medical evidence)
- Condition / disorder / disease

**Action:** Reframe as fitness support, not medication support.

---

**Rule 2: Supplement Claim Grade**
Score the claim's evidence strength:

| Grade | Definition | Safe to Claim? |
|-------|---|---|
| **VERIFIED** | Published research exists; claim is true regardless of appetite situation | YES, fully |
| **LIKELY** | Research exists for the supplement; limited research for this specific situation | YES, with qualification ("may help," "supports") |
| **UNCONFIRMED** | No published research or anecdotal only | NO, unless reframed as testimonial |

**Action:** Only claim at or above your evidence grade.

---

**Rule 3: Wording Guard Rail**

**Safe:**
- "Supports muscle preservation during calorie deficit"
- "Helps maintain hydration and workout endurance"
- "Provides amino acids for recovery"
- "May help with energy if your routine has changed"

**Unsafe:**
- "Works with GLP-1"
- "Offsets appetite suppression"
- "Helps with weight loss on medication"
- "Replaces food for workout energy"
- "Clinically proven for appetite-suppressed athletes"

**Action:** If a claim is unsafe, rewrite using the safe version.

---

### Examples

**Example 1: Electrolyte Brand**

**Raw claim:** "Electrolytes help you stay hydrated on GLP-1."

**Evidence grade:** LIKELY (electrolyte + dehydration research is strong; GLP-1-specific data is new)

**Medical boundary check:** ❌ FAIL (invokes medication specifically)

**Rewritten claim:** "Electrolytes support hydration and workout endurance during appetite changes."

**Evidence grade:** ✅ PASS (VERIFIED for hydration; LIKELY for appetite-change context)

**Why this works:** Acknowledges the buyer situation without claiming medication benefit. Sticks to electrolyte efficacy (published research). Buyer understands without medical overreach.

---

**Example 2: Protein Powder Brand**

**Raw claim:** "Protein helps you keep muscle if your appetite has shifted."

**Evidence grade:** VERIFIED (protein + muscle preservation is well-researched; true regardless of cause)

**Medical boundary check:** ✅ PASS (no medication language; states universal fitness principle)

**Safe to ship:** YES, fully

**Why this works:** Doesn't invoke medication. Claims supplement efficacy, not medication effect. Buyer understands the benefit without confusion about what's medical vs. fitness.

---

**Example 3: Creatine Brand**

**Raw claim:** "Creatine preserves strength on appetite-suppressant medication."

**Evidence grade:** UNCONFIRMED (creatine + muscle preservation is VERIFIED; GLP-1-specific outcomes haven't been studied)

**Medical boundary check:** ❌ FAIL (invokes medication specifically and implies medication-specific outcome)

**Rewritten claim:** "Creatine supports strength and muscle preservation during training—especially helpful if your nutrition intake has shifted."

**Evidence grade:** ✅ PASS (VERIFIED for strength; LIKELY for lower-calorie context)

**Why this works:** Removes medication language. Makes the fitness principle universal. Acknowledges the buyer situation without claiming medication science.

---

### Counterexamples (What NOT to Do)

❌ "This supplement works like GLP-1" — Medication claim
❌ "Prevents the muscle loss from appetite-suppressant drugs" — Implies clinical outcome
❌ "Replaces calories on GLP-1" — Overstates supplement role
❌ "Clinically proven for medication users" — Manufactures medical authority
❌ "The only supplement safe on appetite medication" — False safety claim

---

### Quality Tests

**Test 1: Medical Boundary**
Read the claim aloud. Does it require the buyer to know about medication?
- YES = rewrite to remove medication language
- NO = proceed to Test 2

**Test 2: Evidence Grade**
Can you cite published research for this claim (not in a medical journal specifically)?
- YES = claim passes
- NO = add "may help" or "supports" qualifier, or don't ship

**Test 3: Buyer Net Impression**
Would a buyer in this situation (appetite changes, wants to keep fitness routine) understand what this supplement does WITHOUT assuming medical benefit?
- YES = ship
- NO = rewrite or escalate

---

### Failure Modes

**Failure 1: Medical Creep**
Team member ships: "Works with appetite-suppressant medications."
**Detection:** Medical Boundary Test fails.
**Fix:** Rewrite. Move to Audit tier in service ladder.

**Failure 2: Overclaim**
Team member ships: "Guarantees muscle preservation."
**Detection:** Evidence Grade fails (no supplement "guarantees" anything).
**Fix:** Add qualifier ("may help," "supports"). Retest.

**Failure 3: Context Drift**
Team member ships: "For people on GLP-1" (in a different context, e.g., weight-loss email).
**Detection:** Claim is safe in fitness context; unsafe in weight-loss positioning context.
**Fix:** Review claim + context together. Escalate if unclear.
