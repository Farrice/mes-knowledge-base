## Executive Thesis

Eightward should become the apparel mark for people who carry the joy of Lindy Hop forward.

The brand should not lead as "funny shirts for swing dancers." That can still exist, but as a secondary offer. The main brand should give dancers an identity object: a way to wear love for the dance, respect for its roots, and membership in the social-floor culture.

Best strategic move:

1. Keep Eightward as the lead name while legal clearance continues.
2. Make the core offer culture-rooted identity apparel.
3. Turn joke shirts into limited drops, workshop/weekender pieces, or inside-community artifacts.
4. Build the visual world from floorcraft, count, rhythm, social connection, late-night dance spaces, and original marks.
5. Treat Black Lindy Hop history as the source to honor, not a drawer of references to decorate with.

## What The Research Means For The Brand

Lindy Hop is not merely a vintage dance style. The sources consistently point to Harlem, the Savoy Ballroom, Black social dancing, jazz, improvisation, and dancers like Frankie Manning as central to the dance's formation and spread. The Savoy is repeatedly framed as a historically important, culturally mixed dance venue, with Savoy-style Lindy Hop tied to Black dancers of that era.

The joy matters, but it should not be made shallow. In this context, joy is social, physical, musical, inventive, communal, and historically charged. It is not only "happy retro fun." It is a way people found expression, status, friendship, play, excellence, and freedom inside a specific cultural setting.

The modern scene creates a second responsibility. Lindy Hop was carried into new generations through revival, teaching, camps, film clips, and global dance scenes. That revival is part of the brand's audience, but the brand should not quietly recenter non-Black revival taste as the whole story. Current Black Lindy Hop stewardship work exists because access, memory, mentorship, and cultural credit still matter.

Brand implication: Eightward can be joyful, smart, and contemporary, but it needs the posture of a respectful participant.

## Positioning

Category:

Culture-rooted Lindy Hop apparel and social-dance identity goods.

Positioning line:

Eightward makes apparel for dancers who want to carry the joy of Lindy Hop with taste, respect, and belonging.

Sharper version:

For dancers who carry the joy forward.

Enemy:

Rootless novelty merch: shirts that flatten the dance into a pun, costume nostalgia, or tech-conference humor.

Promise:

Wear the count. Honor the floor. Carry the joy.

The wearer should feel:

- "This was made by someone who knows the room."
- "This respects where the dance came from."
- "This feels good enough to wear outside the dance."
- "This says I am part of the culture without needing to explain myself."

## Remarkability Mechanism

Eightward becomes remarkable when it gives dancers a signal they already want but do not have yet.

What spreads:

- Identity: "I am one of the people who carries this dance."
- Recognition: "Someone who made this has been in the room."
- Taste: "This works as clothing before it works as a joke."
- Story: "The count, the floor, and the joy all point back to something real."
- Scarcity: small capsules tied to drops, weekends, classes, or private scene moments.

What does not spread:

- Generic vintage swing art.
- A paragraph of explanation on a shirt.
- Dance-pun novelty with no garment taste.
- Historical name-dropping without care.
- Nerd references that make dancers feel secondary.

## StoryBrand Spine

Customer hero:

The Lindy Hop dancer, teacher, DJ, volunteer, or serious fan who loves the dance enough to want their clothes to carry the feeling.

Customer desire:

To wear something that signals joy, craft, roots, and belonging without looking like costume merch or a cheap joke shirt.

External problem:

Most dance apparel is either too generic, too novelty-driven, too retro-costume-coded, or too disconnected from the culture's deeper story.

Internal problem:

"I love this dance, but the merch rarely feels like the scene I care about."

Philosophical problem:

A dance with this much history and joy deserves apparel with care.

Brand guide stance:

Eightward does not claim the culture. It studies, honors, and carries a small part of it forward through original design.

Plan:

1. Start with the roots and the room.
2. Build original marks and garments from the social-floor experience.
3. Release small drops dancers can wear with pride.

Core one-liner:

Most Lindy Hop lovers struggle to find apparel that feels serious enough for the culture and joyful enough for the floor. Eightward makes culture-rooted dance apparel so dancers can wear their love for Lindy Hop with taste, respect, and belonging.

Short bio:

Culture-rooted Lindy Hop apparel for dancers who carry the joy forward.

Founder pitch:

Eightward is a Lindy Hop apparel brand built for people who found joy, craft, and community on the social floor. The designs honor the roots, speak the language of dancers, and keep the inside jokes as the reward, not the whole brand.

## Josh's "Acolyte And Ambassador" Idea

The impulse is strong. The public wording needs care.

Keep:

- ambassador
- carrier
- keeper
- steward
- friend of the floor
- student of the dance

Avoid as public positioning:

- acolyte
- disciple
- convert
- worship
- gospel of Lindy Hop

Reason: religious language can make the brand feel culty, overly sacred, or as if Eightward is claiming authority over a culture it needs to honor. Frankie Manning is also widely described as an ambassador of Lindy Hop, so Eightward should avoid sounding like it is taking that mantle. "Carry the joy forward" keeps Josh's intent without the risk.

Recommended phrase:

Eightward is for dancers who carry the joy forward.

## Audience Map

Primary audience:

Serious Lindy Hop and swing dancers who go to socials, classes, workshops, exchanges, late nights, competitions, or practice groups. They care about the dance as a culture, not just a hobby.

Inner circle:

- Teachers
- DJs
- Event volunteers
- Scene builders
- History-aware dancers
- Dancers who travel for weekends and camps
- People who care about Black Lindy Hop lineage and cultural credit

Secondary audience:

- Jazz and swing music people
- Adjacent social dancers
- Vintage-aware but not costume-focused dressers
- Design-conscious dancers
- Nerd-adjacent dancers who like the earlier joke territory

Audience to avoid over-serving:

People who only want a novelty pun. They can buy a satellite design, but they should not define the brand.

## Cultural Safety Rules

1. Do not make Black cultural history a styling prop.
2. Do not imply Eightward owns, certifies, or leads Lindy Hop culture.
3. Do not use historic figures, venues, songs, lyrics, photos, or event marks on products without rights review.
4. Do not present non-Black revival taste as the full center of the story.
5. Build original marks from count, floorcraft, connection, joy, and community behavior.
6. If Eightward uses education content, cite sources and keep claims modest.
7. If the brand ever donates to or collaborates with a cultural organization, make the support real before saying it publicly.
8. Before a serious cultural capsule launches, get feedback from Black Lindy Hoppers or culture stewards, ideally paid.

## Lawyer Questions For This Expanded Direction

Use the existing Eightward legal consult brief as the main trademark document. Add these questions:

1. Can Josh use historical dance terms such as Lindy Hop, swingout, floorcraft, and 8-count in product names or descriptions?
2. What are the risks around using `Savoy`, `Harlem`, `Whitey's Lindy Hoppers`, `Frankie Manning`, `Norma Miller`, event names, or song titles?
3. Are public-domain photos still risky because of publicity, archive, photographer, or estate rights?
4. Can a design contain an educational source note if the product itself avoids protected names and images?
5. Is `Carry The Joy Forward` protectable as a tagline, and does it conflict with existing dance or apparel uses?
6. Should Josh file for `EIGHTWARD` before public brand storytelling expands?
7. Should capsule names be searched before launch even if the main brand is Eightward?

## Research Gaps To Close Before A Public Drop

The brief is strong enough for direction. It is not yet community-validated.

Run a small listening pass:

- 3 Lindy Hop teachers or organizers
- 2 DJs
- 2 frequent social dancers
- 2 dancers who care deeply about history and Black Lindy Hop roots
- 1 Black Lindy Hopper or culture steward paid for review, if Josh is serious about a root-forward capsule

Ask:

- What apparel already feels scene-native?
- What looks fake to you immediately?
- What words should a brand avoid?
- What historical references feel respectful?
- What would you wear to a dance weekend?
- What would you wear outside the dance?
- Which line feels strongest: `Carry the joy forward`, `Wear the count`, `Honor the floor`, or `The count is only the beginning`?

## First 30-Day Path

Week 1:

- Finish legal consult on Eightward.
- Keep Eightward internal until counsel advises on public use.
- Build a two-page founder/culture brief from this packet.

Week 2:

- Run 8-10 listening interviews.
- Test the top 4 lines and 3 visual directions.
- Retire any wording that feels like appropriation, costume, or empty prestige.

Week 3:

- Design one core identity piece and one satellite joke piece.
- Keep both in the same visual system.
- Produce mockups on real garment silhouettes, not flat white product boards only.

Week 4:

- Share privately with 12-20 target dancers.
- Track exact reactions: "I would wear this," "I would buy this," "this feels true," "this feels off."
- Only then choose the first public drop.
