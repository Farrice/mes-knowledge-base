# 16 — The Upsell Bridge (contingency map, not implied path)

**Audience:** Farrice (internal playbook). This file is for the scenario where the menu work generates a conversation that wants to keep going. If it doesn't, this file never gets opened — and that's fine.
**Purpose:** Map the specific narrative arc that *could* take Farrice from "got paid for the audit" to "on retainer for ten concepts," IF that arc emerges from the buyer's side. Not a sales sequence to push. A reference for how to be ready with the right words if and only if the buyer arrives at the doorway on their own.
**Core principle:** *"The upsell doesn't happen in a pricing conversation. It happens when the buyer has already started thinking about Tier 3 on their own, and Farrice arrives with the words for what they're already feeling. If the buyer doesn't arrive there, this whole document is silent. The cover note's 'totally up to you' language is the standard."*

## The reading frame for this file

Before any of the arc below makes sense, the reader (Farrice) needs to hold two things at once:

1. **The menu work is the work.** Files 02, 03, and 04 are what Chris asked for and what the folder is. Everything in this file describes what happens *if* the menu work generates a follow-up conversation that the buyer wants to extend. If it doesn't — if Chris uses the menu intelligence and that's the end — this file is irrelevant. That's a fine outcome.
2. **The buyer is the one who arrives at the doorway.** Nothing in this file gets pushed. The pricing tiers, the proof-point sequence, the "natural expansion sentences" — all of it is dormant unless and until the buyer signals they want to keep going. The cover note's "totally up to you" language is the contract Farrice signed with himself when he sent the folder. Honor it.

With those two things held, the rest of this file is the contingency map: what to do if a conversation wants to extend, with the words ready.

## The arc at a glance

Most consultants think of the upsell as a closing move. It isn't. It's a sequencing move. IF a buyer wants to extend the conversation, the job of the first 90 days is to make Tier 3 feel inevitable — not as a sales outcome, but as the obvious next sentence in a story the buyer is already telling themselves. IF no buyer wants to extend, none of this matters and the menu work stands on its own.

The arc has four acts:

1. **Act 1 — Audit Completion (Tier 1).** Farrice gets paid a small fee to formalize what he already built. The deliverable is already in Chris's hands; the audit adds the POS scan and formalizes the scope.
2. **Act 2 — The First Shipped Win (Tier 2, Month 1).** Yakiya beverage attach moves from 6% to 10%. Real money, documented, presented as a single-paragraph result with the POS report attached. This is the win that buys the right to month 2.
3. **Act 3 — The Portfolio Expansion Moment (Tier 2, Month 2–3).** Something happens in the Yakiya readout meeting that makes Gigi or another stakeholder say some version of "can we do this for Hibachi-San?" This question is the doorway to Tier 3. Farrice's job is to make sure the question gets asked.
4. **Act 4 — The Portfolio Partnership (Tier 3).** The 12-month retainer. Not sold — recognized. By the time Farrice names the price, Gigi has already been thinking about it for two weeks.

## Act 1 — Tier 1 Audit Completion (Days 1–14 of engagement)

**Framing line Farrice uses:**

> "The folder was built as a gift. The audit is the contracted version — same analysis, plus the POS scan, plus the formal scope. This is what Chris would bring to any operations review."

**What Farrice delivers in Act 1:**

- **The POS audit on each of the three locations.** The purpose is not the audit findings themselves (which will be predictable — most restaurant POS data is messy). The purpose is to give Farrice standing to recommend specific Phase 1 work in Act 2, grounded in what the POS can actually support.
- **Formalized competitive pricing dashboards.** Same as the gift folder, but refreshed with a second-pass dataset and branded as contracted work.
- **The manager voice log demo installed on Chris's phone for 14 days.** This is the single most important deliverable in Act 1. Not because it generates revenue — because it generates a story Chris will tell in every future PRG meeting for 12 months: *"I've been using this tool for two weeks and it's actually useful."*

**The proof point this stage produces:**

Chris has a tool running on his phone that works. When anyone at PRG asks Chris "what did Farrice actually do?" he answers with a demo, not a description. That demo is the anchor for everything that follows.

**The conversation Farrice sets up in Act 1's close:**

At the end of Act 1 (day 14), Farrice has a 30-minute call with Chris. He does not pitch Tier 2. He asks one question:

> "You've had the folder for two weeks and the voice log for two weeks. What's the one thing you'd want to see me work on next, if you were picking?"

Chris's answer to that question is the Tier 2 scope. Farrice will write the Tier 2 proposal around whatever Chris names — because whatever Chris names is the thing Chris will advocate for internally. The upsell starts with letting the buyer pick the problem.

## Act 2 — The First Shipped Win (Tier 2, Month 1)

**The target proof point:** Yakiya beverage attach rate moves from 6% to 10% in 30 days.

*(If Chris names a different starting problem in the Act 1 close — Panda Inn review response, Wasabi throughput, review response automation — substitute that. The principle is the same: one specific, measurable, dollar-attributed win in month 1.)*

**What Farrice actually does in Month 1 of Tier 2:**

- **Week 1:** Working session with Chris + Yakiya's GM. Draft a specific beverage script ("would you like to start with our [specific sake pairing] while we bring the first course?") and a measurement protocol (before/after attach rate by server by daypart).
- **Week 1–2:** Deploy the manager voice log → daily digest across Yakiya. Now Yakiya's GM gets a daily 6am brief that flags beverage attach performance by server from the previous night. No dashboard to check — the information arrives.
- **Week 2–3:** Run the test. Servers follow the new script. Variance gets tracked. Farrice is not on-site — he's reading the data remotely and sending a daily 3-sentence note to Chris.
- **Week 4:** Pull the results. Write up the single-page readout. Prepare the Month 1 presentation.

**The Month 1 readout meeting (the single most important event in the entire engagement):**

Farrice runs a 45-minute meeting with Chris + Gigi (if she's available) + the Yakiya GM. The meeting has exactly three slides:

1. **Slide 1 — The before state.** One sentence: *"30 days ago, Yakiya's beverage attach was 6%. Industry floor for Pasadena premium is 20%."*
2. **Slide 2 — What changed.** Three bullets: script change, server awareness via morning brief, one measurement protocol.
3. **Slide 3 — The result.** One number (e.g., "9.8%"), one caveat ("still a month-one read, not a steady state"), and one forward projection ("if this holds, annualized beverage margin unlock of roughly $280K at this location").

Then Farrice stops talking.

**The conversation that follows the readout:**

This is the moment Farrice has been designing for. One of three things will happen:

**Reaction A — Gigi or a stakeholder asks "can we run the same thing at Hibachi-San?"**
This is the dream reaction. Farrice's response: *"That's the right next question. Let me pull what I have on Hibachi-San and come back in a week with a scoped version." Do NOT pitch Tier 3 in the moment.* Set up the follow-up conversation where Tier 3 gets named.

**Reaction B — Gigi says "great result, what's month 2?"**
This is the continuation frame. Farrice pivots to the month 2 Panda Inn founding-myth work, keeps the Tier 2 engagement on schedule, and files the expansion question for the Month 2 readout.

**Reaction C — Gigi is flat or skeptical.**
Unlikely given the specificity of the number, but possible. Farrice's move is to ask: *"What would this result have needed to be for it to feel like the right direction?"* The answer tells him exactly what to optimize for in month 2.

**The proof point this stage produces:**
A Yakiya POS report showing the beverage attach rate lift. Documented. Not a deck. Not a narrative. An actual report pulled from their own system, stapled to a one-paragraph summary of what drove the change.

## Act 3 — The Portfolio Expansion Moment (Tier 2, Month 2–3)

**This is where the upsell actually happens. Not in a pricing conversation — in the space between "we saw it work once" and "when does this become a permanent thing."**

**What Farrice does in Month 2:**

Farrice executes the Panda Inn founding-myth work. This is a categorically different kind of win from the Yakiya beverage work — it's brand-adjacent storytelling, not operational mechanics. The purpose of running this in Month 2 (after a hard operational win in Month 1) is to show Gigi that Farrice's capabilities cover more than one flavor of problem.

The Month 2 readout: *"Panda Inn Glendale Google Business Profile click-through rose from X% to Y% after the founding-myth content activated. Menu inserts with the story are now in all four Panda Inn locations. Zero new menu cost."*

**What Farrice does in Month 3:**

Wasabi throughput diagnostic. This is another flavor shift — operational throughput on a theme-park captive market. By the end of Month 3, Farrice has now shipped three different kinds of wins across three different concepts.

**The Month 3 readout is different from Month 1 and 2.** It is a PORTFOLIO readout, not a single-concept readout. Three slides:

1. **Slide 1 — What we've shipped across the portfolio.** A grid: Yakiya beverage, Panda Inn founding-myth, Wasabi throughput. Three columns: before state, intervention, result.
2. **Slide 2 — The pattern.** One sentence: *"Every win shared the same shape — a specific decision nobody on the internal team had the bandwidth to make, a 30-day test, a measurable result."*
3. **Slide 3 — The question.** Not a proposal. A question: *"What else on the legacy portfolio has this shape?"*

**The question on slide 3 is the doorway to Tier 3.**

The question invites Gigi to list the other opportunities she's been thinking about for the last 60 days — Hibachi-San, the possible Ippudo refresh, the Just Salad marketing calendar, the Uncle Tetsu attach rate problem, whatever. Each thing she names is a potential Tier 3 line item. Farrice is not selling Tier 3 — he's letting Gigi build the Tier 3 scope in her own head.

**The critical move:**
When Gigi starts listing opportunities, Farrice does NOT immediately say "Tier 3 is $22,500 per month." He says:

> "Let me take a week to scope what running all of that at the same time would actually look like. I'll come back with two options — a focused version that covers just the next three things, and a broader version that covers the whole specialty portfolio. You pick which one makes sense for your organization."

This does three things:

1. **It assumes the work is happening.** The only question is scope, not existence.
2. **It gives Gigi two numbers to compare against each other**, which anchors the decision inside the choice between options instead of between "yes and no."
3. **It buys Farrice a week to assemble the Tier 3 proposal** with the specific concepts Gigi named — meaning the proposal is literally built around her own list. Impossible to say no to.

## Act 4 — The Portfolio Partnership (Tier 3)

**Week after Month 3 readout: The Tier 3 scoping conversation.**

Farrice returns with two options:

**Option A — "Focused Expansion":** Covers the three things Gigi named most urgently in the Month 3 meeting. $15,000/month, 12-month commitment. (The lower Tier 3 anchor from the pricing doc.)

**Option B — "Portfolio Partnership":** Covers all four original legacy concepts + the two or three additional concepts Gigi raised (e.g., Hibachi-San, Ippudo, Just Salad). $22,500/month, 12-month commitment.

Farrice presents both, then says:

> "My honest read is that Option B is the right one, not because it's bigger but because the work we've been doing already touches brands beyond the three you originally asked about. You've been asking me about Hibachi-San in every meeting for two months. I think the portfolio version matches how you're already thinking. But Option A is legitimate if you want to test the rhythm with a smaller scope first. Either one works."

**What happens next depends on Gigi's organization:**

- **If Gigi has direct signature authority for $22,500/month**, she signs Option B in the room. The partnership starts.
- **If she needs to take it to Tom Davin or David Landsberg**, Farrice hands her a one-page executive summary of Option B with the ROI math ($370K floor, $1.12M stretch — see Tier 3 pricing page) designed to be forwarded as-is.
- **If she goes with Option A**, Farrice runs it for 90 days, ships another round of wins, and proposes Option B again at Month 6. The graceful path to the bigger scope.

**The critical principle:** Farrice does not pressure either option. Whichever Gigi chooses is a win. Option A is $180K/year. Option B is $270K/year. Both are 10–20x the original Tier 1 audit fee that started everything.

## Red Flags — When NOT to Push the Upsell

There are specific buyer disengagement signals that mean Farrice should NOT attempt the upsell, and should instead complete Tier 2 cleanly and exit warmly:

**Red Flag 1 — Gigi misses the Month 1 readout.**
If the first result meeting gets pushed by the buyer, or Gigi sends a delegate instead of attending, engagement is weakening. The Month 1 wins need to be seen by the decision-maker directly. If they aren't, the upsell arc breaks.

**Red Flag 2 — The Yakiya beverage result doesn't move.**
If the Month 1 intervention fails (attach rate stays at 6% or barely moves), Farrice does NOT push Tier 3. He runs the Month 2 and Month 3 work, delivers a clean audit of why the beverage intervention didn't perform, and offers to scope a revised Tier 2 at a discounted rate. The upsell is dead. The trust isn't. Preserve the relationship.

**Red Flag 3 — Chris becomes quiet.**
If Chris, who was the internal champion, starts going silent between meetings or deflecting questions about PRG's internal reactions, something has shifted politically. Farrice should ask Chris directly in a private one-on-one: *"What's the read inside? I'd rather know if this is in trouble than guess."* Chris will tell him. If the answer is "people upstairs don't like it," Farrice should complete Tier 2 cleanly, thank everyone, and wait 60 days before revisiting.

**Red Flag 4 — Gigi introduces a procurement officer.**
If at any point Gigi says "my procurement team needs to review this," the relationship has shifted from strategic to transactional. This isn't fatal, but the upsell will have to go through a formal process — which means Farrice should stop pushing narrative moves and start preparing a properly structured proposal package. The emotional velocity is gone.

**Red Flag 5 — Anyone at PRG asks for references or case studies from comparable restaurant groups.**
This means the trust bank isn't full. The deliverable and the demo haven't been enough. Farrice should offer references without pushback, but recognize that the upsell arc has stalled and the Tier 3 conversation will need to wait until the next engagement cycle.

**Red Flag 6 — Andrea Cherng's CBO team gets involved.**
If brand strategy enters the conversation, the frame has expanded beyond what Farrice positioned for. He should immediately fall back to the "nothing in this engagement modifies brand expression" pre-empt and clarify that the work is operational. If PRG pushes for brand scope anyway, Farrice should refer them out to a brand strategist and preserve the operations scope only.

That's why WS5's pricing recommendation is to make Tier 2 Month 1 the single most over-delivered 30 days of Farrice's career. The beverage lift from 6% to 10% is the entire upsell arc. Everything else is scaffolding around that number.

## The Natural Expansion Sentences

When it comes time to name Tier 3, Farrice does not invent new positioning. He uses sentences he's been setting up since Month 1. Here is the sequence:

**Month 1 opener (buried in the Yakiya readout):**
> "We focused on one concept for month 1. Month 2 is a different concept, different kind of problem — so we can see whether the same approach generalizes."

*(Plants the seed: "does this generalize across concepts?")*

**Month 2 opener:**
> "Now we've seen it work on operational mechanics at Yakiya and on brand-adjacent storytelling at Panda Inn. Month 3 is Wasabi throughput — the hardest of the three because the constraint is physical, not data."

*(Plants the seed: "he can do multiple things.")*

**Month 3 opener:**
> "Three months, three concepts, three different flavors of win. The question I want to leave you with today is: what else on the legacy portfolio has this same shape?"

*(Plants the seed: "the portfolio is the unit, not the concept.")*

**Tier 3 naming sentence:**
> "The pattern you've watched for 90 days is the engagement. Making it permanent just means writing down the rhythm — which concepts, how often, what decisions per quarter. That's Tier 3. Two ways to scope it — let me walk you through both."

Notice what's missing: Farrice never says "I want to sell you a retainer." He says "the pattern you've watched is the engagement." The buyer has been inside Tier 3 for 90 days. The only thing missing is the paperwork.

That's how the upsell actually lands.

## One Rule That Governs Everything

*"The upsell is the buyer's idea. Farrice's job is to make sure the idea is correct when it arrives, and that the words for it are ready."*

Every move in Acts 1 through 4 is built around that rule. Do not violate it. The moment Farrice pushes Tier 3 into the conversation before Gigi has started thinking about it on her own, the arc collapses.

Patience is the price of the retainer. Build the proof points. Let the buyer do the thinking. Be ready with the words when she arrives at the doorway — because she will arrive. The arithmetic is on Farrice's side.
