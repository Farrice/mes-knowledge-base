# Create Wellness claim-to-answer review

**Prepared:** 2026-08-31  
**Status:** Unsolicited local draft. Not sent.  
**Scope:** Public-facing U.S. product and support language only. This is a creative-strategy and claim-substantiation screen, not legal advice, regulatory clearance, or a finding of noncompliance.

## The finding

Create raised $20 million. The stated uses included retail expansion, product innovation, paid growth, and consumer education about creatine. One support answer viewed for this review appears to tell customers that stacking its gummies and electrolyte product gives them more than **5 milligrams** of creatine.

The published product amounts are measured in grams.

That is probably a unit typo. It is also the smallest version of the problem.

Create has strong quality signals. The NSF Certified for Sport database lists its Watermelon creatine gummies, and the brand says its products are third-party tested. But the product page also asks that quality proof to sit beside outcome language such as “clean energy and sharper focus” and a stated 2-to-3-week benefit window.

The certification can support confidence in tested contents and screening. It does not, by itself, substantiate those benefit claims for the exact product.

**Diagnosis:** In this public sample, Create’s quality proof is stronger than its claim-to-answer system. At national retail scale, the gap can turn consumer education into consumer interpretation.

## Why this matters now

The stakes exceed page polish. Create announced a $20 million Series B led by Alliance Consumer Growth and Impact Capital. Unilever Ventures also participated. The company said the capital would support retail expansion, consumer education, product innovation, and marketing. ACG lists Create as a 2026 partner brand.

Every new shelf, search result, retailer page, support article, creator brief, and AI answer increases the number of places where one product truth must remain intact. A unit error can be fixed in minutes. A loose relationship between certification, ingredient evidence, exact formulation, and benefit language is a system problem.

The commercial cost is not established here. There is no public evidence in this review showing lost sales, customer confusion, ad rejection, legal concern, or poor conversion. Those remain **UNCONFIRMED**. The verified issue is narrower: the public answer layer contains an apparent unit error, while the product layer contains claims whose product-specific support was not available in the reviewed public materials.

## The claim boundary

No express disease claim was found in the reviewed excerpts. The central phrases are structure/function or performance-adjacent claims, which can be permissible when truthful, not misleading, and adequately substantiated.

The evidence question is product-specific:

| Claim | Minimum proof question | Public evidence found in this review | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.5 g creatine per three-gummy serving | Does the label and testing support the amount? | Product page plus NSF product listing | Supported by supplied material |
| “Sharper focus” | Is there competent and reliable human evidence for this exact formula, dose, delivery form, and intended population? | Mixed ingredient-level creatine cognition literature; no exact-product study found | Reviewer required |
| Benefits in 2–3 weeks | Is this a typical-result statement supported by product-specific consumer or clinical evidence? | No support located in the reviewed public materials | Reviewer required |
| “Cognitive performance” for Creatine + Electrolytes | Does the exact 5 g creatine, electrolyte, and taurine product support the breadth of the stated outcome? | Product announcement only; no exact-product evidence package reviewed | Reviewer required |

The broader creatine literature cannot automatically be transferred to the marketed product. Ingredient evidence, exact dose, delivery form, population, and wording all affect how much a specific claim can carry. The scientific literature on cognition is also not one clean verdict: recent reviews include both supportive findings and conclusions that the evidence remains limited or inconsistent. That makes precision more valuable, not less.

## The repair

### 1. Correct the answer layer

Verify the intended stacked dose internally, then replace `5mg` with the correct unit and total. Add the product names and serving assumptions to the answer. A customer should not have to infer whether the article means one serving of each product, a partial serving, or a category-level recommendation.

### 2. Build a claim-to-proof register

For every recurring benefit phrase, record:

- exact wording in market;
- product and serving it refers to;
- evidence type and whether it is ingredient-level or product-specific;
- approved surfaces;
- owner and review date.

Start with `sharper focus`, `clean energy`, `benefits in 2–3 weeks`, and `cognitive performance`. If the exact evidence is held internally, attach it. If it is ingredient-level only, narrow the wording to the ingredient and mechanism the evidence supports. If no adequate evidence is held, soften or remove the outcome.

### 3. Separate quality proof from benefit proof

Use NSF Certified for Sport exactly where it is strongest: screened contents, prohibited-substance testing, contaminant controls, and manufacturing confidence for the listed product. Do not let layout or proximity imply that NSF certified the efficacy of “sharper focus” or a 2-to-3-week result.

This is the category behavior worth rejecting: **certification laundering**, where a legitimate quality mark is made to feel like efficacy proof through placement rather than an explicit claim. The term names an industry pattern, not a conclusion that Create intended to mislead.

### 4. Turn the repair into better creative

The safe creative opportunity is not bland disclaimer copy. It is more specific proof:

> Three Watermelon gummies. 4.5 g of Creapure creatine monohydrate. NSF Certified for Sport for the listed product. The dose is on the page.

This is a working creative direction, not cleared final copy. The brand’s reviewer should confirm the exact certification scope, trademark language, substantiation package, label, and required disclaimer before use.

## The bounded recommendation

Run a one-SKU claim-to-answer consistency pass before the next creative or education push. Begin with the Watermelon gummies and the pages that answer dose, time-to-benefit, focus, and certification questions. The deliverable is a corrected answer set, a claim-to-proof register, and three creative routes that use the strongest approved proof without asking NSF certification to prove efficacy.

The typo opens the door. The valuable work is making sure every answer behind it leads to the same product truth.
