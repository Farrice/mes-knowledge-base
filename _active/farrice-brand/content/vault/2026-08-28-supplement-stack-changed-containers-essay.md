# The supplement stack changed containers.

A man posted a picture of his supplement stack this week. The bottles covered
most of the table. After 370 comments, he explained that the post was half a
joke. “Rate my stack” meant he had literally stacked them.

The picture is funny because the category already knows the problem. People
want the benefits and hate the routine. They do not want another bottle, another
time of day, another reorder and another argument about whether any of it is
working.

Product teams are responding by compressing the stack.

DIZZY launched a one-scoop product for women with creatine, electrolytes,
vitamins, minerals, Lion's Mane and Siberian ginseng. Its page makes the trade
visible: one formula with 12 ingredients at £38 against five separately sourced
products at £93.

Isagenix launched WITHIN as a five-in-one daily foundation across gut health,
immune support and recovery. Attaboy's company release describes one daily
product across energy, focus, strength, recovery and hydration, with growth led
by subscription retention.

The formulations differ. The commercial move is the same. Fewer containers,
fewer steps, one daily habit.

The message problem starts after that product decision.

Every ingredient brings a claim. Every claim brings another buyer state. Focus
belongs to the afternoon. Recovery belongs after training. Hydration belongs to
heat, travel and work. Gut support belongs to a different worry altogether.

The product may carry all of those jobs. A campaign that gives them equal weight
asks the buyer to perform the positioning.

You can see the counterpressure in the same market.

P&G disclosed an agreement to acquire Thorne for $3.8 billion. Its public
explanation emphasized scientific rigor, product quality, practitioner
credibility and trust instead of reciting a giant benefit stack.

That observation says nothing about whether narrow products win or broad
formulas fail. It does signal what becomes valuable when a wellness brand
reaches scale.
The ingredient list can be copied. A trusted explanation, carried by the right
people and repeated across years, is harder to reproduce.

This week also showed the weakness of competing on specifications alone. Two
new creatine products led with 10 grams. The number is immediate and easy to
compare. It is also easy for the next competitor to match. A product can become
broader or stronger on paper while the reason to choose it becomes thinner.

The useful founder question sits between those two moves.

What should this product own after the buyer stops reading the label?

The answer should name one person, one occasion and one belief. The rest of the
formula can support that choice. It does not need to fight for equal billing.

A woman reaching for one scoop before a long workday may care about the same
ingredients as someone reaching for it after training. They are still buying
inside different stories. One page can explain the formula. One campaign should
decide which story leads.

That is the work underneath a useful Angle Map. Three defensible campaign
angles, each tied to proof, followed by one recommendation. The deliverable is
small because the decision is the valuable part.

Inside the scoop sits the old stack. The founder still has to decide what the
buyer carries away.
