Women used to stop me at the counter of a Vitamin Shoppe to ask one question about creatine: will this make me look like the guy on the tub?

I answered it for five years. The honest answer was no, and almost nobody believed me, because everything else in the store said otherwise: the label, the 5-pound tub, the guy on it.

Fifteen years later that question is finally being answered at scale, and the people answering it don't sell creatine.

The most-watched creatine-for-women video I could find this week is an hour-long physiology masterclass on a scientist's own channel. 165,000 views. Behind it, a podcast clip titled with the exact question buyers type into search: should women supplement with creatine? 63,000. Then Good Morning America, between the weather and a cooking segment. And circulating through the same feeds, a scare video tagged #kidneyfailure.

The first randomized trial of creatine in menopausal women landed within the last year. Thirty-six women, four groups, eight weeks, a different form of creatine than the monohydrate in most tubs. That nuance survives in none of the marketing built on it.

So the biggest new buyer this category has seen in years walks in asking "is this for someone like me, at my age, at what dose?" and brands that spent two decades earning shelf space answer with the same tub, the same scoop, the same guy.

She isn't waiting. She's taking her answers from scientists, morning shows, and whoever scares her first.

A market gets narrated once, when it's new. The brands are letting everyone else hold the pen.
