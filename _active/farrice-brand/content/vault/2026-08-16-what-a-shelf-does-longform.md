# What a shelf does to your message

Every DTC supplement brand I know wants to be on a shelf. Almost none of them know what a shelf is going to do to what they say.

Nobody walks a founder through this before the first PO.

**A website has infinite surface. A box has a front panel.**

On your own site you never have to choose. The clinical study gets a page. So does the sourcing story, the founder's origin, the ingredient glossary. Every claim you have ever wanted to make can coexist, because scrolling is free and nobody is standing behind you in the aisle.

A retail package has one front panel and roughly four seconds. Something like ninety-five percent of what you have written about your product does not make the trip. What matters is who does the cutting.

In most brands, nobody does. The packaging designer inherits a folder of claims and picks what fits. And when what fits is thin, the price tag ends up doing the talking.

**What compresses, and what does not**

Some claims survive the trip to a physical shelf and some do not, and it has almost nothing to do with how true they are.

Compresses well: a number, a format, a flavour, a borrowed brand you already trust, an adjective nobody can check. Compresses badly: a clinical trial, a sourcing chain, a mechanism, a caveat, anything that needs a second sentence to be honest.

You can watch this run in both directions this month.

One brand went into every Costco in the country carrying its own published clinical work, and what the shelf actually communicates is the price.

Another licensed Sour Patch Kids and Swedish Fish from Mondelēz and put them into roughly 2,200 Walmart doors and 1,300 GNC doors, which means it decided not to make the buyer believe anything new at all. It borrowed belief that already existed and skipped the compression problem entirely.

The second one is a real answer to a real constraint, and it is a decision, which is more than most brands bring to the shelf.

**Two prices for one product**

There is one more thing retail does, and it is the one that catches founders off guard.

Once your product is on a shelf, it has two prices.

Club and mass channels run on larger packs at a lower cost per unit, which is simply the economics of the door, and everyone entering pays it. Costco caps its own markup at roughly fourteen percent on branded goods, makes most of its profit on membership rather than margin, and asks suppliers for a bigger pack that ships and displays efficiently.

So the outcome is arithmetic rather than strategy: the shelf price per day is going to be lower than your subscription price per day.

Which means, on the morning of your retail launch, your most loyal customer quietly becomes your most expensive customer.

She will find out. She will find out from a pallet, or a friend, or your own comparison page. And in most brands there is no sentence anywhere that tells her what she is still buying.

**The three sentences to write before the PO**

Three things I would want written down before a retail deal closes.

*The shelf sentence.* The one claim that survives onto the front panel, chosen on purpose. If you cannot name it, your packaging designer is about to choose it for you.

*The subscription sentence.* What the subscription is for now that the product is available cheaper elsewhere. It has to be something a pallet structurally cannot do: cadence, timing, guidance, the ability to change what you send someone at day thirty when the shelf can only ever send the same box.

*Reconciling them.* Read the two out loud, back to back. If they contradict each other, you have not finished. If the second one is the first one with the word "convenient" added, you have not started.

**Why this is a message problem and not a pricing problem**

The instinct, when someone raises this, is to reach for pricing. Match the club price. Add a loyalty tier. Bundle something.

That treats a positioning question as a discount question, and discounts are the one move that confirms the customer's suspicion: if the only thing you can offer is a better price, then price was the only thing you were ever offering.

The brands that handle this well do something less expensive and much harder. They decide, in one sentence, what each channel is for, and they publish it before anyone has to ask.

Four seconds, one panel, one shopper who is never going to read your website. Somebody is choosing what that panel says this week. It should be you.
