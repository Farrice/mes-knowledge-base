# The Proof Chain, entry 2: the claim with a clock on it

Every claim on your label has a moment when it was true.

For most claims that moment is permanent. Third-party tested is a fact about an event that already happened. Made in an NSF registered facility stays true while the registration holds. Those claims do not decay, which is why nobody thinks about them in terms of time.

A dose is different, and the category has not noticed.

A dose is a measurement, and a measurement belongs to the instant it was taken. Everything after that instant is a bet on stability. In a dry powder in a sealed tub, that bet is close to free. In a wet matrix it is a real bet with a real clock, and in some chemistries the clock runs fast.

There is a federal class action running right now against a creatine gummy brand. What is unusual about it is that the defendant looks careful.

Every production order goes through Eurofins. The product is NSF Certified for Sport. The company says its own testing showed the plaintiff's units at or above label. And the complaint does not allege a plant that lied. The allegation is chemistry: creatine reacts with the water inside a gummy and degrades over time.

Read that as a marketing problem rather than a legal one and it gets interesting. Both parties can be describing something true. A number measured at production and a number measured months later are two different numbers, and the label has room for one of them.

Which means the claim your buyer compares you on is the only claim on the package with a shelf life, and you have never printed the shelf life.

Buyers have already started checking it without a laboratory. One of them, looking at a gummy pack, wrote: when three bears weighs five grams and they claim to have five grams of creatine, the math doesn't add up. That is mass balance done at a kitchen counter, and it is now part of how this category gets evaluated.

Four questions. You can run them this week on your own label.

**One. Which of our claims are events, and which are measurements?** Certifications, facility registrations and testing programmes are events. Doses, potencies and actives per serving are measurements. Sort every claim on the pack into the two piles.

**Two. For every measurement, at what point was it measured?** Blend, fill, release, or end of shelf life. If nobody in the building can answer that in one sentence per claim, that is the finding, not the homework.

**Three. What is the honest range across our stated shelf life, and do we overage for it?** Most competent manufacturers do. Almost nobody says so anywhere a buyer can see it, which means the most reassuring true thing about your product is invisible.

**Four. If our number moved, would our position survive?** This is the one that decides your next creative round. If the whole reason to choose you was the figure, the position was never stable, and a competitor's label revision would have taken it anyway.

The reason this matters beyond compliance is that the category has quietly agreed to compete on the least durable thing it owns. Two products on this month's new-product sheet lead with ten grams of creatine, and one positions itself explicitly against everyone at five or less. That is a real strategy for about a quarter.

On the same sheet, one launch did not raise a number at all. It named three million people who work through the night and said, in public, that they are not a niche.

A competitor can print a bigger number next month. It is considerably harder to take a named person and a named moment away from you.

Sort your claims into events and measurements. Then look hard at what you have been asking a measurement to carry.
