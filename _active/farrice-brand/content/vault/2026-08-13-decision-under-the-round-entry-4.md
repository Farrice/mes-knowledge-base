# The Decision Under The Round, entry 4: the ads that last are the ones somebody argued about

A brand ran a Facebook ad in September 2024 with a line in it that worked. "No ultra-processed pills, no shakes, just real food," attributed to an investor with a large audience.

A professor of nutrition complained.

The regulator agreed with him, on the grounds that two ingredients in the product had been through more than minimal processing. An appeal followed. This March it was rejected. Zoe's co-founder called the decision a victory for pedantry over public health, and he may be right about the science.

He can be right about the science and still have lost on sequence. The sentence that made the ad was the sentence the proof could not reach, and nobody found that out until a regulator did.

That is the shape I keep seeing, and it explains something in this category that looks like a contradiction.

Somebody pulled the 500 strongest supplement ads on Meta this year and measured how long each one survived. Median: 394 days. Their same study a year earlier put the top performer average at 215.

Meanwhile the operating advice in this exact market is to refresh every two to three weeks and run 30 to 80 new creatives a month.

Those two numbers can live in the same market because they describe different objects.

The frame below is the whole of this entry.

**Every ad stands on three layers.**

**Layer one is the wrapper.** Format, actor, edit, opening frame, music. A variant test changes this and nothing else. Cheap, measurable, and in most accounts the only layer anybody tracks. Wrappers do genuinely fatigue, and two to three weeks is about right for one.

**Layer two, the promise.** What the buyer is told will change, and by when. Semi-durable. It survives a dozen wrappers and fails only when the market's experience contradicts it, which reaches you through a churn export three months later rather than through Friday's dashboard.

**Underneath both sits the layer that does not refresh: the decision.** What the product is allowed to claim. Who has to believe it. What evidence defends that when somebody pushes. Settled or not settled, with nothing in between.

An ad running for 394 days is standing on a settled third layer. An ad you kill at week three is usually layer-one churn over an empty one. Nobody decided anything, so there was nothing to defend, so the only thing left to change was the wrapper.

Now the part specific to supplements, and the reason this category is worse at the third layer than most.

In the same 500-ad study, the opener most associated with running long is the problem callout, where the ad names what is wrong before it mentions the product. It appears in 4.2% of the ads, and almost nobody quotes that number. The largest single category, at 33.2%, is ads that open with no verbal hook at all.

Taste is not what drove the category there. Naming a problem in a supplement is a claim decision before it is a copy decision.

Law lets you say how the product affects the normal structure or function of the body. It stops at any implication that you diagnose, mitigate, treat, cure or prevent something, and the FDA clears neither the claim nor the product before you run it.

So the honest strong sentence sits right against a boundary, the review comes back with it removed, and over a few years an industry learns that the safest opener is a jar on a countertop.

Then it called the result creative fatigue and bought more production.

**What to do with this on Thursday.** Take your single best-performing ad and fill three columns.

1. What problem does it name, in the buyer's words, before it mentions the product?
2. By when does it tell him he should notice something?
3. If a regulator, a retail buyer or a sceptical customer pushes on that, which specific evidence holds?

An empty first column means you are looking at a wrapper. Leave the second blank and your churn stops being a product problem and starts being a promise problem. A blank third column is the Zoe problem, which arrives on somebody else's timeline rather than yours.

Two caveats, because this is the kind of argument that gets over-claimed.

The survival study is one firm's self-published analysis of what its own tool indexes as top ads. Its problem-callout finding rests on sixteen ads, which the authors say themselves. Treat problem-first openers as a cheap experiment rather than a promise.

The Zoe ruling is also UK, from the ASA, and it does not bind a US brand. The mechanism transfers even though the jurisdiction does not.

None of that touches the part you can check yourself. Open the ad library, find three competitors, read the start dates.

Every ad you kill this quarter is a decision somebody deferred.

If you want those three columns filled for your next round, with the problem each angle is allowed to name and the proof that holds it, DM ANGLE.
