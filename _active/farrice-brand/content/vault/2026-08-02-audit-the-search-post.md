Twenty-two percent of supplement uses have no human trial behind them. The man who published that number spent this weekend proving himself wrong.

He runs an evidence site. He mapped 2,156 supplement-and-goal pairings against published trials, put a number on the gap, and gave the dataset a DOI.

Then he went back and checked the empty bucket, because a number that convenient deserves suspicion.

475 pairings had scored zero. Of those, 277 had at least one real trial and 66 had ten or more. Protein for osteoporosis, scored zero, has 104.

Two reasons, both boring. His pipeline searched a capped sample of records instead of querying the library per pairing. And his cleanup counted Citrulline and L-Citrulline as two different compounds, so one scored 0 and the other scored 9. Same molecule. Two rows. Disagreeing with each other.

This is a claims review.

Some Tuesday a team pulls the evidence on their own product, comes back thin, and softens everything. The softened copy ships and reads like a brand with nothing to say.

Nobody audits the search. They just quietly stop claiming things.

A real absence and a broken query look identical in a spreadsheet. One means the science is not there. The other means you could not find proof you already owned, and you paid for it twice: once for the study, once for the sentence you were too careful to write.

His words, not mine: a tidy percentage with a citation looks the same whether the pipeline underneath it is sound or garbage.

He published his own correction. That is why he is the most credible person in his category this week.
