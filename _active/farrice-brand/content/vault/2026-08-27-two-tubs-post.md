# Two tubs

A guy at my counter was holding two tubs, one in each hand.

He was not reading the ingredient panel. He was reading two numbers. One said 5 grams. The other said 10. He looked up wanting permission, not information.

I sold him the 10. I had nothing better to point at, and neither did the label.

This morning's trade launch sheet has two new creatine products at 10 grams on it. One of them states its position in plain text: most products on the market offer doses of 5 grams or less.

Standing on a number costs you three times.

Your competitor prints a bigger one by Tuesday. That is a label revision and a purchase order, and the differentiation is gone with no meeting required.

Your buyer already printed it himself, for free, a year ago. Read what people actually write about high dose creatine. "There's no harm in getting a little extra." "I decided to try out 10g because I saw a YouTube video of a guy who looked hotter than me." Nobody in those threads is discussing anybody's packaging. They took a second scoop, and the only person who got paid was the creator.

And the number moves on its own.

There is a class action running in the Southern District of New York against a well funded creatine brand that is NSF Certified for Sport and tests every production run through Eurofins, because a lab found about ten percent less in the gummy than the label says.

The complaint's own theory is that creatine reacts with the water inside the gummy and breaks down over time. Both sides can be telling the truth. A number can be right when it is tested and smaller when it is eaten.

A dose is a claim with a clock on it, and nobody prints the clock.

On the same launch sheet, one brand did something else. It did not raise a number. It named three million people who work through the night and said they are not a niche, they are a large identifiable audience.

Nobody matches that with a label revision.

Before your team argues about the dose, settle what the product is for and who has to believe it.
