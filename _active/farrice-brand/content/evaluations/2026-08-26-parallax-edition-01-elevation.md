## I was told to pick one thing. I picked all of them.

Somewhere around the $14,000 mark in courses I'd never finish, a business coach looked me in the eye and said, “Farrice, you need to niche down.”

He said it the way you'd tell someone to put down the fork. Like my interests were a problem to be managed. Like the fact that I could hold a conversation about Neville Goddard's imagination doctrine, break down the biomechanics of a Romanian deadlift, and explain why Goku's training arc on King Kai's planet is the most accurate metaphor for mastery I've ever encountered. Like all of that was *noise*.

I nodded. I paid him. I tried.

I spent two years trying to be one thing.

---

A person who sees everything, forced to look through a single lens? You go blind in the other eyes.

I built a coaching business around “just fitness.” I buried the psychology. I hid the spirituality. I never once mentioned anime to a client. I performed a version of myself that was marketable, digestible, and, if I'm being honest, dead on arrival.

The content I created from that narrow place? It worked fine. Got engagement. Got likes. Got nothing that made me want to wake up and write the next thing.

The part they leave out about niching down? When it works, it's a cage with better lighting. You're not free. You're just trapped somewhere people clap for you.

---

The advice to “pick one thing” is built on a lie. The lie says: *people can only understand you if you're simple enough to fit in their mental filing cabinet.*

And it's true for products. Nobody wants a Swiss Army knife when they need a scalpel.

But you're not a product. You're a person. And the paradox of “pick one thing” is that the moment you obey it, you start performing instead of expressing. You curate instead of create. You become the person the algorithm wants, and you lose the person your actual work needs.

I've spent 18 years in human psychology, coaching, and personal transformation. And the single most consistent pattern I've seen across thousands of people is this: **the ones who are struggling hardest are the ones pretending to be less than they are.**

Not the scattered ones. Not the confused ones. The *performers*. The people who picked one thing, got good at it, and then couldn't figure out why success felt like a costume they couldn't take off.

---

When you truly know yourself. Not the LinkedIn version. Not the “niche” version. The full, contradictory, inconvenient whole of yourself. Something shifts.

The resistance drops. Not all of it. But the friction that comes from fighting your own nature? Gone. The energy you were spending on performance gets redirected to expression. And expression, done honestly, is the most magnetic force in any market.

Jun Yuh says “you are the niche.” He's right, but it goes deeper than personal branding. It goes to the bone.

Every spiritual tradition I've studied says the same thing in different language: separation is the illusion. Duality is the trap. The work isn't to become something new. It's to stop pretending you're less than what you already are.

Which sounds like woo until you watch it play out in a content calendar.

The creators people actually become obsessed with? The ones who build audiences that stay for *years*? They're not specialists. They're fully realized humans who gave themselves permission to be everything they are, in public, without apology. And when you encounter that kind of wholeness, where someone's flaws and contradictions and weird interests all exist in the same frame, your brain doesn't file them in a cabinet. It falls in love with them.

Not because they're performing wholeness. Because they stopped pretending to be less.

---

So this is Parallax.

Named for what happens when you look at the same object from multiple positions. You see depth that a single vantage point can never reveal.

This is a newsletter for the people who were never meant to fit in one box. The multi-passionate ones. The ones with a browser of 47 tabs and a bookshelf that makes no sense to anyone else. The ones who've been told their range is a liability when it's actually the most valuable thing they own.

Every edition, I'll write what I actually think. About AI. About consciousness. About what video games taught me that business school couldn't. About fatherhood, and the gap between knowing what to do and doing it. About building systems that let you be present for dinner at 5 and still create work that matters.

And every edition will come with a prompt pack. Not a motivational worksheet. A set of AI prompts engineered from 18 years of coaching psychology that give you a micro-transformation you can run yourself. In 15 minutes. Something shifts.

This is the deal: I write from the intersection of everything I am. You show up with everything you are. And between us, we create the depth that a single perspective can never reach.

---

**What I believe (and what it costs me to say it):**

I believe your interests aren't scattered. They're ingredients, and nobody told you the recipe yet.

I believe the “pick one thing” advice has produced more silent suffering in talented people than almost any other business gospel of the last decade.

I believe inner work isn't woo. It's the prerequisite that every productivity guru conveniently skipped.

I believe AI is the most powerful thinking partner most people are using as a fancy autocomplete, and that changes when you build with intention instead of defaulting to convenience.

I believe Goku trained harder and more honestly than Gary Vee ever will.

I believe that everything I create is a letter to my son. Proof that you can be all of who you are and still be the kind of father who shows up for bedtime every night.

And I believe that if you understood every word in this newsletter, we should be friends.

# Take B — blank page

## The things I stopped saying

For two years, I made my interests wait outside the room.

Fitness could come in. Psychology could come in if it wore gym clothes. Spirituality stayed quiet. Anime never made it past the door.

This was after I'd spent somewhere around $14,000 on courses trying to become easier to understand. A business coach finally gave the whole project a name.

“Farrice, you need to niche down.”

He said it the way you'd tell someone to put down the fork.

So I did.

The business became clearer. The content got engagement. People knew where to place me.

And I could not hear myself in any of it.

That is a strange kind of success: the work performs, people clap, and the person who made it slowly disappears. You can stay there for years because nothing looks broken from the outside.

I did.

The mistake was subtle. I had confused a clear offer with a smaller identity.

A product should solve one recognizable problem. A person has to draw from enough of a life to solve it in a way only they could.

Those are different requirements.

Nobody wants a Swiss Army knife when they need a scalpel. But nobody asks the surgeon to forget everything except the incision, either. The judgment behind the cut comes from everything the hand has learned.

Mine came from 18 years of coaching people through the distance between what they said they wanted and what they could make themselves do. It came from spirituality, from fatherhood, from systems, from video games, from watching Goku train on King Kai's planet and recognizing a more honest theory of mastery than most business books had given me.

Separately, those things looked scattered.

Together, they explained how I think.

That is what Parallax means.

Move your head a few inches and the object appears to shift. The object never moved. Your second vantage point gave it depth.

For years I treated each interest as a reason people might misunderstand me. Now I think the misunderstanding came from showing them one angle at a time and pretending each angle was the whole object.

You may have done the same.

Your browser has 47 tabs open because your mind is not waiting for permission to organize itself into a content pillar. Your bookshelf makes no sense to anyone else because nobody else has lived the life that makes those books belong together. The interest you stopped mentioning may not have been a distraction.

It may have been the missing vantage point.

Parallax is where I put mine back in the room.

I'll write about AI, consciousness, video games, fatherhood, psychology, and the gap between knowing what to do and doing it. I will not pretend the connections exist before I find them. I will follow them far enough to see whether they reveal something a single subject could not.

Every edition comes with a prompt pack built from 18 years of coaching psychology: five prompts, fifteen minutes, one shift you can test in your own life. The first is called **The Interest Audit**. It helps you stop sorting your interests into useful and embarrassing long enough to see what they may be trying to make together.

Everything I create is also a letter to my son. I want him to have proof that a whole person can make clear work. That you can show up for bedtime without leaving the rest of yourself outside the door.

If you understood every reference in this letter, we should be friends.

And if one of them made you feel seen, hit reply and tell me the thing you stopped talking about because it did not fit your brand.

### Take B theory of surpassing

The original argues for wholeness through a manifesto structure. Take B tries to *demonstrate* wholeness through one governing image: interests waiting outside a room, then returning as vantage points. It narrows the philosophical exposition, makes the offer/identity distinction explicit, and gives Parallax a cleaner reason to exist.

### Take B trade

More narrative unity and a clearer business distinction; less of the original's exuberant “I contain multitudes” energy and fewer standalone quotable beliefs.
