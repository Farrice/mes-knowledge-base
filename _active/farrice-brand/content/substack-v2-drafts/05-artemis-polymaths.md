# Edition 05: They Sent Polymaths to the Moon

**Publication**: Parallax
**Subject line**: NASA didn't send specialists to the Moon. They sent multi-class builds.
**Preview text**: Every crew member on Artemis II is proof that "pick one thing" is a lie.
**CTA type**: Share/Forward
**Prompt pack**: The Moonshot Map (4 prompts)
**Arc position**: Edition 5 — Permission + Inspiration

---

On April 1st, 2026, four human beings left Earth's atmosphere and pointed themselves at the Moon.

I watched the launch with JJ on my lap. He's too young to understand what he was looking at. I'm old enough to understand that I was crying and pretending I wasn't.

Christina Koch. Victor Glover. Reid Wiseman. Jeremy Hansen. The Artemis II crew. First woman to fly to the Moon. First Black man. First Canadian. Every headline focused on who they are. I couldn't stop thinking about *what* they are.

Because NASA didn't send specialists to the Moon. They sent polymaths.

---

Christina Koch is an electrical engineer AND a physicist. She's contributed to instruments that measure cosmic rays and she's also done research at Antarctic field stations in conditions that would break most people's will to continue being scientists. She didn't pick one thing. She picked the intersection of several things and went so deep into that intersection that NASA trusted her with a trip around the Moon.

Victor Glover is a Navy test pilot AND an engineer AND a combat veteran who flew 24 missions in Iraq AND a guy who got a master's in flight test engineering AND a military operational art and science researcher. He's also the first Black man to live on the International Space Station for an extended stay. When asked about making history, he said: "It's not Black history, not women's history. It's human history."

Most people think test pilots just fly planes. They don't. Test pilots don't just fly planes. They fly planes that might not work yet, record what happens, translate that data into engineering feedback, and then fly the revised version. They are pilots AND engineers AND scientists AND risk managers simultaneously. The job is definitionally multi-disciplinary. You literally cannot do it by picking one thing.

Jeremy Hansen is a Canadian fighter pilot AND an engineer AND a former member of the Royal Military College. And the detail that stopped me: he was the first Canadian astronaut to be assigned a spaceflight in 15 years. Fifteen years of training without a mission. Training in underwater simulations, in geological fieldwork, in Russian language, in robotics. Cross-training across a dozen disciplines for a decade and a half before anyone said "yes, go."

That's a training arc. If you know, you know.

---

I want to talk about party composition for a second.

In any RPG worth playing (Final Fantasy, Baldur's Gate, even old-school D&D) the most dangerous party isn't four warriors. It's the group that covers every skill tree. A tank, a healer, a mage, a rogue. Each member brings a different toolkit. The party's power isn't in any individual specialty. It's in the coverage.

You know what a multi-class character is? A character who levels up in more than one class simultaneously. They'll never be as high-level in any single class as a specialist. But they can do things no specialist can. They can heal AND fight. They can sneak AND cast. The flexibility is the power.

The niche-down advice says: be a level 20 warrior. Max out one skill tree. Go deep.

NASA looked at the most important crewed mission of the 21st century, a trip around the Moon where equipment failure could mean death 250,000 miles from the nearest hospital, and they said: give us the multi-class builds.

They didn't want four people who were each the best at one thing. They wanted four people who were each *really good at several things*. Because when something goes wrong at lunar distance, you can't call a specialist. You need the person sitting next to you to be a pilot AND an engineer AND a scientist AND someone who can improvise a solution with whatever tools happen to be on the spacecraft.

Range wasn't a nice-to-have. Range was the mission requirement.

---

I don't know what your Moon is.

Maybe it's the business you keep almost starting. Maybe it's the creative project you've shelved because it doesn't fit your professional brand. Maybe it's the career pivot that everyone keeps telling you is "too late" because you're 34 or 38 or 42 and supposedly committed to one trajectory.

Whatever it is, I want you to hold this in your head: the most important crewed space mission of our generation was staffed entirely by people who refused to be one thing.

---

I have to be honest about why this launch hit me the way it did.

I'm biracial. I have never, not once in 37 years, fit cleanly into one box. Not racially, not professionally, not culturally. I've been too Black for some rooms and not Black enough for others. Too much of a trainer to be taken seriously as a strategist. Too spiritual for the tech people. Too technical for the spiritual people. Too many interests for any single label to hold.

Watching Victor Glover orbit the Moon. A Black man who is also an engineer who is also a pilot who is also a combat veteran who is also a researcher. Watching him refuse the narrow story and insist on the human one, "It's human history." Something unlocked in me that I didn't know was still locked.

Because if polymaths are trusted to go to the Moon, maybe the rest of us can stop apologizing for refusing to shrink.

---

This was supposed to be a five-edition launch week. A quick intro. Build some trust. See if anyone shows up.

But writing these has done something to me I didn't plan for. The act of putting all of who I am into one publication. The psychology and the gaming and the fitness and the fatherhood and the AI and the spirituality and the inconvenient opinions. Publishing that without hedging, without niching, without trimming the weird parts...

It's the thing I've been telling you to do. And it turns out the advice hits different when you're the one taking it.

So here's where we are.

Parallax will keep publishing. Weekly. Every edition: an essay from the intersection of everything I'm thinking about, plus a prompt pack that gives you a tool to work with, not just a thought to consume.

If this week meant something to you. If even one of these five editions made you feel *seen* in a way that the niche-down, stay-in-your-lane, pick-one-thing internet doesn't. Forward this to one person who needs it. Not for my growth metrics. For theirs. Because the person in your life who has too many interests and too many tabs open and too many ideas that don't fit one pitch deck? They're not broken. They're a multi-class build. And they might need someone to tell them that before the world convinces them otherwise.
