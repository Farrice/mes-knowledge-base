# Edition 03: Filter Babel

**Publication**: Parallax
**Subject line**: The ad my wife and I keep getting served at 11pm.
**Preview text**: What the feed knew about our fears before we said them out loud.
**CTA type**: Reply
**Prompt pack**: The Friction Inventory
**Arc position**: Edition 3 — Pain Recognition

---

The ad has a warm color palette and a tired mom holding a toddler. The headline asks if your kid's outbursts might be caused by a magnesium deficiency. It's for a multivitamin called EllaOla, and it's been following me and my wife around Instagram for about three weeks.

I first got served it at 11pm on a Tuesday, sitting next to Jen on the couch after JJ finally went down. We'd done some anxious-parenting research that afternoon about whether he was eating enough greens. I'm a personal trainer. I think about nutrition for a living. Nothing crazy, just the normal kind of googling parents do at 10:47 on a weeknight.

The ad found us that night. Not generally. Specifically. The headline it showed Jen was about picky eaters. The one it showed me was about behavioral outbursts from micronutrient deficiency, which, for the record, is a real phenomenon that the supplement industry massively overstates, but which lives right in the crack of something I know too much and too little about at the same time.

That's where it hit me.

---

I clicked through. You know the website. Warm palette. Smiling toddler on grass. Three tiers of subscription. Thousands of five-star reviews. The product might genuinely be great. I'm not here to call out EllaOla, I don't know their formulation, and for all I know it's exactly what some kids need.

What kept me up that night was the targeting. The ad knew to lead me with the word "outbursts" because that's the word a dad who studies behavior and nutrition would click on. It knew to lead Jen with "picky eater" because that's the phrase a mom who tracks what JJ eats would click on. Same brand, same product, two totally different headlines. Two audiences in the same household, sitting on the same couch, getting served a version of the same ad customized to our specific angle of worry.

Jen and I have a running joke that our phones are listening to us. We don't actually think they are. We think something, and "listening" is the closest word we have for it.

The phones don't need to listen. We've been writing everything down for them. Every scroll, every pause, the tabs we leave open at 11pm. That's the transcript.

Jen and I aren't on the same internet. Your partner isn't on the same internet you are either. Each of us is in a private version of it, built in real time from the things we click on, slow down for, and pretend we're not reading.

---

Meanwhile JJ is two. He doesn't have a feed.

When he gets upset, he can't swipe to a reel that's shaped like his feelings. He has to actually feel them. Sit in the highchair. Look at the wall. Let the big wave move through his small body. Jen and I have been modeling breathing with him, and I watch him do this thing where he'll start crying about not getting the cup he wanted, then breathe like we showed him, then come out the other side fine. Genuinely fine. The feeling went through him and left.

Jen has a line about kids. "Loud kids feel safe. Quiet kids don't." I think about it a lot.

I was a quiet kid.

---

At JJ's age I was already running a calculation every time I walked into a room. Who's here. What mood are they in. How much of me is safe to bring. I was a fast, smart, biracial kid in a neighborhood that didn't know what to do with a kid who could keep up with the older kids, and I figured out by about six that the grown-ups got edgy when I was at my best. So I got quieter. I poured my energy into video games and anime and movies, because those were the only rooms where I could be all the way on and nobody flinched.

You know what makes EllaOla's ad work? Not the copy. Not the design. It works because when I was six, the adults in my life responded to my best self by getting uncomfortable, and I learned to read the signals that made people react, and I got really good at reading those signals, and now I'm thirty-seven and the algorithm is using the exact same skill set I built as a kid to sell me a vitamin my son probably doesn't need.

That's the part that gets me.

---

I don't think the answer is throwing the phone in a lake. I tried that once metaphorically and it didn't work.

What I think now is closer to what I'm trying to do with JJ. Manufacture friction on purpose.

Sit in a room with a person who disagrees with me. Actually sit. Don't scroll through the discomfort. Watch an argument between me and Jen play out in front of JJ instead of pretending we never disagree. Let him see the repair too. Argued, worked it out, fine. I want him to grow up with that version of real.

Keep a friend who thinks I'm wrong about something I care about. Read a book whose premise annoys me before I've finished the first page. Let myself get bored the way a two-year-old gets bored. Boredom is the last unmediated state left. An algorithm can't follow you in there.

---

JJ doesn't have a phone yet. He has us. And right now us includes two people who finally noticed how much of our own nervous system we've been handing over to a system that learned how to sell it back to us at 11pm on a Tuesday.

One prompt. Three questions. What you get at the end is a short list of the fears, beliefs, and recent purchases you can't quite prove you arrived at on your own. It shows you which rooms in your head were decorated by an algorithm, and which were yours.
