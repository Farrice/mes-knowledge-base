# Edition 04: Digital Nicotine

**Publication**: Parallax
**Subject line**: A jury just proved what your midnight doom-scroll already knew.
**Preview text**: The same reward loop that hooks you on loot boxes is running your feed.
**CTA type**: Reply
**Prompt pack**: The Attention Audit (4 prompts)
**Arc position**: Edition 4 — Pain + Awareness

---

On March 25th, a jury in Los Angeles awarded $6 million to a 20-year-old who had been addicted to Instagram and YouTube since he was six.

Six years old. 2012. I was 23 and grinding through personal training certifications, and this kid was already being fed into the machine.

The jury found Meta and YouTube negligent. Not just negligent. *Designed to addict*. Internal documents showed Meta executives tracking strategies to, and I'm quoting the filings here, "bring in tweens." Not "reach younger audiences." Not "expand the user base." Bring. In. Tweens. The way a dealer talks about a new block.

The phrase going around now is "digital nicotine." The press is calling it Big Tech's Big Tobacco moment. Over 2,000 similar lawsuits are pending.

And all I could think, reading that verdict, was: *we already knew this.*

Every single one of us already knew.

---

I should lose the right to be self-righteous about any of this.

I built Antigravity (a system of over 100 AI expert agents) in part because I noticed I was spending 2-3 hours a night scrolling content that was making me dumber. Not morally dumber. Cognitively dumber. My attention span was shrinking in real-time and I could feel it happening, the way you feel your grip weakening on a pull-up bar. You know you're slipping. You can't stop slipping.

I'm also starting a newsletter that publishes to the internet. I'm building an audience on LinkedIn. I'm asking people to pay attention to me.

So let's sit in that irony for a second. A man writing about attention theft is asking for your attention. That tension is real and I'm not going to pretend it isn't.

The difference, and this is the only difference that matters, is consent. You opted into this email. You can opt out in one click. Your feed never gave you that choice. Your feed was engineered by people who studied the same psychological mechanisms I studied for 18 years, except they pointed them at your limbic system instead of your growth.

---

Even if you've never touched a gacha game or opened a loot box, you've experienced what I'm about to describe. You just experienced it on a different screen.

Variable reward schedules. That's the term. B.F. Skinner figured this out with pigeons in the 1950s: if you make the reward unpredictable, sometimes you get the rare drop, sometimes you get nothing, the behavior becomes compulsive. Not habitual. *Compulsive*. The pigeon keeps pecking even when the food stops coming entirely.

Slot machines run on this. Every game with loot boxes runs on this. Destiny, Genshin Impact, FIFA Ultimate Team. The mechanic is the same. You pull the lever. Maybe you get the exotic engram. Maybe you don't. The uncertainty is the product.

Now look at your social media feed.

Sometimes the next scroll is a post that makes you laugh so hard you screenshot it. Sometimes it's engagement bait that makes you want to throw your phone. Sometimes it's a photo of someone living the life you're saving for. You never know which one is next. That's not a bug. That's the loot box.

Your feed is a slot machine that pays out in dopamine and charges you in hours you didn't agree to spend.

I spent almost two decades studying how dopamine loops work in fitness. The pump. The endorphin hit after a hard session. The way progressive overload creates a chemical reward for showing up. Those loops are beautiful. They build something. Your body adapts. You get stronger. The reward is tethered to reality.

The dopamine loop in your feed builds nothing. It's a treadmill bolted to the floor of a room with no windows. You're running. You feel like you're going somewhere. You are not going anywhere.

---

Now it gets spiritual, and I don't mean that as decoration.

Every contemplative tradition I've encountered says the same thing in different words. Neville Goddard, the Stoics, Buddhist attention practices, even the clinical literature on flow states: **what you attend to, you become.**

Not metaphorically. Literally. Your neural pathways physically restructure around your dominant inputs. Attention isn't a resource you "spend." It's a mold that shapes you. The question isn't whether social media is good or bad. The question is: *what is it shaping you into?*

When I look at JJ. He's a toddler. He doesn't have a phone. We're years away from this fight. I still feel the weight of it. Because the landscape he's growing into was engineered by people who see his future attention as a revenue line item. That keeps me up more than any midnight scroll ever did.

And I know I'm not the only parent thinking about this. But I also know it's not just a parenting problem. It's an everyone problem. Because the same 35-year-old who worries about screen time for their kids is losing 90 minutes every night to a feed that was designed the same way as the game their kid won't put down.

---

I'm not anti-social media. That would be hypocritical and stupid.

I'm anti-unconscious-use.

There's a version of this where you check LinkedIn with intention, engage with people who sharpen your thinking, and close the app. There's a version where you use YouTube to learn compound lifts or watch a breakdown of Evangelion's psychological framework and then you're done.

And then there's the version where you pick up your phone to check one notification and surface 40 minutes later in a part of the internet you didn't choose, watching a stranger react to another stranger's hot take about a thing you didn't care about 41 minutes ago.

The first version is a tool. The second version is a tool using you.

The jury in LA put a dollar amount on that distinction: $6 million. But the real cost is the thing money can't calculate. The hours. The creative energy. The presence you didn't bring to the conversation with your kid or your partner because some algorithm decided what you needed to see next.

---

The same influencers telling you to post 3x a day and "build in public" and "stay visible" are participating in the machine. I say that as someone who is about to tell you to go run the prompt pack below. I'm in the machine too. The question isn't how to escape it. The question is how to stop letting it run you.

The prompt pack for this edition is called **The Attention Audit**. Four prompts. Fifteen minutes. You'll map where your attention actually goes, identify which apps are using variable reward schedules on you, build an intentional attention budget, and reclaim one hour.

One hour doesn't sound like much. But one hour a day, redirected from consumption to creation, is 365 hours a year. That's fifteen 24-hour days. That's a book. A business. A skill that compounds. A presence your family can feel.

A jury proved what you already knew. The question is what you do with the knowing.
