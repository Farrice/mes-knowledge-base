# Edition 04: The Coachella Reckoning

**Publication**: Parallax
**Subject line**: I watched 90,000 people perform being there.
**Preview text**: The Bieber drag was a mirror nobody wanted to look into.
**CTA type**: Reply
**Prompt pack**: The Presence Audit (1 prompt)
**Arc position**: Edition 4 — Power Demonstration

---

The girl in front of me at Travis Scott's set screamed "THIS IS MY SONG" and then filmed the entire thing through her phone without looking up once.

I know because I watched her. For four minutes. I was supposed to be watching Travis. But I got hypnotized by the specific way she angled the camera so her face was in the corner of the frame. She wasn't watching him either. She was watching the version of herself that would be watching him later. And somehow, by some physics I still can't explain, neither of those people were actually there.

Then the drop hit. She yelled. The phone stayed up. I realized I was doing the same thing, just without the phone. Taking mental snapshots. Composing the story I would tell my wife on the drive home. Already editing the weekend into a highlight reel while the weekend was still happening.

---

By Sunday, my Twitter feed had decided Justin Bieber's set was "phoned in." Lazy. Disrespectful. Gen Z was furious. He stood there, they said. He wasn't really *present*. He was performing the idea of performing.

Here's the thing nobody wanted to say.

Neither was anyone else.

---

I walked through the main field at 2am Saturday, after Olivia Rodrigo, when the desert gets cold in that specific way that makes your skin feel thin. The art installations were glowing. The playa dust was in everyone's hair. And I counted — actually counted, because I'm a freak like that — the number of people walking past me with their phones pointed at their own faces versus the number with their phones pointed at anything else.

It was 4 to 1. For their own faces.

Not capturing the moment. Not documenting a friend. Performing a version of being there, to an audience that wasn't there, for a feeling they would have later, about a night they weren't actually living.

The Bieber drag was projection. He just happened to be the one on stage when a whole generation noticed their own reflection and didn't like what they saw.

---

There's a concept in psychology called *dissociation*. Most people think it means the dramatic thing — trauma survivors going blank, losing time, watching themselves from the ceiling. But the clinical definition is wider than that. Dissociation is any break between the person doing the experience and the person having it.

Filming a song you love so you can feel that you loved it later is a form of dissociation.

Shouting "I love this song" to a song you don't know, because the person next to you is shouting it, is a form of dissociation.

Composing the caption in your head while the set is still playing is a form of dissociation.

And here's the part that wrecked me: I've been doing it for years. Not at Coachella. At dinner with JJ. In conversations with my wife. Watching sunsets with a camera already half-raised. The phone is just the visible symptom. The real condition is that we've trained our nervous systems to experience life one layer removed — always composing, always narrating, always half-present and half-in-the-post-about-it.

Performance of presence has become the default operating system. And you don't even feel it happening until something cracks the frame.

---

Here is what cracked mine.

Friday night, Bad Bunny. About ninety minutes in, he went into a section where the production dropped back and it was just him and the crowd on "DtMF." The woman next to me — mid-fifties, Latina, eyes closed, tears on her face — was mouthing every word without making a sound. No phone. No performance. Her whole body was moving in the way bodies move when nobody is watching, because for her, in that moment, nobody was.

I watched her for maybe ten seconds and then I put my phone away and I didn't touch it again for two hours.

Something came back online. I can't describe it except to say the music got *louder*, even though the volume hadn't changed. The colors on the stage got more saturated. The people around me got more textured — actual faces, not a crowd. My body stopped narrating and started listening.

The Buddhists call this *witness consciousness*. The state where you are no longer the person having the experience or the person watching the experience — you're just the experience itself. No distance. No commentary. No highlight reel running in parallel.

Goku calls it Ultra Instinct. Same thing, different language. The moment the narrator voice shuts up and the body just *is*.

And in that state, the music was mine in a way it had not been mine when I was trying to capture it.

---

The people who got the most out of Coachella 2026 were not the ones with the best content. They were the ones who let themselves disappear into it. Who were willing to have an experience that no one else would ever see. Whose presence was so total they forgot to document it.

That's the whole game. That's what Bieber fumbled and what the crowd fumbled right back.

Because presence isn't a performance you can rehearse. It's what happens when you stop performing.

---

**What I believe about this, and what it's costing me to say it:**

I believe most of us are not suffering from a lack of experience. We are suffering from a lack of contact with the experiences we're already having.

I believe the attention economy didn't steal our presence. We traded it, voluntarily, for a version of ourselves that looks more interesting than the one living the actual life.

I believe the Bieber drag was a mirror, and the people shouting the loudest were the ones who saw themselves in it and couldn't bear it.

I believe witness consciousness — the capacity to be fully in a moment without narrating it — is the most endangered cognitive resource of our generation.

And I believe the next competitive edge in every domain that matters — creative work, parenting, love, spiritual practice — is going to belong to the people who can still disappear into what they're doing.
