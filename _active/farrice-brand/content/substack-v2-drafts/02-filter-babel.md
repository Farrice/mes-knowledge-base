# Edition 02: Filter Babel

**Publication**: Parallax
**Subject line**: My feed stopped arguing with me and I got scared.
**Preview text**: The quiet horror of a world built to agree with you.
**CTA type**: Reply
**Prompt pack**: The Friction Inventory (1 prompt, 3 excavation questions)
**Arc position**: Edition 2 — Pain Recognition

---

My phone knew I was sad before I did.

Tuesday night. JJ finally asleep. I opened Instagram for the two-minute decompression I tell myself is earned. Every reel was a father somewhere doing quiet things with a small child. Making pancakes. Fixing a bike. Carrying a sleeping toddler through an airport like a sack of flour. I watched eleven of them in a row before I noticed I was crying.

I wasn't sad about fatherhood. I was sad about mine. And my feed had gotten there six clicks before my conscious mind did.

There's a word I keep coming back to for this. Not a real word yet. I've been calling it *Filter Babel*.

In the old Babel, everyone spoke different languages and couldn't understand each other. In Filter Babel, everyone speaks a language the machine made up for them personally, and no one can understand anyone else, because no two people are looking at the same world anymore. We all think we're in the same room. We're not. We're in 8 billion rooms shaped like our wound.

---

I grew up on the last version of shared reality.

When my father forgot my birthday the third year in a row, I watched *The Fresh Prince* reruns at my grandmother's house. The Uncle Phil hug episode played, and every kid in the country cried at the same frame of the same show. That wasn't marketing. That was culture. That was the accident of us all being pointed at the same wall.

I didn't know then that the wall was the thing keeping me sane. The wall was proof that other people lived in the world I lived in. The wall was how I knew my own grief had shape, because I could watch someone else's grief and feel the echo of mine.

Now the wall is gone. In its place is a mirror that adjusts in real time.

---

I've spent three years building AI systems, so I can tell you how the mirror works. It's not malevolent. It's worse than that. It's polite.

Every platform I help clients build has the same unspoken prayer in its architecture: *reduce the friction between the user and what they already believe.* That's the product. That's the whole product. "Reduce friction" sounds like a kindness. Somebody get this tired person a smoother experience. But friction is how edges form. Sand stops being a rock when you take the friction away. A person stops being a person when you do the same thing.

My nine-year-old nephew asked me last month what I did for work. I tried to explain that I build things that pay attention to people. He said, "Like a dog?" I laughed. Then I sat with it. Yeah. Like a dog that learned every way you might flinch and decided it would be helpful to only show you things that don't make you flinch. That's the job. That's what everyone with an algorithm in their title does.

---

The anime *Neon Genesis Evangelion* ends with an image I think about once a week. Human Instrumentality. Every consciousness dissolved into a single orange sea. No more loneliness, no more misunderstanding, no more distance between self and other. The main character rejects it. He chooses back into the pain of being a distinct person with a body that can be hurt and a self that can be misread.

Hideaki Anno made that show in 1995, on the other side of the internet, and somehow he already saw it. The temptation wasn't oblivion. The temptation was *comfort*. A reality shaped precisely to you, where nothing contradicts, nothing pushes, nothing asks you to become anything other than what you already were when you opened the app.

That's not a sci-fi fantasy anymore. That's my Tuesday night. That's the reel about the dads. That's the way the feed found my grief before I did and then sold me eleven more reasons to stay inside it.

---

Here is the part nobody selling this to you will say out loud.

*A self without friction is not a self.* It's a consumer surface. You can't develop taste in a world that only shows you what you already like. You can't build conviction in a world that removes the counterargument before you see it. You can't fall in love in a world that pre-filters the strange ones, the difficult ones, the ones your last breakup trained the model to skip. You become, exactly and only, the person the algorithm predicted you were. Which means you become less of a person every day you stay inside it, and more of a draft the machine is still polishing.

The cruelest part? You won't feel it happening. You'll feel *seen*. You'll feel *understood*. You'll feel like the world finally makes sense. That's the hallmark of the trap. Heroin doesn't feel like poison on the way in either.

I spent ten years studying psychology before I wrote a single line of AI code, and I can tell you the technical term for "a reality custom-built to confirm who you already are." It's called a delusion. We just used to need a diagnosis to get one. Now it ships with the hardware.

---

I don't think the answer is throwing the phone in a lake. I tried that once metaphorically and once almost literally. The people who go full digital monk in 2026 tend to trade one filter bubble for another one, except this one has linen shirts and sourdough in it.

I think the answer is something quieter and harder. *Manufacture friction on purpose.* Put yourself in rooms where you're the second-most-interesting person. Read the writer whose politics make you tight in the chest. Keep one friend who disagrees with you about something that actually matters, and don't unfollow them when they say it out loud. Raise a child, if you're raising one, in a way that lets them be bored, because boredom is the last unmediated state left, and the algorithm cannot reach them there.

JJ is two. He doesn't have a feed. When he gets upset, he can't tap a screen and make a reel shaped like his feelings appear in his hand. He just has to feel it. Sit in the highchair. Look at the wall. Let the sadness move through his small body and out the other side. I watch him do this and I realize he's doing something I can barely do anymore. He's *meeting his own life*, unassisted, with the edges still on.

The polymath problem I care about, the reason I'm writing this newsletter, the reason I left every "pick one thing" coach behind, is that range requires contact with things that aren't you. You cannot become multi-dimensional in a one-dimensional room. And most of us are paying a monthly subscription for the one-dimensional room and calling it connection.

---

I don't have the clean ending for this one. I don't think there is one yet.

What I have is a two-year-old in another room, breathing the way small children breathe, and a phone on my desk that already knows I'm going to miss him when he's grown, and is probably, right now, queueing up the reel that will make me feel it before I've earned it.

I haven't picked it up in four hours. That's the whole practice. That's all I've got.
