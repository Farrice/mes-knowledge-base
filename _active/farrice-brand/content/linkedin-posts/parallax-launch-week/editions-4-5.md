## Edition 4 — Variant A: The Insight Post (Digital Nicotine — Variable Reward Loop)

Your feed is a slot machine.

You just don't see the lever.

B.F. Skinner figured this out with pigeons in the 1950s.

Make the reward unpredictable and the behavior becomes compulsive.

Not habitual. Compulsive.

Slot machines use it. Loot boxes use it. Genshin Impact, FIFA packs, Destiny engrams.

Now look at your feed.

Sometimes the next scroll makes you laugh so hard you screenshot it. Sometimes it's engagement bait that makes you want to throw your phone. Sometimes it's someone living the life you're saving for.

You never know which one comes next.

That's not a bug. That's the loot box.

I spent 18 years studying dopamine loops in fitness. The pump. The endorphin hit after a hard session. Progressive overload creating chemical reward for showing up.

Those loops build something real. Your body adapts. You get stronger.

The dopamine loop in your feed builds nothing.

It's a treadmill bolted to the floor of a room with no windows.

2,000 families are suing Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat right now. 42 state attorneys general filed suit. Internal Meta docs show execs tracking strategies to "bring in tweens."

Not "reach younger audiences." Bring. In. Tweens.

The way a dealer talks about a new block.

We didn't need the lawsuits to know this. We know because it's midnight and we picked up our phone for "one quick check" and surfaced 40 minutes later wondering where the time went.

I'm not anti-social media. That would be hypocritical. I'm literally asking for your attention right now.

I'm anti-unconscious use.

One hour a day redirected from consumption to creation is 365 hours a year. That's a book. A business. A skill that compounds.

I wrote more about this in the latest Parallax edition, including a prompt pack called The Attention Audit that maps exactly where your hours are going.

Link in comments.

What's the app you KNOW is costing you the most, and what would you build with that time back?

**CTA note**: Low-friction, dual-part question. First half is easy to answer (everyone has an app). Second half plants a seed about creation vs. consumption. Drives comments AND forward-looking thinking.

Prompt 3 builds an intentional attention budget. Like a financial budget, but for the thing that's actually more scarce.

Prompt 4 reclaims one hour.

## Edition 4 — Variant C: The Contrarian Take (Attention Theft Irony)

The people telling you to post 3x a day are part of the machine.

I say that as someone asking you to read this.

Sit in that for a second.

Every "build in public" evangelist. Every "stay visible" guru. Every content strategist (including me, right now, on this platform) is feeding the same system that 2,000 families are currently suing for addiction.

Meta's internal docs showed execs strategizing how to "bring in tweens." Congress hauled them to the stand. The US Surgeon General issued an advisory.

And here I am, writing a post optimized for your engagement.

The irony isn't lost on me. It shouldn't be lost on you either.

I studied dopamine loops for 18 years in fitness. The pump, the progressive overload, the chemical reward for showing up. Those loops are beautiful. They build muscle. They build discipline. The reward is tethered to something real.

Your feed runs the exact same neurochemistry.

Except nothing gets built.

The difference that matters is consent. You chose to read this. You can close this app right now. Your feed at midnight didn't give you that choice. It was engineered by people who studied the same psychological mechanisms I studied, except they pointed them at your limbic system instead of your growth.

I wrote a full edition of Parallax about this. The uncomfortable parts. The parts where I admit I'm in the machine too.

Because the real question isn't how to escape it. It's how to stop letting it run you.

What's one thing you'd do differently if you treated your attention like your most expensive asset?

## Edition 5 — Variant A: The Insight Post (Artemis II — Multi-Class Builds)

NASA sent polymaths to the Moon.

The "niche down" crowd went quiet.

Christina Koch: electrical engineer AND physicist. Contributed to instruments measuring cosmic rays. Also survived Antarctic field stations that would break most people's will to keep being scientists.

Victor Glover: Navy test pilot AND engineer AND combat veteran (24 missions in Iraq) AND flight test engineering masters AND military research. Also the first Black man to live on the ISS long-term.

When asked about making history, he said: "It's not Black history, not women's history. It's human history."

Jeremy Hansen: fighter pilot AND engineer AND Royal Military College. Trained for 15 years without a single mission. Underwater simulations. Geological fieldwork. Russian language. Robotics.

15 years of cross-training before anyone said "go."

That's not a scattered resume. That's a training arc.

If you've played any RPG worth its salt, you know the most dangerous party isn't four warriors. It's the group that covers every skill tree. Tank, healer, mage, rogue.

NASA looked at the most important crewed mission of the 21st century. Equipment failure means death 250,000 miles from the nearest hospital.

They didn't want four people who were each the best at one thing.

They wanted four people who were each really good at several things.

Because when something breaks at lunar distance, you can't call a specialist. You need the person next to you to be a pilot AND an engineer AND a scientist AND someone who can improvise with whatever tools happen to be on the spacecraft.

Range wasn't a nice-to-have. Range was the mission requirement.

I wrote about what this means for the rest of us in the latest Parallax edition. Link in comments.

What skill outside your "main thing" has saved you in a moment that mattered?

**CTA note**: Easy to answer, reveals reader's own multi-disciplinary identity. Comments become a thread of proof that range matters. Every answer reinforces the post's thesis.

## Edition 5 — Variant C: The Contrarian Take (Permission to Stop Shrinking)

"Pick one thing and go all in."

The most expensive advice on the internet.

I've watched people amputate parts of themselves to fit a niche. Kill the side project. Mute the weird interest. Flatten everything into a tagline that fits a LinkedIn headline.

And I did it too. For years.

Too much of a trainer to be taken seriously as a strategist. Too technical for the spiritual people. Too spiritual for the tech people. Too many interests for any single label.

Then NASA put four polymaths on a rocket and pointed them at the Moon.

Not specialists. Polymaths.

Koch didn't pick physics OR engineering. She picked both and went so deep into the intersection that NASA trusted her with a trip around the Moon.

Glover didn't pick flying OR engineering OR combat OR research. He chose all of it. Then became the first Black man to live on the ISS long-term.

Hansen cross-trained across a dozen disciplines for 15 years before getting the call.

The niche-down crowd says: be a level 20 warrior. Max out one skill tree.

NASA says: give us the multi-class builds.

Because at 250,000 miles from Earth, you can't call a specialist. You need the person next to you to be four things at once.

I'm biracial. I have never fit cleanly into one box. Not racially, not professionally, not culturally. I spent most of my life thinking that was the problem.

Watching Victor Glover orbit the Moon and refuse the narrow story, "It's human history," something unlocked that I didn't know was still locked.

If polymaths are trusted to go to the Moon, maybe the rest of us can stop apologizing for refusing to shrink.

Who told you to pick one thing, and what did you give up when you listened?

**CTA note**: Emotionally loaded question that surfaces a specific memory. "Who told you" names an authority figure. "What did you give up" names the cost. Comments will be deeply personal, high-dwell-time stories. This is the post most likely to be shared.
