## The Post

There's a moment, right before you write the line that would actually land, where you feel yourself softening it.

You can feel it physically. The line you were about to write was too sharp, too direct, too close to something that might make a stranger uncomfortable. So you start hedging. The qualifier slips in. The verb gets buried. The specific thing becomes the general thing, the gentle thing, the thing nobody could possibly object to.

You tell yourself you're being thoughtful, and most of the time you're just scared.

I've watched smart people do this for a year now. People with real things to say. Real expertise. Real care for the audience they're trying to reach. They sit down to write and produce something that sounds like every other person in their niche, because every direct line they had got softened before it hit the page.

The story they tell themselves is that they're being ethical. That direct copy is manipulation. That persuasion is gross. That the high road is hedged language and gentle invitations.

That's not the high road. That's the comfortable one.

Empathy is doing what it takes to get someone to the outcome they actually need, with positive intent and love in your heart for the person on the other end. If that intent is real, the same techniques people call manipulation become the most caring thing you can do, because you're willing to risk looking pushy, looking certain, looking like you give a fuck, in service of someone else getting what they came for.

The test is simple. Are you willing to say the thing in the way that gets them to the outcome they need, even if it doesn't make you look good in the process?

If the answer is no, you're not being ethical. You're protecting your image. You're optimizing for how strangers perceive you over whether the one person who needed to hear it actually heard it.

Performative ethics is cowardice with extra steps.

I'm not saying go be a dick. Love has to be in the room. The intent has to be real. But once it's real, you owe the person on the other side the version of the message that reaches them, not the version that keeps you feeling clean.

Most writing that fails fails here. Not at structure. Not at strategy. At the moment the writer chose their own comfort over the reader's outcome and didn't notice they were doing it.

The next time you feel yourself softening the line, ask whose protection you're writing for.

If the answer is yours, rewrite it.
