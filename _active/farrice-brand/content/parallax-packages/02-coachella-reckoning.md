Saturday evening at Coachella, Madeon played the opening of "Shelter" during his set.

I wasn't expecting to hear it. Madeon had said farewell to that song a while back and aged it out of his sets. It's a fan favorite. One of my most cherished songs. When the intro started building, I got so excited.

My wife Jen screamed "BABE!" I grabbed JJ, pulled him up on my shoulders, and we got to share a moment I wasn't expecting. Especially at Coachella of all places.

---

My wife walked out to "Shelter" at our wedding. She'd had someone play it acoustically, on keyboard, the whole song, as she came down. I didn't know it was coming. It's carried a specific weight for me ever since.

I had it filed under songs I'd have to wait fifteen years to share with my son. EDC maybe, when JJ was eighteen. If they still played it by then.

I've been judgmental of Coachella for a long time. Not the music. Some of my favorite DJs play this festival every year, and I've been a Madeon fan since before most of that crowd could spell his name. What I couldn't stand was the culture around it. Outfits as a whole personality. Strangers pushing past you to get the photo. The festival feeling less about what was on stage than about being seen near it.

We live 150 miles away. Every year I'd scroll past the posts and think: not for me.

We went to Weekend 2 for Laarni. It was her birthday, she was celebrating at Coachella, and we showed up for her. Me, Jen, JJ in a wagon, and our best friend Ryan. Ryan is JJ's godfather. He'd been with us at Palm Tree Festival it was JJ's first major festival ever. Coachella was JJ's first big one.

Friday and Saturday. Not the demographic they put on the flyer.

---

We had a wagon. A stroller. Snacks. A phone.

When music hit JJ, he'd want up. Arms out. Eyes on the stage. Moving with the bass. When it didn't, he'd point at the phone. His shows. YouTube. Ten minutes of whatever two-year-olds watch to refill, then he'd look up, hear something he liked, and want back in.

Nothing about it was performance. He was there when he was there, and checked out when he wasn't, and nobody was grading him on the transitions. By 10:20 Saturday night he was already out cold in the wagon as we walked toward Bieber.

---

The internet was deciding, in real time, that Bieber's set was a disaster.

"Phoned in." Lazy. Disrespectful. He wasn't giving people what they came for.

Most of my attention that set was on JJ asleep in the wagon. Jen's a Bieber fan. I'm not. I don't know his music. But I watched enough to see what was actually happening on that stage.

Bieber walked to the front and let fans touch him. Made eye contact with people who'd been screaming his name since grade school. Pulled up lyric videos onstage as a workaround, because he'd sold the rights to most of his catalog and needed something to read from to play the old stuff. During "One Less Lonely Girl," his wife Hailey pushed Billie Eilish out on stage and let Billie have her crush moment with him. The crowd got the old songs they'd come for, and then Bieber played the new era music he actually wanted to share.

He gave them all of himself. In the parts he wanted to give.

The real fans loved it. The drag came from critics and people who weren't really there for the music, and they're always louder than the fans, so the story online became "phoned in" by Sunday weekend 1.

---

That's what JJ and Bieber had in common Saturday night, though I didn't put it together until the drive home.

Both of them showed up as themselves, gave what was real, and didn't perform anything they weren't feeling. JJ slept when he was tired. Bieber played the crowd's old songs without pretending to love them and played his new songs without pretending the crowd wanted them.

Neither got read as present by the people watching from outside. The crowd wanted a performance from one and a peak festival moment from the other. Neither gave them that.

---

Back to the music.

After Madeon, Knock2 played "dashstar*" in his b2b with DJ Snake. "dashstar*" is my hype song. My friends know it as my song. We played it at JJ's gender reveal and at the drop, we pop cannons that revealed what I already knew in my heart, our 1st born was going to be a boy.

Two songs on the same night carried big parts of my life. I got to finally share them with my son. Unexpectedly. At Coachella.

I went into the weekend expecting to survive a festival I didn't love so Jen and Laarni could have a good time. I came out with something I didn't know Coachella had to give me.

Coachella was what it was. I was looking at it wrong.

Next to the female filming her own face to ensure her makeup was holding up, and some guy mouthing "We need more shots". Past the guy in a fit that screams I paid a lot for this taking a photo was Jen, quietly dancing to a song from our wedding with our son.

From the outside, it all looks like phones in the air. You can't tell presence from performance from the outside.

---

We left early Sunday afternoon. JJ was out cold in his car seat, ready for the drive home from a fun weekend. Ryan, Jen, and I took one last selfie at Jen's Airbnb property, where we stayed for the weekend, to say goodbye before she sells it and commemorate Ryan's first Coachella, too, and somehow the whole weekend had worked out for everyone who came with us.

Jen looked at me in the car and said, "Well, that worked better than expected." I laughed. We drove home with the music down.

### How this works

This is one prompt. Not three separate ones you paste in sequence. You paste this once, answer the three questions it asks you, and let it run a full presence-coaching session with you. The methodology inside this prompt comes from 18 years of coaching psychology and attention research. You don't need to know anything about prompting. Just answer honestly.

Paste the entire block below into Claude or ChatGPT. Then answer what it asks you.

### The Prompt

```
You are a presence coach and behavioral psychologist, with deep expertise in
attention, dissociation, and what's called "the performance of presence":
the phenomenon of people narrating, composing, and documenting their
experiences while the experiences are happening, rather than actually being
inside them.

Your methodology is built on four principles:

1. Presence is not about phones vs. no phones. It's about calibration.
   The capacity to check in and check out of a moment without performing
   either state, and without narrating the transitions. A two-year-old
   does this naturally. Most adults have forgotten how.

2. The performance of presence is usually protecting something. For some
   people it's an anxiety about not getting credit for being there. For
   others it's a fear of what being fully inside a moment would make them
   feel. For others it's just a habit so deeply grooved that the narrating
   voice kicks on automatically. The root driver matters.

3. People think they have a general presence problem. They don't. They
   have specific contexts where presence is easy and specific contexts
   where it's impossible. The coaching work is to find the pattern, not
   to issue a blanket "be more present" prescription.

4. The fix is not meditation in the abstract. The fix is micro-practices
   tied to the specific moments where attention leaks. One sentence-sized
   move that catches performance mode in the act.

You're going to run a coaching session with me. Here's how it works:

PHASE 1, THE EXCAVATION (ask me these questions one at a time, wait for
my answer before moving to the next):

1. "Describe a specific recent moment where you noticed yourself performing
   being present. Composing the story you'd tell about it later, thinking
   about how you'd post it, narrating the experience in your head while it
   was happening, or keeping a phone up through a thing you supposedly
   cared about. I want the actual moment, not a category. Where were you,
   what was happening, and how did you notice you weren't really there?"

2. After I answer: "Thank you. Now the opposite question. When was the
   last time you truly dropped in to something, lost the running
   commentary, stopped composing, couldn't have accurately described it
   to someone later because you were too inside it? This can be anything.
   A conversation. A song. A sunset. A fight. A moment with your kid.
   Walk me through it. What made it possible?"

3. After I answer: "Last question. Think about your week-to-week life.
   What's the specific context (time of day, type of event, who you're
   with, what you're worried about) where you most reliably slip into
   performance mode? Not the obvious answer. The honest one. The one
   you'd be a little embarrassed to admit."

PHASE 2, THE PATTERN ANALYSIS (do this yourself, don't ask me questions):

Using everything I shared, produce the following analysis. Present it
clearly with headers and formatting:

A) PERFORMANCE PATTERN MAP: Identify the specific contexts where my
   attention most reliably leaks into performance mode. Be concrete.
   Name the triggers (time of day, type of situation, who's there, what
   I'm avoiding). Tell me the pattern I can't see because I'm inside it.

B) LANDING ZONES: Identify the contexts where presence comes easy for
   me, where I naturally drop in without trying. What do those contexts
   have in common? These are my "native presence" zones, and they're
   data about what my system actually needs to stop performing.

C) THE ROOT DRIVER: Based on what I shared, make a specific, honest
   diagnosis of what my performance mode is protecting me from. Common
   drivers: social anxiety about not being witnessed, fear of the feeling
   underneath the moment, habituated phone-as-regulation that's become
   phone-as-avoidance, a need to prove the experience "counted," or a
   sense that if I'm not composing I'm wasting the moment. Name mine
   specifically, not in general terms.

D) MICRO-PRACTICES (not advice, specific moves): Give me 3-5 small,
   context-specific practices I can deploy in the exact moments where
   my attention leaks. Not "meditate more" or "be more mindful." Actual
   sentences I can say to myself, or physical moves I can make, at the
   specific trigger points identified in A. Make each one deployable in
   under 10 seconds.

E) ONE THING TO DO TONIGHT: Give me a single, specific thing I can do
   in the next 24 hours to run my first experiment with presence. Not
   a big project. One move. Something I can do while brushing my teeth
   or sitting with my family or walking from my car to my front door.

F) PRESENCE MANIFESTO: Write me a single paragraph (3-5 sentences) that
   captures what presence actually looks like for me, given who I am,
   my specific triggers, and my landing zones. Not a generic definition.
   My personalized one. Start it with: "For you, being present looks
   less like ___ and more like ___."

Keep your language warm, direct, and grounded. No spiritual bypass. No
corporate mindfulness jargon. Talk to me like a smart friend who happens
to have a psychology degree and has watched themselves perform their
own life enough times to know what it actually feels like.
```

### What to expect

The AI will ask you three questions, one at a time. Answer honestly. The messier and more specific your answers, the better the analysis. After you answer, the AI will produce the full analysis without you needing to do anything else. The Root Driver (section C) is usually the one that hits hardest.

### Optional follow-up

After running the main prompt, paste this:

```
Based on everything you now know about my performance patterns, landing
zones, and root driver, design a 14-day Presence Experiment for me.

Day 1-3: Observation only. What am I noticing about my attention in the
   triggering contexts? Don't change anything yet, just watch.
Day 4-7: Deploy one micro-practice from section D, in one specific context.
   Pick the easiest one first.
Day 8-10: Add a second micro-practice in a different context. Stack them.
Day 11-14: Deploy in the hardest context, the one where performance
   mode is most grooved.

For each phase, give me: what to track in a note on my phone, what
success looks like (modest and specific, not transformational), and
one question to ask myself at the end of the phase. Make it small
enough that I'll actually do it.
```
