## Essay

JJ is sitting on the edge of a bed that's been his for nine days.

It's 1:14 in the afternoon. His eyes are doing the glaze that means we're already past the window. Crib is gone. The bed is full of the wrong associations now, books, two stuffed dogs, a Hot Wheels track that he and I built last weekend on the floor and somehow ended up on the duvet. He knows he's tired. He also knows that if he closes his eyes, the room stops being the room he was just playing in.

A lot of parents at this point say "time for nap." It rarely works on a kid this tired in a room full of toys.

I've been watching him for eight months. The thing that gets JJ to a nap is not authority and not negotiation. It's a specific sequence. I sit down on the floor next to the bed, not on the bed. I don't make eye contact for the first thirty seconds. I tell him, quietly, that the dogs are tired. I ask which one is the most tired. He picks the brown one. I tell him the brown one needs his help to fall asleep. He looks at the brown one. He looks at me. He lies down with the brown one, on his side, the way he sleeps. I put my hand on his back. He's out in four minutes.

If a colleague described that sequence to me about a client instead of a toddler, I'd recognize it as a small, intentional campaign.

I read him. I knew which lever moved him today. I built the smallest possible bridge between what he wanted (control, agency, not being told) and what we both needed (him asleep before he tipped into the meltdown that wrecks the rest of the day). I raised the stakes by giving the dog a problem only he could solve. I said exactly what he needed to hear in exactly the order he could hear it.

And as I walked out of his room, the line landed in my head: *I just manipulated my two-year-old son, and I don't know how to feel about it.*

I sat with that for the rest of the afternoon.

The skill I just used on my two-year-old is the exact skill most marketers refuse to use on the audience they claim to serve. They refuse it for reasons that sound like ethics but function like cowardice. They want to write the version that flatters them. They want to sound smart, sound generous, sound like they care, while saying things in a way that makes the listener nod politely and do nothing.

What I did with JJ wasn't pretty copy. The whole sequence was specific to him, met him in the room he was actually standing in, and never once tried to make me sound like a thoughtful father.

The meltdown the day before was the reason I noticed any of this.

We've been letting JJ have screens. Not a lot. Just enough to keep him occupied while one of us cooks dinner or takes a call or finishes a deck. Enough that his nervous system started shaping itself around it. On Sunday he had the biggest outburst he's ever had. Forty minutes of a kind of crying I had not heard from him before, the kind that comes from a small body that learned to expect a hit of cheap dopamine on a schedule and didn't get it. Withdrawal from an iPad, at two years old.

I stood in the hallway outside his room and watched my excuse die in real time.

The excuse was: I'm grinding for our family. I'm putting food on the table. I have to take this call. He'll be fine on the iPad for twenty minutes. The excuse sounded like sacrifice. It was actually outsourcing. I was outsourcing my son's nervous system to a screen so I could chase a client, and the bill came due in his bedroom on a Sunday afternoon.

The new rule is simple and it has cost me already. Work happens when he sleeps. Presence happens when he's awake. If I'm using a screen at all, I'd better be doing the work I told myself I was sacrificing for, not scrolling, not half-watching a podcast while answering Slack. The screen is allowed only if my reason for it is real.

Which brings me back to the bedtime move.

What people call manipulation, when they want to feel clean, is just the willingness to actually study the person on the other end of what you're saying. To raise the stakes high enough that your words have to land. To say the thing in a shape they can take in, even if the shape is uncomfortable for you to write. Empathy and manipulation use the identical technique. What separates them is whether you'd run that exact move on someone you actually love.

I would never run the stuffed-dog move on JJ to trick him out of a candy bar he was excited about. I'd run it every single time to get him asleep before he tipped into the meltdown that would cost him his afternoon. The vocabulary is identical in both scenarios; what changes is why I'm bothering to open my mouth.

When a copywriter softens a hard message and calls it kindness, what's usually happening is that they're choosing their own comfort over the reader's outcome and asking the reader to pay them for the privilege of being protected from the truth they came for.

I've done both versions. The polite one lands softly, converts nothing, and lets the audience nod and stay exactly where they were when they came to me to get unstuck.

If I'm not willing to study you, raise the stakes, and say the thing in the shape you can hear it, what I'm actually doing is performing helpfulness while protecting the way I look in your eyes. That's the version of marketing I'm walking away from.

The brown stuffed dog is on the duvet. JJ's hand is on it. His breath has gone slow. I'm sitting on the floor next to the bed because the floor was the move today and tomorrow it might be the chair. I'll watch him again tomorrow and find out.

## Paired Prompt Pack — The Honest-Read Audit

**Use this:** Paste this into Claude or ChatGPT with a piece of your own copy, content, or positioning attached. The prompt will run a four-pass audit on whether you're saying the thing in the shape your reader can take in, or whether you've quietly chosen your own comfort over their outcome. About twenty minutes if you do it honestly.

### Prompt 1 — The Reader You're Actually Writing To

```
You are a senior conversion copy editor with twenty years of experience reading copy for what it actually does, not what it claims to do.

I'm going to paste a piece of my own copy below this prompt. Before you analyze it, I need you to do something specific.

Read the copy and reverse-engineer the audience I appear to be writing to. Not the audience I would say I'm writing to if you asked me. The audience my actual word choices, sentence shapes, level of specificity, and assumed objections suggest I'm imagining as I write.

Tell me:
1. The age, profession, and life stage of the person my copy is performing for.
2. The level of expertise I'm assuming they have on this topic.
3. The objections I am anticipating and pre-handling, and the objections I am avoiding.
4. The version of myself I'm trying to look like in their eyes.

Be specific. Use evidence from the copy itself. Quote sentences back to me as proof.

[Paste your copy below this line]
```

### Prompt 2 — The Reader You Actually Have

```
Now I'm going to describe my actual audience, the real one, in as much specific detail as I can. Read it carefully.

[Paste a 200-400 word description of your real ICP. Be specific. Their job, their day, what they searched to find you, what they tried before you, what they're worried about right now, what they would say at a dinner party about their problem if they were two glasses of wine in.]

Compare the audience my copy is performing for (from prompt 1) to the audience I actually have (above).

Tell me:
1. Where the gap is widest. Be specific. Which sentences in my copy are written for the imagined audience instead of the real one.
2. What my real audience needed to hear in that exact moment that my copy did not give them.
3. The single line in my copy that is most clearly written to make me look smart rather than to move them.
```

### Prompt 3 — The Stakes Test

```
Look at my copy again, line by line. For each section, ask the following:

If my real reader read this exact line and felt nothing, what would they do next?

For each line that produces "scroll past," "nod and move on," or "save it for later and never come back," flag it.

For each flagged line, give me a rewrite that does one of three things:
A) Raises the stake by naming a specific cost of inaction this reader actually feels.
B) Says the harder version of the same idea, the one I softened to protect myself.
C) Replaces a generality with a specific image, scene, or number from this reader's actual life.

Show me the original line, the rewrite, and one sentence on what changed. Do not produce a generic before/after. Do the work specifically on my copy.
```

### Prompt 4 — The Empathy-vs-Comfort Diagnostic

```
Read my full copy one more time. For each soft, hedged, or polite line you find, I want you to tell me whether it is in the reader's interest or in mine.

The test:
- If the line is soft because the reader is not yet ready for the hard version and softening it earns me the right to deliver the hard version later, that is empathy. Mark it KEEP.
- If the line is soft because the hard version would make me sound aggressive, presumptuous, or salesy, and softening it protected my self-image at the cost of the reader's outcome, that is comfort. Mark it CUT.

Show me every line marked CUT, and rewrite each one in the version I should have written if I cared more about the reader's outcome than my own image.

Then write me one sentence I should tape to the wall above my desk, in my own voice, that I will violate the next time I sit down to write copy. That sentence is the rule I keep breaking.
```

### Prompt 5 — The Bedtime Move

```
Now zoom out. Based on everything you've seen in my copy and my description of my real audience:

If my reader were a tired two-year-old who knew they needed help but was resisting it, what would the bedtime move be? Meaning, what is the smallest, most specific bridge between what they want (autonomy, dignity, control) and what they actually need (the outcome only I can deliver)?

Give me:
1. The exact opening line of copy that would meet them where they are without flattering them.
2. The "stuffed dog" — the small, concrete, vivid story or image that gives them an indirect way to step toward the outcome without feeling pushed.
3. The single sentence I should land with that does not summarize what I just said but instead leaves them looking at their own situation differently than they were three minutes ago.

Write all three in my voice, using my actual copy as the voice reference, not in generic copywriter prose.
```
