> Publish-ready. Five LinkedIn posts + one Parallax (Substack) edition opener, engineered for eyeballs and attraction on the big-domino belief, in Farrice's voice. Mode B: confrontation kept, profanity dropped. Confrontation is aimed at the reader's self-deception and the people profiting from it, never at the reader as a person.

## The one belief every piece installs (big domino)

You do not have a knowledge problem. You have a finishing problem. And more knowledge does not fix a finishing problem, it feeds it. The stall lives in the gap between knowing and doing, not in a shortage of information. Move that belief and every objection ("I just need one more course / I should figure this out myself / I need to try harder") loses its footing.

## What this is (and isn't)

Built on the reshaped offer per the red-team verdict, not the original. The public promise is the ship/finish outcome (the system stuck at 90% goes live and runs in your margins). The custom AI build is the vehicle; the inner-game is the differentiated how. "Systems AND soul" is a private design intent, never a public label here. First-attention content self-selects; it does not pitch. Closes are declarations, never cheap question sign-offs.

## Ethics Gate (passed)

Second-person details ("a course you finished 18% of") are rhetorical mirrors drawn from the ICP research, not testimonials presented as real clients. No fabricated screenshots, no fake scarcity, no numbers Farrice cannot defend.

## POST 1 — The course graveyard (big-domino flagship)

*Targets belief B1 (one more tool/course) + B2 (willpower). Self-selection: repels people still shopping for the missing piece, magnetizes operators who already own everything and still haven't shipped.*

Somewhere on your hard drive is a course you paid two grand for and finished eighteen percent of.

You know the one. You can still quote the sales page from memory. You bookmarked module three for "when things calm down."

Things did not calm down. You bought another course.

I'll say the thing the person selling you course number nine never will: you don't have a knowledge problem. You have a finishing problem. And more knowledge doesn't fix a finishing problem. It feeds it.

Notice who actually gets stuck here. It isn't beginners. Beginners finish things, because they're too new to know how much they don't know, so they just go. The graveyard of half-done programs belongs to the smart ones. The veterans. The people who could teach the course they abandoned.

That's the tell. You're not behind because you don't know enough. You're behind because, somewhere along the way, knowing quietly became the thing you do instead of doing.

The purchase is the reward. The dopamine fires at checkout, not at completion. You've trained yourself, at considerable expense, to feel productive while standing perfectly still. (That isn't learning. It's the most expensive kind of standing still: procrastination with a receipt and a certificate you never earned.)

And the next one will feel like progress too. It'll feel like this is finally the one. It won't be. Not because it's a bad course, but because the problem was never a shortage of information.

So no. I'm not going to tell you what to study next.

If you're reading this thinking "fair, but I really do need to understand one more thing first," then go buy it. Sincerely. I'll be here when it's parked at 18% beside the other twelve.

And if you're reading this a little irritated, because you already own everything you need and you still haven't shipped the thing that matters, good. That irritation isn't shame. It's the first honest reading you've taken off your own dashboard in a while.

The graveyard was never proof you're lazy. It's proof you've been solving the wrong problem with a credit card.

## POST 2 — The curse of expertise

*Targets belief B3 (a serious expert should figure it out alone, especially with AI). Self-selection: the flinch at "ask for outside eyes" names the fit; the secure operator leans in.*

You can look at a stranger's business for twenty minutes and tell them exactly what's wrong with it.

Your own? You've been circling the same problem for a year. Maybe longer.

It's a special kind of crazy-making. "I give better advice in a DM than I take myself. What is actually wrong with me?"

Nothing's wrong with you. You're standing too close to the thing you built to see its shape.

There's a name for it: the curse of expertise. The deeper you go into a craft, the more your own blind spots hide from you, because the knowledge that makes you good also makes you assume everyone (you included) can already see what you see. You end up unable to find the door you're standing directly in front of.

This is why "I should be able to figure this out myself" is such a beautiful trap. Especially now, with AI on your desk. The story only gets louder: I'm smart, I'm technical, I can prompt my way to anything. And you can. You can build ninety percent of it yourself. You already have. It's sitting at ninety percent right now.

The ten percent you can't reach has nothing to do with skill. It's a vantage-point problem. You cannot stand inside the frame and see the frame at the same time. More intelligence doesn't solve that. Past a point, intelligence is the thing making it worse.

The least flattering sentence I'll write this week: the most capable person in the room is often the most stuck one, for the exact same reason they're capable.

The people who break out of it didn't finally get smart enough. They stopped treating "I need an outside eye" as a confession of weakness and started treating it as what it is: a move only available to people secure enough to make it.

If asking for a set of outside eyes feels like losing, notice that flinch. That flinch is the thing that's kept you circling your own block for a year.

You didn't get this good by being fragile. Don't get stuck now by pretending you have to see around your own corner.

## POST 3 — Willpower was never the fix

*Targets belief B2 (discipline/willpower problem) + B5 (buying help = admitting failure). Self-selection: recognition in the chest is the qualifier; the person who genuinely just needs a better planner exits.*

You keep waiting to feel disciplined enough to finish.

Save yourself the wait. That feeling isn't coming. You've been waiting three years, and the offer, the program, the thing that would change everything is still parked at ninety percent, one push from done, exactly where it's sat the whole time.

Everyone treats the 90% stall as a willpower problem. Try harder. Wake up earlier. Get an accountability partner. White-knuckle it across the line.

Then look at your own evidence. You built a career that took years of showing up on days you didn't feel like it. You have world-class discipline. It's one of the most reliable things about you. So explain this: why does that iron discipline evaporate at ninety percent, every time, on the projects that matter most?

It isn't running out of willpower. Stopping at ninety percent is doing a job for you.

As long as the work is unfinished, it can't be judged. It can't flop. It can't turn out that your best swing wasn't enough. It stays a beautiful maybe. Ship it and it becomes something other people get to grade, and some quiet, load-bearing part of you would genuinely rather keep the maybe than risk the verdict. So it taps the brake, right at the edge, and hands you a very reasonable excuse for why now isn't the time.

That part isn't lazy. It's protective. It's running a survival program written a long time ago, doing precisely what it was built to do. (Where it got written is a longer story than this post.)

Which is why every productivity fix has bounced off. A protection pattern doesn't care about your new planner. Better mornings don't disarm it. Pushing harder just teaches it to push back.

You don't out-discipline this thing. You out-design it. The last ten percent has to be built so that finishing doesn't hinge on you being in the right headspace on the day it's due, because your headspace on a given day is the least reliable thing you own, and you know it.

If you read that and felt something tighten in your chest, sit with it for a second. That feeling is recognition. And recognition is the only place this ever actually starts.

## POST 4 — The best-kept secret

*Targets the visibility / referral-ceiling bottleneck (the deep-researched Invisible Expert). Leads the first cohort. Self-selection: the private anger at watching someone lesser get chosen names the fit; the happily-referred expert is told, sincerely, to keep going.*

There's a sentence that lands like a slap, and if you've been genuinely good at your work for a while, some version of it has probably reached you: "I almost didn't find you. You don't really come up when I search."

Fifteen years of depth. Results that would make a stranger's jaw drop. And you nearly lost the client to someone with a nicer website and a tenth of the substance.

That's the quiet tax on being excellent at something: nobody can tell from the outside.

You've felt the version of this. You watch someone with a fraction of your experience land the speaking slot, the podcast, the client you'd have served ten times better. And the story writes itself. They're louder. They're shameless. Good for them.

Maybe. Or maybe they just showed up somewhere you refused to.

The real trap is quieter. Referrals built your business, and referrals are wonderful, right up until the day your best referrer changes jobs and the pipeline goes quiet for a quarter and your stomach drops. You built something that only works when other people remember to talk about you. That's not a moat. It's a hope you've dressed up as a strategy.

And you already know the fix. That's the maddening part. You've known for two years. "I should put my thinking out there." You've even started. There's a drafts folder to prove it. Seven posts written, zero posted, because in the second before you hit publish a voice says who are you to say this, and this feels like bragging, and my real work should speak for itself.

Your work is excellent. Excellent work in a locked room helps no one. The person who needs you is Googling their problem tonight, and someone with half your ability is answering them, badly, because they were willing to be findable and you weren't.

There's nothing braggy about being findable. It's the whole difference between the people who need you finding you, or finding the loud guy instead.

This one isn't for everybody. If you're happy on referrals and you sleep fine when the pipeline dips, genuinely, keep going. Nothing here to fix.

But if you've ever watched someone half as qualified and twice as loud get chosen, and felt that specific private anger you don't say out loud, then you already know the room you're brilliant in has walls. The door's been unlocked the whole time. You just keep almost opening it.

## POST 5 — The map I drew on the way up

*Targets belief B6 (AI = soulless/generic) + the proof problem. Pre-revenue, Ethics-Gate-clean: self-as-first-case-study, honest about no client results yet. Self-selection: repels people shopping for a finished guru, magnetizes people who trust a builder who needed the thing first.*

I help people finish the thing they keep not finishing. I have also restarted this post more times than I want to admit.

Not a fun way to introduce myself. But if I hid it, I'd be one more guy selling a summit he's never actually climbed. I'm the other kind. Still climbing, and I happened to sketch a usable map on the way up.

The pattern I hid for years went like this. I'm exceptional at getting things to ninety percent. I can architect a system, a business, a plan, faster and cleaner than almost anyone I know. And then, right at the edge of done, I'd stall. Reliably. On the things that mattered most.

For a long time I told myself the obvious story about that. I get ninety percent of the way and then I sabotage it. Something in me is broken. So I white-knuckled it. Read the books. Did the inner work, plenty of it, some real, most of it floating free of anything I was actually building.

What finally moved wasn't more willpower. It was one reframe, and it took me an embarrassing number of years to reach it: I don't have to become someone who muscles through the last ten percent. I can design a system that carries it for me. The genius was never in forcing the finish through sheer character. It's in the architecture — building the thing so shipping doesn't depend on whatever mood I happen to wake up in.

That reframe traces back a lot further than business. To a childhood, a specific silence, a lesson about finishing and whose word is allowed to hold. Longer story than a post can carry, and I'll tell it properly somewhere with more room. The professional version is simple: "just be more disciplined" is advice from people who've never met the thing that's actually stalling you. I lived on the far side of that advice for a decade. It doesn't reach.

These days I build these systems with AI, and I want to be exact about what that means, because "AI-powered business" usually conjures getting flattened into the gray sludge already filling everyone's feed. That's the opposite of the aim. Built well, the machine runs on your actual thinking, in your voice, shaped around the specific way your mind works. It comes out sounding more like you, not less. An amplifier, never a blender.

I don't have a wall of client results for this offer yet. It's early, and I won't manufacture them. You will never get a staged screenshot from me. What I do have is eighteen years of walking real people through real change, and my own long climb out of the exact pattern I just described to you. That turns out to be the only honest ground worth building on.

If you want a finished guru who has it all handled, there are ten thousand of them and their DMs are wide open. If you'd rather build with someone who drew the map because he needed it first, and who'll hand you the unflattering truth before you go digging for it, then you and I are going to get along fine.

## PARALLAX — Edition 1 opener

*Substack. Memoir-grade interiority. Zeitgeist-first (the forever-learning economy). Owns the father wound and the "brave Tuesday" line. Launches "The Final 10%" as the content franchise.*

### The Final 10%

*The most capable people I know are the most stuck. It has almost nothing to do with what they know.*

There's a specific person the entire internet has quietly agreed to lie to.

They're not beginners. They're not lazy. By any honest measure they're excellent, a decade or two into a craft, the one other people call when the stakes are real. They've forgotten more than most of their competitors will ever learn.

And they are stuck. Not stuck like they don't know what to do. Stuck like they know exactly what to do and cannot, for reasons that would embarrass them to say out loud, get themselves to do it.

The internet sells this person the same thing every time. Another course. Another framework. Another certification, another tool, another guru at a whiteboard swearing that this is the missing piece. And they buy it, because buying feels like motion, and they finish a fifth of it, and file it beside the last eleven, and lie awake wondering what exactly is wrong with them.

I can tell you what's wrong with them, because I am one of them, and I spent years being lied to by people I paid.

Nothing is wrong with them. They were sold more input for a problem that was never about input.

Call it the 90% pattern. You build something almost all the way to finished, a business, an offer, a body of work, a version of your own life, and then, right at the edge, just before it turns real and public and out of your hands, you stop. And you make the stopping respectable. You call it research. You tell yourself it isn't the right season. It's the most sophisticated procrastination on earth, because from the outside, and from the inside, it looks exactly like diligence.

The engine underneath it works like this. At ninety percent, the thing is still safe. Still potential. The market hasn't voted, nobody's rejected it, and you get to keep the beautiful private story that it would have worked. Finishing puts that story at risk. So some old, load-bearing part of you, installed long before you ever ran a business, pulls the brake at the exact moment the work stops being a dream and starts being a verdict.

I know the shape of that part intimately. Mine traces back to a house where my father was present and absent at the same time, and to a lesson I absorbed without anyone ever saying it aloud: that a man's word doesn't have to hold. Finishing is your word holding. And you cannot break a promise you never technically completed. So I got very, very good at ninety percent.

For years I wore that as my defining flaw. I white-knuckled it. Read the whole shelf. Did the inner work, some of it real, most of it drifting free of anything I was actually trying to build.

What finally moved wasn't more willpower. It was a single reframe, and it's the reason this letter exists:

I don't have to become the kind of person who muscles through the last 10%. I can design a system that carries it.

That's the whole thesis. The genius was never in forcing the finish through raw character. It's in the architecture, building the thing so that shipping doesn't hang on whether you feel brave on some ordinary Tuesday. Because staking your life's work on feeling brave on a Tuesday is precisely what has failed you, and me, every single time.

So that's what these letters are about. The psychology of why capable people freeze at the edge of done. The systems, more and more of them built with AI, always built around the specific way your particular mind works, that carry you across that edge. And the quiet, inherited patterns that bolted the brake on in the first place.

One honest warning before you subscribe. If you came for another framework to collect, you're going to hate it here. I'm not handing you a thirteenth thing to half-finish. I'd sooner give you back the eleven already sitting on your drive.

But if you're the capable, stuck, privately furious person I described up top, the one who knows more than anyone in your field and keeps getting passed over by people with a fraction of your depth, then pull up a chair. You're not broken. You've just been believing the people who profit when you never finish.

Let's go find the last 10%.
