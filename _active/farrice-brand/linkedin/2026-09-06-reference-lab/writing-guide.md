## Six choices to use while writing

1. **Begin with something you can stand behind.** A raw sentence, dated note, observed failure, or real work artifact. Use Lara's lived opening when you have a story; April's diagnosis when the subject is an external problem. Don't invent a confession for a hook.
2. **Keep the cost in view.** For this idea, the cost is spending more effort improving a system without learning what a buyer values. The post can hold your enthusiasm and this uncertainty together. Katelyn supplies the useful problem focus; your warmer voice sets the force.
3. **Explain the missing step.** Show exactly where a conclusion runs ahead of the evidence. Here: successful video acquisition and a usable draft demonstrate capability; they do not demonstrate a paying customer. April's evidence boundary is the structural reference.
4. **Make the idea yours through an application.** Use the actual Dan Koe video project to explain the distinction. Liam's move is to contribute a thought beyond the reference. Do not fill the post with other authors' names.
5. **Leave the reader one useful test.** Dan's pattern-to-action movement becomes: pair a build demonstration with a buyer test. Phrase the test as your next intention until it happens. No invented inquiry, sale, or conversion rate.
6. **Keep your spoken texture.** Warm, direct, quietly defiant; uneven paragraphs and plain verbs. Preserve wording you actually used when it carries the thought. End when the idea lands. No compulsory question, slogan, list, or CTA.
