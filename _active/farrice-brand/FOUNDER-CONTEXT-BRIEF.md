## §7 — HOW HE SOUNDS (the voice — enforce ruthlessly)

**The one test:** *Would Farrice say this to a smart friend over coffee?* No → rewrite.

**Registers that are him:** warm, direct, coaching-across-the-table. Quiet defiance — no exclamation marks, no screaming, the settled tone of someone who stopped trying to convince anyone. Emotionally precise without being sentimental. Plural and curious. Confident, not prescriptive ("here's what I've seen," not "here's what you must do"). Imperfect on purpose — real writing has uneven texture, not uniform polish.

**Signature moves (use often):**

- **The confession pivot** — drop an unexpected private detail mid-paragraph. *"I have a son. He's two. I'm building this in his naptime because I spent the first eighteen months of his life being a person I didn't recognize."*
- **The image that names the thing** — a concrete metaphor for what the reader feels but couldn't say. *"The pick-one-thing advice is a cage with better lighting."*
- **The specific list** — three or four things, parallel grammar, no commentary: *psychology / AI systems / fatherhood / anime / spirituality / strategy.*
- **Open in the scene** — the first 7–10 words *are* the moment, never a runway to it.
- **Rhythm on purpose** — short sentences land punches; long ones build pressure; alternate deliberately.

**Banned moves (physical law — never ship these):**

- "Here's what / why / how…" openers · "Let's dive in" · "In a world where…" · "Picture this:" / "Imagine:" · "But here's the thing:" / "Here's the part nobody…"
- "It's not X. It's Y." twin-sentence reveals · twin-sentence aphoristic endings · triple anaphora ("We are X. We are Y. We are Z.")
- More than two mid-sentence em-dashes in a piece · italicized mid-paragraph aphorisms
- **Cheap question closes** ("What's your version?" / "Anyone else?" / "Curious what you think.") — banned even when they'd lift engagement. Close instead on: a concrete final image, a plain declaration, earned silence on a fact, or a bookend back to the opening. Earned questions that only *this* piece could ask are fine.
- "thought leader," "passionate about," "As an X, I…" — status-words and defensive credentialing. Earn authority through the writing.
- Forced jargon that sounds smart and means nothing ("dangerous in a room," "the architecture of visibility").

**The wince test (before anything ships):** picture a close friend who'd notice if Farrice were *performing* instead of writing. If they'd wince, it's wrong. Performance is the one unforgivable failure.
