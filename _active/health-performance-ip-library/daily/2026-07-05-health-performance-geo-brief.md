## 1. The Pick

Two FTC cases closed four months apart this year on kids' supplements, and together they prove something more useful than either one alone: brands are faking proof from both directions at once, the study *and* the crowd, and most health brands have never checked which side of that line their own claims sit on.

Farrice could say this out loud: "If the person who invented the product is also the guy who wrote the study proving it works, that's marketing wearing a lab coat, not proof."

The founder or buyer who cares today: any founder of a kids', family, or MLM-adjacent wellness brand who has ever pointed to "our own research" or "our five-star reviews" as evidence, and any parent-facing brand watching the FTC's health-claim enforcement get louder in 2026.

This beats the other researched signals (a broader FDA warning-letter roundup, a generic DTC-brand trend scan, and a pure AI-hallucination stat) because it hands Farrice a two-sided teardown with public court documents behind it, not a trend he has to argue is real.

## 2. Why It Has Juice

**Visual scene**: a parent scrolling Instagram at 9 PM, landing on a "Kids Happy Juice" post with 400 heart-reacts and a caption that says "clinically shown to support mood and focus," never knowing that the study behind that line tested ten children, had no placebo group, and was co-authored by the man who invented the product.

**Buyer tension**: parents check exactly two things before buying a kids' wellness product, is there a study, and do other parents like it, and case by case, regulators just showed both checks can be manufactured by the seller.

**Belief shift**: from "I checked, there's a study and good reviews" to "I checked who wrote the study and who wrote the reviews."

**The thing Farrice can say that the category usually won't**: most brands treat "we have a study" and "we have reviews" as finished proof instead of the start of a question. The FTC just ran that question for two companies and did not like the answer either time.

**Plain-English translation**: "source hierarchy" here just means: who paid for the study, and who benefits if the answer is yes. "Proof surface" just means the study, the reviews, and the founder's credential, the three things a buyer actually looks at before trusting a health claim.

**Five raw takes Farrice could riff on without more research**:

1. A ten-person study with no placebo group, run by the guy who invented the product, reads like a clinical claim but functions like an opinion with a citation stapled to it.
2. Five-star reviews used to be the fast trust check. After TruHeight, they're the first thing worth doubting.
3. Kids' health products get the least scrutiny from parents and the most scrutiny from the FTC, and that gap is where the risk lives.
4. Every regulated industry has a version of "we graded our own homework." Watching supplements do it in public is a free lesson for any founder making a claim about anything.
5. The next wave of trust in wellness will belong to brands willing to show where their proof came from and who benefited from it, not to brands that just get louder.

A supplement company ran its own clinical trial.

The brands that win the next few years of buyer trust, and AI search, will be the ones whose proof survives that question, not the ones with the loudest proof.
