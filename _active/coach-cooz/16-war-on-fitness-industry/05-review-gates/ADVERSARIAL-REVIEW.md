# Adversarial Review — "War on Fitness Industry" Funnel

## Executive Summary

The funnel is directionally strong and structurally clean, but it has two load-bearing weaknesses that will surface within the first 200 flyers deployed. First, the unbranded profanity card asks LA business owners to become anonymous distributors of a card their customers may find offensive — the ask-script assumes a peer trust Cooz hasn't earned from every venue, and refusal rate will be high in salons/juice bars (the exact "captive audience" targets). Second, the squeeze page delays the brand reveal until Section 6 (offer), which means a suspicious first-time scanner spends 700+ words reading anti-industry breakdowns from someone whose credentials arrive only when the price does — this is a "who is this guy" trust gap that will suppress conversion below the 0.6-1% viability threshold in STRATEGY-SPINE.md. Neither is fatal; both are fixable pre-launch. The bigger post-launch risk is the call-out vulnerability: the funnel makes the fitness industry the villain for using "photos as proof," then relies on Cooz's own inverted photo as proof three sections later. A competitor can and will screenshot that contradiction. SHIP WITH FIXES.

## Key Findings

### MUST-FIX (blocks deployment)

1. **The Cooz photo contradicts the villain frame.** Section 2 says transformation photos are fabricated theater. Section 3 shows a two-photo transformation of Cooz. The defense — "mine is inverted, showing lean=miserable, heavy=happy" — is subtle. A hostile screenshotter will crop Section 2's attack next to Section 3's photo and post "he does exactly what he says the industry does." Fix: reframe Section 3 as one photo plus a paragraph, OR add an explicit inoculation line ("Yes, I'm showing you a before-and-after. The difference: I'm telling you the leaner one felt worse. The industry inverts that lie."). The inoculation needs to be IN the section, not implied.

2. **The brand reveal is too late.** By Section 6, a skeptical scanner has read 700 words from an unnamed authority. Standard funnel physics: name + proof of practice must arrive within the first two scrolls. Fix: add a 40-word "Who's writing this" block after Section 3's photo — "I'm Coach Cooz. I train in [LA neighborhood]. I've coached [N] people through this. Here's what I actually do." Not a bio. A trust anchor. Without it, conversion sits below the 0.6-1% viability line.

3. **The flyer distribution ask will get refused at upscale venues.** "Tell 'em you don't know" asks salon and juice-bar owners to lie to their clientele — the exact venues with brand-conscious customers who complain about profanity cards at the register. Fix either (a) segment distribution — barbershops, laundromats, physical therapy, chiropractic get the profanity variant; salons/juice bars/co-working get Variant 3 (no profanity) or a cleaned version — or (b) rewrite the ask so the venue owner isn't asked to hide the source ("If anyone asks, tell 'em it's from a local trainer testing an idea"). The current ask puts venue relationships at risk to preserve mystery.

### SHOULD-FIX (improves resilience)

4. **Offer math needs to be a visual stack, not a prose paragraph.** Three lines: "Triage Audit: $1,000 · 90-Day Protocol: $5,000 ($4,000 with podcast credit) · Audit credits toward Protocol = $4,000 net if you continue." Current prose forces mental math at the moment of highest CTA friction.

5. **No verification anchor on testimonials.** Every testimonial is verbatim per the plan doc — leverage that. One line under the proof block ("All quotes verbatim from coachcooz.com/reviews and Yelp") converts the strongest asset into a verifiable one at zero cost.

6. **"Only 3 spots this month" is the exact urgency pattern the villain section attacks.** The page critiques manufactured urgency ("shame marketing," "just believe harder"), then closes with manufactured scarcity. If 3 spots is real, say why ("I take 3 audits/month because each one is 90 min plus 3-4 hrs of prep"). If it's not real, cut it. The current line is the funnel's most contradictable claim.

7. **No location signal anywhere.** Flyer is LA-local, distribution is LA-local, booking priority is in-person LA — but the squeeze page never says "LA" or "Los Angeles." A scanner from outside LA wastes attention; a scanner from LA doesn't get the "he's here, in my city" trust boost. One line.

### INTERESTING RISK (awareness only)

8. **Variant 3 undermines its own hypothesis.** "This isn't a gym ad. Scan it anyway." — the second sentence tells you it IS a gym ad. If someone crumples fitness ads, this line confirms it before they scan. Kill Variant 3 or rewrite it.

9. **"Feel like shit" language collides with "I don't make medical claims."** The strategy spine bans depression/anxiety claims. Fine. But "feel like shit" is going to be read by half the audience as depression-adjacent. If a client shows up in an actual clinical episode expecting Cooz to help, the intake conversation gets harder. Not a legal risk — a filtering/UX risk.

10. **Testimonial gender skew.** Six testimonials on the squeeze page: Karima, Allison, Jess, Robin, Sammy, Jessica. Five are female-coded; Sammy is ambiguous. The flyer's tone is masculine-coded ("Hey man," profanity, protest-sign design). The proof block signals "I coach women who feel unseen"; the flyer signals "I talk to men in barbershops." Not fatal, but the two audiences receiving different signals from the same funnel will each wonder if they're in the right place.

11. **No email capture before Triage booking.** The funnel goes flyer → squeeze → $1,000 booking. That's a very steep single-step ask from a stranger. No middle-ground opt-in ("get the Resurrection audio," "get the anti-industry breakdown as a PDF") means the funnel captures only buyers, not warm leads. Reasonable at Phase 1; will bottleneck at Phase 2.

## Defensibility Narrative

The call-out vulnerability score is 5/10. Cooz needs a pre-built response to three predictable attacks:

**Attack 1: "He shows before-and-after photos while attacking before-and-after photos."**
Counter-narrative to bake into the page: "Yes, I'm showing you two photos. Here's the difference: the industry shows you week 1 vs. week 16 and tells you the transformation was the point. I'm showing you a photo of me at my leanest and telling you it was my worst. I'm showing you a photo of me heavier and telling you it was my best. The industry uses photos to make you want a body. I'm using photos to tell you the body wasn't the point." This has to live in Section 3, not in Cooz's head.

**Attack 2: "Self-reported body fat means the numbers are made up."**
Counter-narrative: "I could get a DEXA scan. I haven't. Not because the numbers wouldn't back me up — because the numbers weren't the point. The point was: at my leanest I felt worst, at my heaviest I felt best, and no scanner in the world measures that." Own the choice, don't defend it.

**Attack 3: "'Are you tired of feeling like shit?' is shame marketing."**
Counter-narrative: "Shame marketing tells you you're broken and only their product fixes you. This card asks a question and lets you walk away. You either recognize yourself or you don't. That's not shame. That's a mirror." Have Cooz say this on camera, once, and hold it in reserve for the moment someone attacks the flyer publicly.

Without these pre-built responses, the first hostile competitor post on IG will define the frame. With them, Cooz responds within an hour and the attack strengthens his position.

## Confidence

**8/10.** High confidence on the structural findings (brand reveal timing, offer math, photo contradiction, distribution ask friction) — these are mechanical funnel physics that don't depend on market interpretation. Lower confidence on the call-out vulnerability score, because it depends on how prominent Cooz becomes; at low traffic, no one attacks him and the vulnerabilities never activate. The 5/10 assumes the funnel works and generates enough visibility to attract critics. If it stays under 5,000 impressions, the vulnerabilities are theoretical.
