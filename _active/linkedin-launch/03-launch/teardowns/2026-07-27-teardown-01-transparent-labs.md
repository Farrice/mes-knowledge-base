## 1. CREDIT WHERE IT'S REAL

The Bulk Pre-Workout label is fully open. Eight grams of L-citrulline malate, four grams of beta-alanine, 2,500mg of betaine, 300mg of Alpha-GPC, 200mg of caffeine, all printed with the number attached. No proprietary blend, nowhere to hide.

That is the most expensive kind of honesty in this category, because once you print a dose you can never quietly shrink it. Most of the shelf will not do this. It costs real money per tub and it forecloses every future margin trick.

The doses are also not decorative. Eight grams of citrulline malate is a working dose, not a label dusting. Somebody in that building fought for it.

## 2. CLAIM → PROOF → GAP

**Claim A.** Homepage hero: *"Hold your supplements to a higher standard."* Supporting line: *"Reach your health and fitness goals with clean ingredients and transparent labels, so you know exactly what you're putting in your body."*

- **Proven:** the labels are genuinely open. The claim is true.
- **Gap:** "clean" and "higher standard" are free. Puori's homepage says *"Pure, natural and superior food supplements."* Momentous says *"Not The Industry Standard. The Momentous Standard®."* Three brands, one adjective, no separation. Higher than what is never answered anywhere on the page.

**Claim B.** Homepage pillars: *"Natural & Grass-Fed Ingredients"* · *"Complete Label Transparency"* · *"Clinically-Dosed Formulas"* · *"3rd-Party Lab Tested"*

- **Proven:** all four, on the label.
- **Gap:** three of the four are now table stakes. Grass-fed, transparent, third-party tested are printed on most premium tubs in 2026. Only one of the four is a real weapon, and it sits third in a list of four, phrased as a category.

**Claim C.** The testing page. *"Certificate of Analysis: Confirms a product's quality and purity by detailing test results from an accredited lab."*

- **Proven:** COAs are searchable by product name. That is a real system and most brands don't have one.
- **Gap:** the lab is never named. Not on that page, not anywhere in the flow. Momentous names LightLabs, NSF Certified for Sport, and Informed Sport. Puori names the Clean Label Project and IFOS. The brand called Transparent Labs says "an accredited lab."

**Claim D.** Homepage trust bar: *"58,934 Verified 5 Star Reviews From Our Customers."*

- **Proven:** presumably accurate.
- **Gap:** the whey isolate page shows 4.7 stars across 6,890 reviews. Counting only the five-star reviews is the one move on the whole site that reads like everybody else. A brand selling transparency should publish the average, which is excellent, rather than the filtered count, which is a technique.

## 4. THE GAP IN ONE LINE

They own the only word in the category nobody else can afford, and they are spending their homepage on the word everybody already has.

The word is not clean. Everybody is clean. The word is **full**.

---

## 5. THREE ANGLES I'D SHIP

**Angle 1 — Full, not clean.**
Retire "clean" from the hero. Lead on dose. The hero is a label photograph with the numbers legible and one line under it: eight grams of citrulline, and the dose is the whole product. Every competitor's hero becomes a comparison the moment yours is a number. This is the only one of the three that a competitor cannot copy by Friday, because copying it costs them margin.

**Angle 2 — Name the lab.**
One sentence and one logo on the testing page changes the entire claim from a promise to a receipt. Right now the strongest trust asset on the site is written in the passive voice. If there is a contractual reason the lab can't be named, say that instead, because the reason is more credible than the silence.

**Angle 3 — Sell the dose in the customer's own words.**
The reviews say "no digestive issues," "absorbs quickly," "helps with my recovery." Those are felt outcomes already in the customer's mouth. Tie each one back to a printed number on the label. The claim stays factual, the number does the persuading, and the language stays theirs. That is a full campaign and none of it requires a new study.

---

## 6. THE COMPLIANCE LINE

Dose disclosure is the safest claim surface in this category. Stating that a tub contains 8,000mg of L-citrulline malate is a fact about the product, not a health claim, so it carries no substantiation burden at all.

The line to watch: pairing a printed dose with a promised outcome converts a fact into a structure/function claim, which then needs the disclaimer and the evidence behind it. Keep the dose factual and let the reader draw the conclusion, and the whole campaign stays inside the fence.

The five-star-only review count is not an FTC problem on its face, but it is the kind of thing that ages badly for a brand whose name is a promise about honesty.
