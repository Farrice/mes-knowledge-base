**Changed 2026-08-06 via `/enchant elevate`** (Farrice's verdict: Take A). Two things moved:

- **Plural → singular.** "Claims" reads as a service menu; "the one claim" reads as a verdict, which is what the offer actually is — a decision, written down. It does the 5-second test's work for free.
- **A shape the ear can hold.** *buyer / buy* is one word in two forms, and "buy" carries both senses at once — buys the claim, buys the product. It lands on a hard one-syllable Saxon word, per the Latin×Saxon delivery fix in the voice card §6.

Prior version — `Claims your buyers believe | …` — failed the misquote test: a bare noun phrase with no repetition, reversal, or hard landing, so nothing for memory to grab. It was built for search retrieval and did that job; the keyword and proof segments are therefore unchanged.

```text
At The Vitamin Shoppe, a guy would walk in asking for a testosterone booster. He already had a diagnosis in his head. Low energy. Slow progress. Not enough muscle. The bottle seemed to connect all three.

I would ask about his training. His sleep. What he ate that day. How much protein he was getting.

Sometimes the better recommendation was whey.

Different person, different answer. A premium formula could earn the recommendation when the company disclosed the ingredients, used doses I could make sense of, and gave the customer something real to evaluate. Sometimes the right answer was no sale.

I was trying to match the product to the problem. The sale mattered, but the bigger risk was what the customer believed when the promise failed.

What the customer was really buying

People rarely walked into the store looking for powder, capsules, or a tub with a good label.

They wanted their energy back or the mirror to change. Mostly, they wanted proof that all the work could finally lead somewhere.

The product carried that hope.

When the promise failed, the customer did not always blame the formula or the marketing. Sometimes he blamed his body. His discipline. His genetics. He could leave with less confidence in the category and less confidence in himself.

That is a much more expensive loss than the price of the bottle.

A weak product sold with a strong story can take someone's money once. If the experience also teaches him that his effort never works, the damage follows him into the next purchase, the next program, and the next honest person trying to help.

I watched that doubt show up in the questions people asked. They were evaluating the next product, but they were also testing whether anything in the category deserved another chance.

The certification was only half the job

I have coached since 2008. I eventually earned Tier X at Equinox and worked with 1,000+ people.

The science mattered. So did everything the certifications never warned me about.

An independent trainer has to learn marketing, copywriting, selling, client acquisition, retention, and how to run a real business. You have to explain why your process makes sense without turning the client into a physiology student. You have to earn trust before the result exists. Then you have to keep earning it when progress gets messy.

Supplement questions were part of the job from year one. What should I take? Is this stack worth it? Will this help me get there faster?

Those questions taught me something I use in creative strategy now: the stated question is often carrying a deeper one.

The customer asks about an ingredient.

Underneath it, he may be asking whether the outcome is still possible for him.

The claim sits inside a longer chain

A supplement claim starts as a line of copy, but it lands inside a real decision. Someone wants a change. The brand makes a promise. The evidence has to hold up. If the product fails, the customer carries that doubt into the next purchase.

That changes the questions I want answered before anyone writes another hook:

1. Name the change the customer hopes this product will create.
2. Identify what has to already be true for the product to help.
3. Show the proof the brand can state plainly and defend when pushed.
4. Draw the line around what the customer should never expect this product to solve.

Once those answers are clear, the creative team knows what to say, what proof to lead with, and where restraint will earn more trust than another adjective.

The hook comes later.

Better marketing protects the attempt

I like supplements. I still buy them. The fit between the person, the goal, the formula, and the rest of the plan matters to a recommendation.

I have also seen opaque formulas, fairy-dust doses, and labels doing all the work.

The brands worth helping are the ones with something real to defend. Their problem is often that the truth takes longer to explain than the competitor's fantasy.

My job is to make the product's strongest true claim easy to understand and easy to defend.

Somewhere, someone is standing in an aisle or scrolling at 11 p.m., holding a product promise next to a private fear that nothing works for him.

A sale can leave him with a useful product and more control over the next step, or with an empty tub and one more reason to doubt himself.

That is the cost I care about.
```
