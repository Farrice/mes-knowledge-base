# The AI-Search Visibility Test

### 7 checks to find out whether ChatGPT can even see your brand — and whether the proof it finds will get you cited or get you a warning letter.

> **Run this on your own brand. Takes about 10 minutes.** No opt-in, no tool, no login. Just you, a ChatGPT tab, and an honest look at what the machine actually says about you. By the end you'll know exactly where your visibility is leaking — and whether the leak is a citation problem, a proof problem, or both.

---

Your rankings stayed flat but the clicks vanished.

If that sentence made you wince, you already know the thing the rank tracker won't tell you: search stopped being about *ranking* and started being about *being named*. When someone asks ChatGPT or Google's AI "what's the best magnesium for sleep" or "is your category's hero ingredient actually backed," the AI writes a paragraph and names a few brands as the answer. Inside that answer, people click out only about 8% of the time (Pew Research). Ranking #1 on a page nobody scrolls to is worth almost nothing now. Being **named in the answer** is worth almost everything.

And here's the part nobody tells health brands: in a regulated category, getting named is a **two-gate problem.** The AI won't carry a claim it can't trust — and the claims it trusts are, increasingly, the exact same claims that survive an FTC substantiation check. So you can't separate "will AI cite me?" from "will this claim survive scrutiny?" anymore. They're the same gate. This test grades you on both.

**How to score it:** Each check is GREEN (you're carried), YELLOW (you're inconsistent — the riskiest place to be, because you think you're fine), or RED (you're invisible or exposed). Be honest. A YELLOW you talk yourself into being a GREEN is exactly how a brand gets quietly written out of the category story.

## CHECK 1 — Does the AI name you for your category's #1 question?

**The signal:** Generative citation. Whether the machine surfaces *your* brand when a buyer asks the obvious question — or names a competitor instead.

**How to check:**
1. Open ChatGPT (logged out or in a temporary chat, so your own history doesn't bias it).
2. Type the single question your best customer would actually ask before buying in your category. Not your brand name — the *need*. ("Best protein for someone who keeps skipping workouts." "Magnesium glycinate vs. citrate for sleep." "Is ashwagandha actually backed by research.")
3. Read the answer. Are you named? Is a competitor named? Is *anyone* named, or does it stay generic?

**What good looks like:** Your brand appears by name in the answer, ideally with a reason ("X is often recommended for…"). **GREEN.** A competitor is named and you aren't: **RED** — and write down who got named, because that's the brand whose proof is organized for the machine, not the brand that's bigger than you. Nobody gets named: **YELLOW** — the category is up for grabs and you're not claiming it.

> The brands getting cited aren't bigger. Their proof is just organized so the machine can carry it.

## CHECK 2 — Do ChatGPT and Google's AI tell the *same* story about you?

**The signal:** Cross-engine consistency. The engines disagree on whose proof counts — ChatGPT cites elite health sources about 1% of the time; Google AI Overviews does it 33% of the time (BrightEdge). A brand that wins one and loses the other has a structural gap, not a luck problem.

**How to check:**
1. Take the same category question from Check 1.
2. Run it in ChatGPT. Then run it in Google and read the AI Overview at the top (the gray AI box, not the blue links).
3. Compare: are you named in both, one, or neither? Do they describe you the same way?

**What good looks like:** Named in both, described consistently. **GREEN.** Named in one engine but not the other: **YELLOW** — you're optimized for one citation market and invisible in the other (and they're separate markets now). Named in neither: **RED.** If the two engines describe you *differently* — one says "premium recovery brand," the other says "budget flavor packets" — that's a sign your proof isn't organized; the machine is guessing.

## CHECK 3 — Does your proof live anywhere other than your own website?

**The signal:** Third-party and community surface area. AI engines trust what *other* people say about you more than what you say about yourself. Reddit is the #1 cited domain across all three major engines (Otterly). If your authority only lives on your own .com, you're invisible to the machines that read everything else.

**How to check:**
1. Search your brand name + your hero claim on Reddit, in YouTube descriptions, in third-party "best of" roundups, and on any forum your buyer uses.
2. Ask ChatGPT directly: "What do people say about [your brand] on Reddit or in reviews?" See what it pulls.
3. Count the surfaces where your proof shows up that *you don't own.*

**What good looks like:** Your claims and your name appear on third-party and community surfaces — reviewed, discussed, cited — not just on your own product pages. **GREEN.** It's all on your own site: **RED** — to the machine, a brand whose proof only lives on its own marketing pages reads as "just marketing" and gets filtered. Sparse third-party presence: **YELLOW.**

> If your brand is invisible in community discussion, you are invisible in AI search.

## CHECK 4 — Can your biggest claim survive a substantiation check? (the gate most brands skip)

**The signal:** FTC substantiation. The AI-trust gate and the legal gate are now the same gate — content that survives FTC scrutiny is the content AI cites. The FTC requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence" for objective health claims, and the penalty is **$53,088 per violation** (effective Jan 2025). A claim the machine *and* a regulator both distrust is a double liability.

**How to check:**
1. Pull your single strongest marketing claim — the one on the hero banner or the PDP headline. ("Clinically proven to…" "Boosts…" "Supports faster…")
2. Ask the brutal question: *if a regulator asked me to show the evidence behind this exact wording, could I — for THIS product, at THIS dose, in a human study?* Not "the ingredient has research." This product, this dose.
3. Check the verb: are you saying a supplement "treats / cures / prevents" anything? Supplements may only *support* a structure or function (21 CFR 101.93). "Treats anxiety" is a landmine; "supports a calm response" is defensible.

**What good looks like:** Every objective claim ties to evidence for *your* formula at *your* dose, and your verbs stay in structure/function language. **GREEN.** Strong claims you'd struggle to document, or "treat/cure/prevent" language anywhere: **RED** — this is the claim that blows up, and it's also the claim AI won't carry. Borderline wording: **YELLOW.**

> You're paying more for the marketing than for what's actually in the bottle — that's what your customer thinks the moment a claim doesn't match the science they can find in 10 seconds. The machine thinks it too.

## CHECK 5 — Is there a credentialed human entity behind your content?

**The signal:** Entity authority. AI engines reward content with a real, identifiable, credentialed person attached — a named expert with verifiable standing, not an anonymous "Team" byline. Citations, statistics, and expert quotations lift generative visibility by up to 40% (Princeton/Georgia Tech). "Reviewed by the [Brand] Team" is not an entity. A named human with credentials is.

**How to check:**
1. Look at who's credited on your blog posts, your PDP science sections, your "about the research" content. Is it a named person? Do they have verifiable credentials a machine can find?
2. Ask ChatGPT: "Who is the expert behind [your brand]'s content?" See if it can name anyone.
3. Check whether that person exists as a consistent entity across surfaces — LinkedIn, author bios, quotes in third-party pieces — so the machine can connect the dots.

**What good looks like:** A real, named, credentialed person is consistently attached to your claims and findable across the web. **GREEN.** Anonymous "Team" bylines or no author at all: **RED** — the machine has no entity to trust, so it trusts a Reddit thread instead. A founder or expert who *has* the credibility but never shows up in the content: **YELLOW** — this is the most common gap, and the easiest 40% to win back.

## CHECK 6 — Does your content read like it was written by someone who's done the thing?

**The signal:** Lived-expertise depth (the un-synthesizable layer). AI didn't flatten your brand voice — it revealed you never had one. The flood of "polite LinkedIn thought-leader" content all sounds the same because a free model wrote it. The content that gets *carried* is the content a free model couldn't have generated: specific mechanism, real physiology, a point of view only experience produces.

**How to check:**
1. Take your last 5 pieces of content. Ask honestly: *could ChatGPT have written this in 10 seconds with no special knowledge?* If yes, the machine has no reason to cite you over its own answer.
2. Look for the tell: does your content explain the *failure* (why most products in your category don't work, the actual physiological reason), or does it just make a louder promise?
3. Read one piece out loud. Does it sound like a corporate press release written by someone who's never actually done the thing — or like someone who's been inside the problem?

**What good looks like:** Your content carries specific, lived, mechanism-level insight a generic model can't reproduce — it explains the failure, not just the promise. **GREEN.** It reads like scaled, edgeless, "could've been written by anyone" content: **RED.** Some depth, inconsistent: **YELLOW.**

> In a loud market, authority comes from explaining the failure, not making a louder promise. AI is your research engine — it should never be your voice.

## CHECK 7 — When AI describes your category, are you the *source* or the *footnote*?

**The signal:** Source authority vs. erasure. The deepest version of the visibility problem: not "am I mentioned" but "when the machine explains my whole category, does it speak in my brand's framing — or am I a footnote in a competitor's answer?" This is the difference between owning the category narrative and being written out of it.

**How to check:**
1. Ask ChatGPT: "Explain [your category] to someone deciding what to buy — what should they look for?"
2. Read the framing. Are the criteria the machine lists the criteria *you* champion? Is your brand positioned as the example, the standard, the source? Or does the answer sound like a competitor wrote the rules and you're (maybe) listed at the bottom?
3. Notice whose language the machine uses. The brand whose framing the AI adopts owns the category in AI search.

**What good looks like:** The machine explains your category using criteria you own, and positions you as a/the source. **GREEN.** You appear, but as a footnote inside a story a competitor's framing is telling: **YELLOW.** The category gets explained and you're nowhere in it: **RED** — you're being written out of your own category story, and the rank tracker will never show you this.
