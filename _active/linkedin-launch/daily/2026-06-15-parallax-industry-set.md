## VARIATION A — "The two numbers" *(confessional / coach's lens)*
**Cook:** open on another person's documented collapse → sit in it → confession pivot to Farrice's 18 years watching worth measured by the wrong number → POV: the market only ever paid for the typing. **Bucket:** Growth/recognition. **Close:** bill-coming-due declaration, anchored to the sister.

```
A copywriter I'll never meet wrote down the two numbers that ended his business. Six hundred thousand dollars in 2022. Under ten thousand last year.

In between, he had to let go of his whole team. One of them was his little sister.

The detail that stayed with me wasn't the money. It was something he wrote almost flatly, like a man reading off the weather: being told, subconsciously or outright, that your expertise isn't valued or needed anymore does something to a person. It dehumanizes you.

I sat with that longer than I meant to.

I spent eighteen years in rooms where people's worth got measured by the wrong number. A scale. A skinfold reading. A before-and-after somebody could post. I watched coaches who genuinely understood how a body works lose clients to whoever promised the fastest result, because the market couldn't see the difference between real knowledge and a clean graphic. The skill was invisible. The output was the only thing anyone paid for.

AI walked into that room and stood next to everyone in it. Suddenly you could tell, from across the floor, who had been selling the thing they made and who had been selling the judgment about what to make.

If your value lived in producing the thing, a machine that produces the thing in seconds was always going to feel like a death. If your value lived in knowing which thing to make, and why, and for whom, the machine just handed you a faster pair of hands.

I won't pretty that up. Try telling a man who let his own sister go that this is a healthy repricing of talent. The pain is real, it is landing on people who did everything the old rules asked, and the old rules are exactly what broke.

The work was always the judgment. We just got away with charging for the output, because for a long time the output was the hard part.

The market never paid for the part it couldn't see. It paid for the typing. So the day a machine could type for free, the invoice collapsed, and it took his sister with it. That bill was always going to come due.
```

## VARIATION B — "The most expensive average ever made" *(observational / strategist's lens)*
**Cook:** open on a specific artifact (the Coca-Cola AI ad) → name the half-second wrongness → pattern-spot up to WPP/Ogilvy → POV: they aimed the most powerful divergence tool ever built at the median. **Bucket:** Authority. **Close:** divergence-engine principle, declaration.

```
Coca-Cola made its holiday ad with AI again this year. Second year in a row. The reporting put it near seventy thousand generated clips, refined by a team of about a hundred people, to rebuild thirty seconds they used to simply film.

Watch it and you feel something a half-second before you can name it. The snow falls correctly. The trucks glow. The faces almost hold. And the warmth the old version used to take from you without asking just isn't in the room.

The data caught what the body already knew. Positive sentiment toward the campaign slid from around twenty-four percent to ten after it went live.

What I keep turning over is the waste of it. Coca-Cola is holding the most powerful divergence engine ever built. A tool that could take a hundred years of one specific feeling and push the brand somewhere no competitor could follow. They used it to land in the exact middle. They made the most expensive average ever produced.

And it isn't one brand. WPP just collapsed the agencies that built modern advertising into a single AI-run company. Ogilvy, the shop founded on the belief that the work had to be unmistakably its own, now helps power something the holding company literally calls a Super Agent.

Every model is trained on the same internet. Point a thousand brands at the same model with the same instincts and they all drift toward the same center, the same competent shimmer. The strategists already have a name for it. Sameness. The work blurs, people stop noticing, and performance quietly drops, because familiar is just another word for invisible.

Identical is the real cost. And a machine trained on everyone has no other setting to land on.

The brands that win the next ten years will be the ones who understood that a tool trained on the average is only dangerous if you ask it to take you there.
```

## VARIATION C — "I argue with a machine every morning" *(framework / contrarian POV)*
**Cook:** open on Farrice's own daily ritual → bring the replaced-consultant story as the foil → teach the divergence-engine lens (how it SHOULD be used: proprietary inputs, refusals, aimed off the median). **Bucket:** Conversion/authority. **Close:** declaration + 6am callback, the part they can't freeze.

```
Most mornings, the first real work I do is argue with a machine.

Not prompt it. Argue with it. I hand it a draft and tell it why the obvious version is wrong, what I would never say, who exactly I'm writing for and the fear sitting under the thing they actually asked for. Then I point it somewhere the average can't follow.

I work this way because of a story I can't shake. A strategy consultant lost the retainer that covered half her living when her biggest client gave thirty days' notice. She's convinced they spent those thirty days building her replacement out of two years of work she'd already handed them, a custom model trained on her own deliverables. If she's right, she trained the thing that replaced her and never knew she was doing it.

Everyone passes that around as a warning about AI. To me it's a warning about something older: what happens when all of your value sits on the outside of you, copyable the moment someone collects enough of it.

A model walks toward the average of whatever you feed it. Feed it the open internet and it hands the internet back to you, smoothed. Feed it two years of one consultant's deliverables and it hands back a competent forgery of that consultant. It is a mirror that averages. Whatever you aim it at, it drifts to the middle of.

Most people use it exactly backwards. They pour in the generic, write me a post about leadership, and they get the generic back, then decide the tool is shallow. Call it shallow if you want. It was only being obedient. It went precisely where the inputs pointed, which was nowhere in particular.

My whole job now runs the other direction. I don't ask the machine to be me. I hand it the things that are only mine, the way I diagnose a problem, the moves I refuse to make, the specific person and the specific wound I'm writing toward, and I aim it off the average on purpose. Same tool everyone else is holding. Pointed where the average can't go.

That's the thing the spreadsheet hasn't caught yet. The client who replaced the consultant didn't capture her judgment. They captured her output and froze it. The first day the work needed to change, the forgery kept confidently producing what she would have said a year ago.

Her frozen ghost still answers every brief in that year-old voice, fluent, certain, a little more wrong each month. That is what a captured judgment becomes. Mine stays alive because I make it fight me at six in the morning. So feed the machine the part of you that keeps moving, the part that was never for sale. That is the only part they can never freeze.
```
