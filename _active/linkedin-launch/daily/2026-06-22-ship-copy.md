POST 1

Pharma spent an estimated 8 billion on ads last year. The AI engines were asked this month which brands to trust, and the budget didn't move the answer.

5W published the test on the 19th. The biggest direct-to-consumer ad category in the country, and the models named the brands they named for reasons that had almost nothing to do with who spent the most.

We already watched this happen in supplements. Earlier this year the same firm pushed 3,800 category questions through ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google's AI answers. Four brands took almost half the citations. AG1, reportedly the largest greens brand by revenue, came in third. The winners all shared one trait: they were the most quotable brands in the category.

What you can buy is the problem. Reach, polish, another ten variants, none of it moves the buyer who never reaches your site, because she asked a model first and the model already answered.

Spending more to win that shelf is like buying the best billboard on a highway after they rerouted the traffic. The sign still works. The cars are somewhere else now.

A model reads your substance, not your budget. It quotes the dosing, the mechanism, the study, the one claim it can repeat without hedging. If your real proof is trapped inside a PDF and a beautiful paragraph, the machine can't quote it, so it quotes whoever made theirs legible.

I spent 18 years watching people pay for the gym and skip the one thing that actually changed the body. This is the brand version of that. You can buy almost everything except the proof that you're worth recommending.

The shelf that matters now was never for sale. It goes to whoever made their proof clear enough for a machine to read out loud.


POST 2

LinkedIn spent June demoting the carousel.

The format that won last year is the tell.

For about 18 months the PDF carousel was the closest thing this platform had to a cheat code. Swipeable, save-able, easy to template, easy to outsource. So everyone built them, then built tools to build them faster.

This month the people who watch the algorithm closely started seeing the same thing in their dashboards. Carousels sliding. Plain text posts that pick a real fight, the kind that pull thirty people into the comments arguing, climbing right past them.

I'm not surprised, and not because I understand LinkedIn. Because I spent 17 years in gyms.

A program would get someone shredded. Word spread. Everyone copied it move for move. Within a season the copy stopped working, while the person who understood why it worked adjusted and kept winning. The exercise was the same the whole way through. What it lost was its rarity.

Nothing about the carousel got worse. It became common, and common is invisible. A feed, like a buyer, stops seeing the thing it has already seen a thousand times this week.

Watch what's underneath it. The platform is doing to formats exactly what AI is doing to content. A template costs nothing to make now, so it gets priced down. The thing a machine can't make, a point of view somebody will argue with on a Tuesday, is the only thing left holding its price.

So the format you reach for should fail one test on purpose. A thousand people with the same tools should not be able to clone it by Friday.

That's the only thing this feed has ever rewarded. We keep mistaking the format for the reason.


POST 3

2024: AI content was the shortcut everyone wanted.

2026: the platforms are stamping it with a warning label.

The premium flipped, and most brands missed it.

The backlash against AI content stopped being a vibe this year and became infrastructure. Feeds are starting to label it. People are organizing against the flood of it. The thing everyone rushed to use is now the thing platforms are quietly tagging.

There's a moment I saw a hundred times coaching people. Someone automates the part of their week that was supposed to save them time, and a year later that exact shortcut is the reason they're stuck. They optimized the thing that didn't matter and outsourced the thing that did.

Wellness brands are living that story right now. They automated the trust layer, the part where a real, named human stands behind the claim, to save a few hours. Then they flooded the discovery layer with content the machines ignore. Backwards on both ends.

When perfect gets free, the imperfect human signal becomes the expensive one. Ogilvy already named it, the authenticity recession. The labels just made it official.

Your buyer knew before the platforms did. She can smell automation by the second line, because she's read AI-written everything and she trusts her gut over your homepage. She's buying one thing the label can't hold: the belief that a person who has lived this stands behind the promise.

I spent 18 years watching what gets someone to keep a promise to their own body. What kept them going was never the polished plan, it was the sense that a real person had them.

The face, the name, the reputation on the line. That stopped being the soft part of the brand. It's the one part a machine can't counterfeit, and the last part your buyer is still paying full price for.


ARTICLE

The Two Funnels: Why AI Makes Your Discovery Cheaper and Your Trust More Expensive

Two things happened to your brand in the last eighteen months, and they pull in opposite directions.

Producing content got close to free. And being believed got harder than it has ever been.

Most founders only felt the first one. The cost of a post dropped to nothing, so they did the rational thing and made more. More posts, more variants, more "we ship daily now." It felt like progress. It was the slow sanding-down of the one thing they couldn't replace.

There's a more useful way to see your business. You are not running one funnel. You are running two, and AI is doing the opposite thing to each.

The top of the funnel, discovery, now belongs to the machines.

When someone wants to know what to take, what to buy, who to trust, they don't open ten tabs anymore. They ask a model, and they take the answer it hands them. That answer is the new shelf. If you're not on it, you don't exist, however good the product is.

This month gave us the cleanest proof yet. 5W published its Pharma AI Visibility Index on June 19. U.S. pharma spent an estimated eight billion dollars on direct-to-consumer ads last year, the largest category-level ad commitment in the country. Asked which brands to trust, the AI engines landed on the brands they landed on for reasons that had almost nothing to do with who spent the most.

We already watched the same physics play out in supplements. Earlier this year the same firm pushed 3,800 category questions through ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google's AI answers. Four brands (Thorne, Seed, AG1, Momentous) took more than 47% of the citations tracked. AG1, reportedly the largest greens brand by revenue, came in third. What earned the shelf was structured, machine-readable proof, not budget or beauty: dosing, mechanism, a claim a model can quote without hedging.

Fight this half of the funnel with human craft and you lose. Feed it clean, quotable substance and you win. The brutal part: the things you can buy, reach, polish, another ten variants, do almost nothing here, because the buyer got intercepted upstream by a model that never saw your homepage.

The middle is where the format wars are being lost.

Look at what just happened on LinkedIn itself. For eighteen months the PDF carousel was the cheat code, swipeable, save-able, easy to template. So everyone made them, then bought tools to make them faster. This month the people who track the algorithm are watching carousels slide while plain text posts that start a real argument climb past them.

That's not a LinkedIn quirk. It's the same law operating one level up. When a format becomes cheap to mass-produce, the feed treats it as wallpaper and stops seeing it. That's exactly what's happening to AI content everywhere. The platform is pricing down what a machine can make and pricing up what it can't. A template costs nothing now. A point of view someone will argue with on a Tuesday is the only thing holding its price.

You can hear the same note in the agency world. WPP, Omnicom, and the rest are cutting tens of thousands of roles and calling it AI efficiency; Forrester expects a 15% cut to the US ad workforce by 2027. But read the fine print. Even insiders debate how much of that is AI capability and how much is AI as a convenient story for ordinary cost-cutting. What's being removed is the 55 to 70% of agency hours that were always execution and coordination. What survives, as Digiday put it, is taste, the thing that can't be manufactured at scale and has to be earned one good call at a time.

The bottom of the funnel, the decision to trust you, runs on the opposite fuel.

Your buyer arrives having read AI-written everything, and skepticism is her default setting. This year the backlash stopped being a vibe and became infrastructure: platforms are labeling AI content and deprioritizing the obvious slop. Ogilvy named the underlying shift the authenticity recession. As scalable perfection floods the world, visible human imperfection becomes the premium. The labels made it official.

This half of your funnel rewards exactly what the top punishes. A real face. A named practitioner. A claim with a reputation riding on it. The founders losing money optimized the wrong half: they automated the trust layer to save time and flooded the discovery layer with content the machines ignore. Backwards on both ends.

Below is the tangible asset for this week. Run it on your own brand, or a client's, in about twenty minutes.

[The Funnel Bifurcation Audit, Part 1: open ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI mode and paste your real category questions one at a time. Best category and why. Is it worth it and which brands are trusted. Customer situation and what to consider. Compare the top brands. What the research says about your key ingredient. Score each: do you appear, is it accurate, does it cite your real proof. If you're absent or misrepresented, the problem is rarely the product. It's that your proof lives in formats a machine can't parse. The fix is to publish your dosing, mechanism, and study data as clean, structured, quotable text. Boring to a designer, catnip to a model.]

[Part 2: walk your buyer's path, first click to checkout, and count where a stranger could prove a real human stands behind the claim. A named person answering, not "our team." A face on camera making the promise. A practitioner with a verifiable reputation. Real, specific testimonials. A claim a regulator could trace to a person. The read: low at the top means you're invisible to the machines now doing the recommending. Low at the bottom means you're discovered and then disqualified at the door by a buyer who can smell automation. Almost everyone is lopsided.]

I spent eighteen years watching what moves a body through change. What moved them was never the most polished plan, it was the sense that a real person had them and a process they could feel working. The brands that win the next two years run on the same truth: legible enough for a machine to recommend at the top, unmistakably human at the moment someone decides to trust you.

The cheaper your content gets, the more your humanity is worth. Price it accordingly.
