## LinkedIn Post - Ready Draft

### Teardown Tuesday: the sleep claim that sounds stronger than its receipt

A supplement brand says its magnesium blend helps you finally fix your sleep.

The buyer wants to believe it.

She is tired.

She has tried melatonin, no-phone rules, blue-light glasses, and the same five "sleep hygiene" tips every brand repeats.

So "finally fix your sleep" hits.

The problem is not that the promise is emotionally wrong.

The problem is that the receipt is missing.

Magnesium is a real mineral. NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements notes that it is involved in hundreds of enzyme systems and supports normal muscle and nerve function.

That gives the brand a real starting point.

But a sleep product page has to carry more than a starting point.

If the page implies the product fixes sleep, treats insomnia, or solves a sleep disorder, the claim has moved into a much heavier proof lane.

FTC expects health-related claims to be backed by competent and reliable scientific evidence.

The dietary supplement structure/function lane also has boundaries. A brand can describe support for normal structure or function, but it cannot drift into treating, curing, or preventing disease.

This is where most wellness copy leaks trust.

The claim is written for conversion.

The evidence is buried somewhere else.

The caveat disappears.

The buyer is asked to trust too early.

If I were auditing this page, I would not start by polishing the headline.

I would ask five questions:

1. Which form of magnesium is in the product?
2. What dose is used per serving?
3. What source supports this exact sleep-adjacent claim?
4. Does the page distinguish relaxation support from treating sleep problems?
5. Can a skeptical buyer find the proof within one scroll of the promise?

If those answers are missing, the stronger copy is not louder.

It is more carried.

A safer line might sound like:

"A magnesium blend designed to support normal muscle and nervous-system function as part of your evening routine."

That line will not win a hype contest.

But it gives the brand room to place the actual receipt:

- magnesium form
- dose
- study or source note
- who it is for
- who it is not for
- what the product does not claim to solve

That is the part AI search can repeat without flattening the brand into every other sleep supplement.

And it is the part a smart buyer quietly looks for before she trusts you.

The useful test:

Take your strongest product claim and ask:

"What proof would a skeptical buyer need to repeat this claim to someone else without feeling sold to?"

If the answer lives in a PDF, podcast, founder interview, or old email, the claim is not weak.

It is trapped.

That is the audit.

## Proof Notes

### VERIFIED

NIH ODS describes magnesium as involved in many enzyme systems and notes its role in normal muscle and nerve function.

Source:

https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/

FTC guidance says health-related advertising claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence. The guidance also warns against relying on weak or mismatched evidence for strong health claims.

Source:

https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance

eCFR 21 CFR 101.93 governs structure/function claims for dietary supplements and requires the standard disclaimer when applicable. It also separates structure/function support from disease claims.

Source:

https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-F/section-101.93

### LIKELY

Sleep supplement buyers often respond to relief-oriented language because sleep problems carry frustration, fatigue, and a desire for a simple answer.

Label this as buyer psychology, not a sourced clinical fact.

### UNCONFIRMED

No actual brand page was tested.

No AI-search prompt was run.

No claim is being made that ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, or Gemini currently name or ignore any specific magnesium brand.
