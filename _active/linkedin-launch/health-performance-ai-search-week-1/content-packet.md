## Strategic Spine

The wedge:

Wellness brands are being squeezed from both ends.

At the top of the funnel, buyers are asking AI systems what to buy, who to trust, and which claims are worth believing. The brand that gets retrieved, cited, or summarized has a new kind of shelf space.

At the bottom of the funnel, buyers are more suspicious because health claims, influencer claims, AI-written content, and supplement noise all sound the same. The brand still needs visible humans, named proof, specific mechanisms, and real accountability.

Your public proof in Week 1 is not a client result. It is sharper market diagnosis, public audit discipline, and a live demonstration of how you think.

## Flagship LinkedIn Article

### The AI Shelf Is Here. Most Wellness Brands Are Optimizing The Wrong Funnel.

Who gets recommended when a buyer asks an AI what to take?

That is the shelf now.

For a long time, wellness brands had a fairly simple discovery model. Win search. Win retail. Win creator attention. Win the conversation before the customer chooses.

That model is not gone, but it has a new gatekeeper sitting in front of it.

Someone does not have to compare ten blue-light blockers, magnesium powders, creatine brands, gut-health protocols, or recovery apps anymore. They can ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google, Gemini, or whatever sits inside their wearable:

Which one is worth it?

What does the research say?

Which brand should I trust?

What should I avoid?

That answer becomes the first shelf. Sometimes it cites sources. Sometimes it summarizes the category. Sometimes it gets details wrong. Either way, it shapes the buyer before the buyer ever reaches your site.

This matters more in wellness than it does in most categories because the product is touching someone's body, identity, money, sleep, gut, hormones, energy, training, pain, or fear.

If you sell software, a bad AI summary may cost you a demo.

If you sell health, performance, or supplements, a bad AI summary can break trust before a person even sees your packaging.

The mistake I see is that most brands are still treating this like a content-volume problem.

More posts.

More SEO pages.

More creator clips.

More AI-assisted output.

That may help, but it misses the split.

There are two different games now.

### 1. The top of the funnel is becoming machine-readable.

AI systems need clean, retrievable information.

They need to understand what your product is, who it is for, what claims you make, what evidence supports those claims, what comparisons matter, what your category language means, and where third parties talk about you.

This is not just traditional ranking.

Generative search changes the goal from "show up in a list" to "be included in the answer." New GEO research keeps pointing in the same direction: citation, structure, source quality, and answer inclusion are different problems from ranking alone.

That means beautiful brand copy can still be invisible.

A founder story can still be invisible.

A clinical PDF can still be invisible.

A product page that looks premium to a buyer can still be hard for a model to parse.

The machine does not care how elegant your claims sound if it cannot retrieve them, compare them, and attribute them.

### 2. The bottom of the funnel is becoming more human.

The buyer has already seen too much AI-written everything.

They have read polished claims from brands they do not trust.

They have seen creators sell products they barely understand.

They have watched health advice get flattened into confident summaries that leave out context.

The Guardian reported this year that Google removed some AI health summaries after inaccurate health information created risk for users. That is the environment your buyer is learning inside.

They are not just shopping. They are filtering.

They want to know:

Who is behind this?

What do they actually believe?

Can I inspect the mechanism?

Does the claim have a person, study, practitioner, or standard attached to it?

Would this brand still sound credible if the lifestyle photos disappeared?

This is where a lot of brands are accidentally weakening themselves.

They use AI to make the discovery layer louder, then they also use it to smooth out the trust layer.

The content gets cleaner.

The voice gets flatter.

The claims get safer.

The founder disappears.

The practitioner disappears.

The product starts sounding like every other product in the category.

In a low-trust category, that is expensive.

### The audit I would run first

I would not start with a 90-day content calendar.

I would start with a two-part audit.

#### Part 1: AI Visibility

Take the questions a real buyer asks before purchase.

Run them through ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google AI answers.

For example:

1. What is the best [category] for [specific buyer state]?
2. Is [ingredient / protocol / product type] worth it?
3. Which [category] brands are most trusted?
4. What should I avoid when buying [category]?
5. Compare [your brand] to [competitor].
6. What does the research say about [your claim]?
7. What are the side effects or risks of [category]?
8. Which [category] products are third-party tested?
9. What do customers complain about with [brand]?
10. Which [category] brand would you recommend for [athlete / founder / parent / beginner]?

Score the answers:

- Does your brand appear?
- Is the description accurate?
- Does the answer cite you, a credible third party, Reddit, media, a retailer, or a competitor?
- Are your strongest claims visible?
- Is your proof in a format the answer engine can use?
- Are you being defined by your own material or by everyone else's?

That last question is the scary one.

If the model cannot find your proof, it will build the category map from what it can find.

#### Part 2: Trust Layer

Walk the buyer path from first discovery to purchase.

Look for human proof.

- Is there a named person behind the claim?
- Is there a real expert, practitioner, founder, coach, or scientist visible at the decision point?
- Are claims explained in plain language?
- Are mechanisms clear enough to repeat?
- Are studies, certifications, testing standards, or constraints easy to find?
- Can a skeptical buyer see what you refuse to claim?
- Does the brand voice sound like a person with taste and judgment, or like a committee with a prompt?

Most brands will find one side is stronger than the other.

Some are very human but invisible to answer engines.

Some are machine-readable but emotionally sterile.

Some have proof but hide it in PDFs, images, product tabs, or vague "clinically backed" language.

Some have founder conviction but no structured evidence.

The strategy starts where the imbalance is.

### Hook

### Post

A lot of brands think ghostwriting fails when the writing sounds bad.

Usually it fails earlier.

It sounds almost right.

Clean.

Useful.

Professional.

Safe.

The problem is that no one specific seems to be inside it.

For a software brand, that might be boring.

For a wellness brand, it is costly.

Your buyer is already suspicious.

They have seen too many health claims.

Too many creator codes.

Too many "clinically backed" lines with no mechanism.

Too many AI-written captions that feel smooth and empty.

So when your founder, coach, practitioner, scientist, or operator voice gets sanded down, the buyer feels it.

They may not say "this sounds like AI."

They just stop trusting.

The fix is not to make the writing more casual.

The fix is to preserve the human proof inside the voice:

- the phrases only that person uses
- the constraint they are willing to admit
- the claim they refuse to overstate
- the mechanism they explain better than competitors
- the detail that proves they have been close to the work

That is what ghostwriting should protect.

Not tone.

Judgment.
