## SECTION 2 — Claim-Safety Map · Score: 5/10 (the body-expert section)

**Opens with:** *"You're paying more for the marketing than for what's actually in the bottle."* Create's bottle is honest — 5g creatine monohydrate, the most-studied form. The risk isn't the product. It's the *cognitive* and *healthy-aging* framing the whole category is racing toward, where a real benefit gets stated one notch stronger than the evidence supports. FTC penalty: **$53,088 per violation [VERIFIED]**. Supplements may only *support*, never *treat/cure/prevent*; structure/function claims need the **21 CFR 101.93** disclaimer and "competent and reliable scientific evidence" **[VERIFIED — FTC]**.

### Claim inventory (representative category phrasings — **[VERIFY LIVE]** against Create's live copy)

| # | Claim (verbatim — confirm on page) | Type | FTC risk | Net-impression risk | AI-citable as written? | Safe + citable rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Creatine for your brain and body" | structure-function (cognitive) | **Med–High** | Buyer may hear "improves brain function / prevents decline." Cognitive creatine evidence is real but **condition-specific** (sleep-deprived, older adults, stressed states) — not a blanket "boosts your brain." | No — too vague to lift, too broad to be safe | "Creatine is being studied for cognitive support under demand — like sleep deprivation and aging. [cite a specific trial]. *Not evaluated by the FDA; not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.*" |
| 2 | "Supports memory and mental clarity" | structure-function (cognitive) | **High** | "Memory" reads adjacent to cognitive *decline/dementia* — a disease-prevention implication the evidence does not carry for healthy adults. | No | Drop "memory" unless tied to a specific cited population. "Supports mental energy during cognitively demanding periods. [cite]." |
| 3 | "Backed by 500+ studies" | substantiation claim about creatine generally | **Med** | True of *creatine as an ingredient*; implies *this product/these benefits* are all study-backed. Net impression overshoots. | Partially — strong if scoped | "Creatine monohydrate is one of the most-researched supplement ingredients, with 500+ published studies on the molecule. [link a review]." Scope it to the molecule, not the gummy's every benefit. |
| 4 | "Clinically studied 5g dose" | ingredient/dose | **Low** | Defensible — 5g/day is the studied maintenance dose. Net impression sits inside the evidence. | **Yes** — a number + a mechanism is exactly liftable | Keep. Add the citation inline so the machine can carry it. |
| 5 | "Great for women — fights fatigue and supports healthy aging" | structure-function (population + aging) | **High** | "Fights fatigue" + "healthy aging" together drift toward treating a condition / anti-aging benefit. Women's creatine data is promising but early; "fights fatigue" is a near-drug claim. | No | "Women are often under-studied in creatine research; emerging work looks at strength and energy support across the lifespan. [cite]. Supports normal energy metabolism." Replace "fights fatigue" with the structure/function frame. |
| 6 | "As effective as powder" | comparative efficacy | **Med** | Bioequivalence of a gummy delivery vs powder needs *this product's* data, not creatine's general data. If the gummy's creatine isn't stability-tested, this is unsubstantiated. | Yes if proven | "Each gummy delivers a verified 5g of creatine monohydrate, third-party tested for content. [link COA]." Claim the *dose delivered*, which you can prove — not "as effective," which needs a head-to-head. |

**The body-expert move a generalist misses:** Claims #1, #2, #5 all *sound* fine and a GEO shop would happily "optimize" them for citation — which would amplify an FTC-exposed claim into the AI answer. A legal reviewer would flag them but couldn't tell you the *citable* version, because they don't know which creatine-cognition studies support which population. Grading these correctly requires reading the actual evidence behind the physiology claim: cognitive creatine benefits are real but **load-dependent and population-specific** (sleep-deprived, stressed, older adults), not a general "brain booster." That single distinction is the difference between a claim that draws a letter and a claim the machine cites. **That is the seam — and the safe rewrite raises the citation score every time, because a scoped, cited claim is the carryable one.**

**Score 5/10:** Real product, honest dose (#4 is exemplary), but the cognitive and women's-health framing (#1, #2, #5) carries High net-impression risk *and* is uncitable as written — the two failures are the same failure. Closing them closes both gates at once.

A High flag is a "have your counsel look before this goes wider," not a verdict. No finding here is a diagnosis, treatment recommendation, or health representation about any individual.*

## SECTION 5 — Divergence Angles + Named Position · Score: 8/10 (high opportunity)

The gaps in 1–4 are wide open *because* the proof is real. That's a high-opportunity score.

### 4 divergence angles (each fuses: buyer wince → Create's real proof → claim-safe frame → retrieval cue)

1. **"Creatine for women is under-studied — here's exactly what the research does and doesn't say."** Owns the women's-creatine question honestly (the High-risk #5 claim, reframed as authority). Retrieval cue: a dated, cited "what the evidence supports / what it doesn't" definition block. Being the brand that *names the limitation* is the trust move **[VoC: "authority comes from explaining the failure, not making a louder promise"]**.
2. **"Gummy vs powder: what's actually equivalent, and what isn't."** Turns the #6 comparative-efficacy risk into Create's most citable asset — *if* backed by the COA/dose-delivered proof. Retrieval cue: a number (verified 5g delivered) + a method (third-party content test).
3. **"The truth about creatine and 'brain fog.'"** Reframes the #1/#2 cognitive claims into the claim-safe, population-specific version (sleep-deprived, stressed, aging) that's both defensible and exactly what ChatGPT's .gov-leaning retrieval will trust.
4. **"Why creatine monohydrate beats the fancy forms."** A single-ingredient brand's natural moat; cites the 500+ studies scoped to the molecule (#3, corrected). Retrieval cue: head-to-head form comparison with citations.

### The named position to own
> **"The creatine brand whose claims are the ones the science actually supports — especially for the women and aging buyers the category keeps over-promising."**

It's a category coordinate, not a tagline: the intersection where being *cited* and being *claim-safe* are the same move. Momentous owns "elite/certified performance." Create can own **"honest, science-bounded creatine for the mainstream — the brand that under-promises and gets cited for it."** No one owns it because no one is fusing the claim-safety discipline with the citation play. That's the empty quadrant, brand-sized.
