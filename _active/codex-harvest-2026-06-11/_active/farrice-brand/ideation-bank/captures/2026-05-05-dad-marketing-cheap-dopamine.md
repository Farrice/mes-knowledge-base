## Distilled Thesis

Being a dad is forcing Farrice to confront the real cost of cheap dopamine, distraction, and outsourced presence. The same lesson applies to marketing: ethical persuasion is not saying the pretty thing that makes the speaker look good. It is saying what the person needs to hear, in the way they can actually receive it, when the outcome is genuinely in their best interest.

## Raw Thought

For my creative strategy, marketing, and AI consulting, being a parent, being a dad, has been very challenging. JJ just had one of the biggest outbursts he's ever had yesterday, and we saw the effects of getting comfortable and relying on cheap dopamine and cheap entertainment at such a young age. It's crazy how numb we are as adults, but seeing JJ and how it affected him really opened my eyes. It's tough, and it's the muscle I need to flex to be creative and be present with my son.

I put the hustle of work, getting clients, and putting money and food on the table ahead of that, and I lost track of it in the moments when I need to work. I need to be present with him, and when he's down for his nap, I grind out hustle and bust my ass. In those times when he's sleeping, I work, but while he's awake, I have to be present with him. I can't use the excuse that I'm sacrificing something so important by just allowing him to use the screens, and if I am using the screens, I better goddamn do what I'm supposed to do while he's using it, because I know what I'm sacrificing now.

What I've noticed with him and with marketing is that everyone wants to say the pretty stuff that makes them look good, sound good, and feel good about themselves. Nobody wants to talk truly the way the audience or the ICP needs to hear you in order to get what you want from them.

I had this hook idea:

> "I just manipulated my two-year-old son, and I don't know how to feel about it."

The whole ethos is that being a parent is like a master class in marketing, persuasion, and influence, and sometimes you're too much in the trenches to notice it and you forget. There are so many times I try to talk to JJ like an adult or like a toddler, or how I think he needs to hear it. If I am present enough with him and know what's going on with him enough, and I'm locked in with him, I can see what picks up and what registers for him. I have to say things exactly the way he needs to hear them and raise the stakes and intention so that I can get the outcome that I want. I only use those tactics when it's truly in his benefit.

For example, he was about to start getting overtired for his nap, and we just recently moved him to his bed from the crib. He's two, and there are a lot of play associations on his bed, so he doesn't knock out as quickly, but he does know he needs to go to sleep. If I allow my son to get overtired, which then messes me up but even messes him up, I have to deploy what I know to get him to help himself.

That is the duty of a good marketer, a good creative strategist, and entrepreneur. There are so many products and people that need certain things, and if you're not willing to do what it takes to get that person there for their benefit, as long as you're doing it with their positive intent and good intent and love in your heart, then you shouldn't be playing this game. That's where some people might say is manipulation. To me, that's empathy and actually giving a fuck.
