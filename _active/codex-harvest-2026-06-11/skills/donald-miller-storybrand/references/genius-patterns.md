# Donald Miller StoryBrand Genius Patterns

## Source Package

Primary expansion source: `extractions/donald-miller/storybrand-message-clarity-system/`

## Patterns

| Pattern | Operating Rule | Apply It By |
|---|---|---|
| Hero inversion | The customer is the hero; the brand is the guide. | Rewrite any message where the brand is the main character. |
| Problem-first sequencing | The problem earns attention before the product appears. | Open with the customer's pain, then introduce guide, plan, product, and outcome. |
| Three-level problem stack | External, internal, and philosophical problems deepen motivation. | Identify all three before writing a BrandScript or one-liner. |
| Product-position discipline | A product has weight only after the customer understands the problem. | Keep product language out of the first move unless the customer context already implies the pain. |
| CTA as decision resolution | CTAs should resolve the customer's internal debate. | Use conditional framing that connects the action to the customer's problem. |
| One-liner compression | The full message must fit into one repeatable sentence. | Build Problem -> Solution -> Result before expanding into copy. |
| Story loop momentum | Each section should create the next question in the buyer's mind. | Move from problem to guide to plan to stakes without dead informational blocks. |
| Clarity over cleverness | Clever language fails when the customer must decode it. | Replace vague phrasing with customer-recognizable words. |
| Soundbite-to-BrandScript bridge | Sound bites are the simple language; BrandScript is the story architecture. | Use locked phrases inside BrandScript elements rather than inventing new language. |
| Repetition creates memory | A message that changes every channel never becomes word of mouth. | Preserve exact core language across website, social, email, sales, and proposals. |

## Quality Checks

- The first line identifies the customer's problem, not the company's identity.
- The customer can repeat the one-liner after hearing it once.
- The product appears as answer, plan, or tool, not as hero.
- The message includes both stakes and success.
- A 12-year-old can understand the final copy without explanation.
