## Executive Summary

- **Core Genius**: Oren distills the chaos of "what should our brand do on social?" into **5 strategic archetypes** selected through a resource-reality audit — not aspiration, not trend-chasing, but what you can actually execute with the people and assets you have.
- **What Makes This Different**: The archetype is NOT a content pillar system. It's a **strategic commitment** that constrains all downstream decisions. One archetype per brand. The constraint is the strategy.
- **Deployable Skills**: Archetype diagnosis for any brand, resource-reality audits, content roadmap generation from archetype selection, archetype-to-exemplar matching for client presentations.
- **Hidden Knowledge Captured**: The resource audit as the real decision engine (not the archetype descriptions); the two-account method; the "nothing is too boring" conviction as a diagnostic unlock; omnipresence mechanics for product-embedded content.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Resource-Reality Gate

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Before discussing ANY content strategy, Oren forces a resource inventory — who can be on camera, who can design, what budget exists, what can actually be showcased. The archetype is selected FROM this audit, not the other way around.

**Executable Behavior**: Run a 4-question resource audit before recommending any content strategy:
1. Who can be on camera? (Expert/founder vs. hired creator vs. nobody)
2. Who can produce? (Internal designer, agency, budget for freelancer?)
3. What's showcasable? (Physical product, process, space, knowledge?)
4. What resources can you GET that you don't have?

**Deployment Context**: First meeting with any brand client, social media audit kickoff, content strategy proposals.

**Success Metric**: The recommended archetype is constrained by actual resources, not aspiration. If the client has no expert to put on camera, Oracle is eliminated before it's even discussed.

---

### Pattern 2: The Single-Archetype Discipline

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Oren never recommends multiple archetypes simultaneously. "Stop trying to be everything to everybody and lock in on one archetype." This is the opposite of content pillar thinking, which distributes attention across categories.

**Executable Behavior**: After running the resource audit, select ONE archetype. All content ideation, format decisions, and production planning flow from that single selection. If the brand is doing "a little Oracle, a little Helper, some Performer skits," diagnose this as strategic incoherence.

**Deployment Context**: Brands with unfocused social presence, content teams producing inconsistent work, strategy audits.

**Success Metric**: The brand's social feed is recognizable within 3 posts — every piece clearly belongs to the same archetype.

---

### Pattern 3: The Exercise-as-Deliverable Model

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Oren doesn't present strategy as a document. He presents it as a facilitated exercise — "a series of questions, filling out a document, and making a plan." This creates buy-in because the client participates in building the answer.

**Executable Behavior**: Structure any content strategy engagement as a guided workshop, not a presentation:
1. Present archetype options
2. Run the resource audit together
3. Cross-reference resources against archetypes
4. Generate content ideas from the selected archetype
5. End with a content roadmap the client helped build

**Deployment Context**: Client kickoffs, team alignment sessions, leadership buy-in meetings (critical for "younger employees trying to convince leadership").

**Success Metric**: The client leaves the session with a plan they feel ownership over — not a document they received.

---

### Pattern 4: The Omnipresence Principle (Performer Archetype)

**What He Does Unconsciously**: For entertainment-driven content, Oren identifies a specific mechanic: the product doesn't need to be mentioned. It needs to be VISIBLE. "The clothes are always on" — Duer Shoots never talks about the clothes, but the clothes are in every frame. This is the opposite of CTA-driven content.

**Executable Behavior**: For Performer-archetype brands, audit every piece of content for product omnipresence WITHOUT product promotion. The product is scenery, not subject. Mohawk Chevrolet's Office skits take place at the dealership. LC Sign's skits feature signs. The product is architecturally embedded, never pitched.

**Deployment Context**: Brands with visual products, physical spaces, or wearable goods who want entertainment-driven social.

**Success Metric**: A viewer watches 10 posts and knows what the brand sells without ever being explicitly told.

---

### Pattern 5: The "Nothing Is Too Boring" Conviction

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Oren repeatedly attacks the belief that certain industries are inherently boring. "I don't think anything is too boring." He gives the industrial parts manufacturer as the signature proof — "there's a story behind every screw, every bolt, every welding piece." This is a diagnostic intervention disguised as encouragement.

**Executable Behavior**: When a client says "our industry is boring," treat this as a DIAGNOSTIC signal, not a legitimate objection. Deploy the Interest Excavation Protocol:
1. What does an insider find fascinating that outsiders don't know?
2. What arguments happen between experts in this space?
3. What's the history behind something everyone takes for granted?
4. What process would blow someone's mind if they saw it?

**Deployment Context**: B2B, manufacturing, professional services, SaaS, or any "boring" industry brand.

**Success Metric**: The client identifies 10+ content ideas they'd previously dismissed as uninteresting.

---

### Pattern 6: The Two-Account Method

**What He Does Unconsciously**: For Oracle-archetype brands where the founder has personal expertise, Oren architects a TWO-account system — the brand's standard social AND the founder's personal account. "In this two-account method, you're able to post your typical content on main... but then his social media is the real driver."

**Executable Behavior**: When the brand has a charismatic, knowledgeable founder:
- Brand account → curated, polished, product-focused content
- Founder account → top-of-funnel expert content, trends, opinions
- Cross-pollination → founder drives awareness, brand captures intent
- Don't force all expertise onto the brand account

**Deployment Context**: Brands with strong founder personalities, expert-led companies, professional services.

**Success Metric**: Founder account drives 3-5x the reach of the brand account; brand account has higher conversion metrics.

---

### Pattern 7: The Expert-Adjacency Funnel

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Oren maps a specific content funnel for Oracle brands — historical/educational content at top-of-funnel (wide reach), development details in the mid-funnel, and product releases at the bottom. Ken Sakata is the canonical example: talks about salvage denim history → shows his development process → releases Front Office products.

**Executable Behavior**: For Oracle brands, design a 3-layer content architecture:
1. **Top of funnel**: Wide-interest educational content (history, trends, why-things-are-the-way-they-are)
2. **Mid-funnel**: Process and development content (what you're working on, how you think about it)
3. **Bottom funnel**: Product releases, launches, and carousels

**Deployment Context**: Any brand where the product requires trust or expertise justification before purchase.

**Success Metric**: Product release posts get meaningfully higher engagement than cold product posts because the audience has been educated into wanting it.

---

### Pattern 8: The Aspiration vs. Practical Split (Catalyst vs. Helper)

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Oren draws a razor-sharp line between two similar archetypes. Catalyst content is ASPIRATIONAL — "it's shared because it's motivational." Helper content is PRACTICAL — "it's just realistic." This distinction drives completely different content formats, sharing mechanics, and audience relationships.

**Executable Behavior**: When diagnosing a brand's educational approach, ask: "Is our customer sharing this because it makes them FEEL like they're becoming better? Or because it SOLVES a specific problem?" The answer determines archetype:
- Aspiration → Catalyst (inspirational overlays, challenges, community curation)
- Problem-solving → Helper (tutorials, comparisons, "don't buy X, get Y instead")

**Deployment Context**: Brands that default to "educational content" without specifying which emotional register.

**Success Metric**: Content engagement patterns match the intended sharing mechanic — Catalyst content gets saves and shares; Helper content gets comments and DMs.

---

### Pattern 9: The Show Question

**What He Does Unconsciously**: For Performer-archetype selection, Oren asks a single qualifying question that eliminates false positives: "If our brand had a show, what would it be? What would it be about? Would people watch it without knowing it was ours?"

**Executable Behavior**: Use this question as the Performer archetype gate. If the brand can't answer it compellingly, they're not a Performer — they're forcing entertainment content without a narrative engine. Redirect to Oracle or Catalyst.

**Deployment Context**: Brands considering skit-based, entertainment-driven, or narrative content strategies.

**Success Metric**: The "show" concept is describable in one sentence and would genuinely be watched by non-customers.

---

### Pattern 10: The Niche Audience Math

**What He Does Unconsciously**: Oren reframes small audience size as a BUYING signal, not a failure metric. On the industrial parts manufacturer: "maybe there's not 100,000 people a video to watch it, but say there's 2,000. If they're nerding out about welded parts, you're probably adjacent to an industry that's going to be procuring them."

**Executable Behavior**: For B2B or niche brands, calculate audience VALUE instead of audience SIZE:
- 2,000 engaged viewers in welding × average contract value = massive ROI
- Compare this to 100,000 unqualified views with zero purchase intent
- Use this math to justify Oracle or Helper strategies to leadership

**Deployment Context**: B2B brands, high-ticket services, niche manufacturers, professional services.

**Success Metric**: Leadership evaluates content ROI by qualified attention, not vanity reach.

---

### Pattern 11: The Content Roadmap Exit Condition

**What He Does Unconsciously**: The archetype exercise doesn't end with "pick an archetype." It ends with a specific deliverable: "you've picked an archetype, you've listed the resources... and you actually have a list of ideas people are excited about." The exercise produces a ROADMAP, not a strategy document.

**Executable Behavior**: Every archetype selection session must end with:
1. Selected archetype (one)
2. Resource inventory (what you have, don't have, and can get)
3. Content idea brainstorm list (minimum 15-20 ideas from the archetype)
4. Buy-in confirmation (people are "excited about" the ideas, not just agreeing)

**Deployment Context**: Every content strategy engagement.

**Success Metric**: The team can start producing content within 48 hours of the session, not weeks of "strategy refinement."

---

### Pattern 12: The Organic-to-Paid Bridge (Helper Archetype)

**What He Does Unconsciously**: For Helper-archetype brands (like Marpipe), Oren identifies a specific mechanism: informational organic content makes paid ads perform better because the brand has ambient recognition. "Just being that entry point, helping reduce the money or the barrier to entry it requires when you're advertising is a massive value add."

**Executable Behavior**: For brands with complex or technical products, measure organic content impact by paid ad performance lift, not organic metrics:
- Organic helper content → ambient brand recognition → paid ad click-through improves → CAC decreases
- This is the ROI justification for "unglamorous" helper content

**Deployment Context**: SaaS, technical products, any brand where the paid funnel is the primary revenue engine.

**Success Metric**: Paid ad CPAs decrease as organic content velocity increases.

## Hidden Knowledge

### 1. The Exercise Format as Professional Credibility Tool
Oren explicitly frames the archetype exercise as something that "makes you look more professional" and "helps establish buy-in." This reveals his understanding that content strategy is a SELLING problem, not just a strategy problem. The format of the engagement (facilitated exercise vs. presented deck) determines whether the strategy gets executed.

### 2. The Leadership Communication Hack
He identifies the specific persona who needs this most: "a younger employee faced with the challenge of how do you convince leadership to act on these ideas." His solution: the archetype framework is borrowed from "overall brand strategy" that "CMOs and executives are familiar with." He's translating social media tactics into boardroom language.

### 3. The "Make Your Own" Flexibility Signal
"There's definitely more. You can make up your own. You can cut it down to just the two or three that are relevant." The five archetypes are a STARTING framework, not a dogma. This reveals Oren's meta-methodology: give structure to the unstructured, but don't over-prescribe.

### 4. The Risk Hierarchy Across Archetypes
Oren sequences archetypes from safe to risky: Oracle (safest — just share expertise), Helper (low risk — just be useful), Catalyst (medium — requires aspirational framing), Performer (higher — requires entertainment skill), World Builder (highest — "this is best for teams and founders willing to take a risk"). He calls this out explicitly: "Is this an easy strategy? No."

### 5. The "Bad Product, Great Content" Signal
The Cluey example is deliberate: "their product objectively is bad. No one knows what Cluey does. Two people are buying from Cluey, but their social media is amazing." He uses this as proof that content strategy and product quality are SEPARATE variables — and as provocation: "if someone was taking the same strategy but applying it to a business that actually has some legs, could do a lot."

### 6. The iPhone Production Floor
Multiple exemplar brands (Cheese Store, Chunky Fit, Kiyoko Beauty) produce content on iPhone only. This implicitly reinforces his anti-overproduction mandate from the content-team-architecture domain — but here it's even more radical. Not just "don't use a RED camera" but "you literally only need a phone."

### 7. The Community Content Flywheel
Artifaxing is cited as the example of what a Catalyst brand becomes at scale — "a community-based content, right? They are curating stuff their community sends them." This is the end state: the brand's audience PRODUCES the brand's content. The archetype selection must account for this future state.

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Exemplar 1: Ken Sakata / Front Office (The Oracle Funnel)

**Context**: Oracle-archetype brand where a single expert drives the entire content ecosystem.

**The Example**: Ken Sakata creates historical explainer videos about workwear — the background of a pilot's jacket, why salvage denim matters, the design history of work pants. These wide-reach educational videos build his expertise perception. Then, weeks or months later, he releases his own version of that garment under Front Office. "When he then releases it a couple months later, you're like, 'Yeah, this guy's really studied the art.'" The funnel: historical videos (top) → development details (mid) → product carousels (bottom, e.g., "fingerprint pants").

**What makes this excellent**: This is the complete Oracle content funnel in one brand. Education → credibility → product release, where each layer justifies the next. The product launch content performs because the audience has been pre-educated into trusting the maker's judgment.

### Exemplar 2: Death to Stock (The World Builder at Full Expression)

**Context**: World Builder archetype for a stock photography platform — a commodity product in a commoditized market.

**The Example**: Death to Stock doesn't make content about stock photography. They make immersive intellectual content about "what is happening in the world around them" — packaging trends, cultural aesthetics, the cyber deck movement. "They are talking about all the things that are trending in the visual world. And then when it ties to the brand is, yeah, we do stock photos that are also on trend." The result: "People build a real affinity with the brand. They immediately go, 'Oh, we get it. These people understand us.'"

**What makes this excellent**: This is World Builder taken to its logical conclusion — the content IS the brand's value proposition in action. They don't explain what they sell; they demonstrate the sensibility that produces what they sell. By far the hardest archetype to execute and the highest differentiation when it works.

### Exemplar 3: Crease Group (The Catalyst for "Boring" Industries)

**Context**: Manufacturing company — the archetypal "boring business" — executing Catalyst-archetype content.

**The Example**: Crease Group makes content about manufacturing processes: what makes a good brief, how laser manufacturing works, sustainable fabrics, label choices. They attend trade shows and share highlights for their audience that can't afford to go. "They're basically helping be that catalyst from where their audience wants to be to where they are."

**What makes this excellent**: This shatters the "our industry is boring" objection. It's also the clearest example of the Catalyst's core mechanic — the brand is the BRIDGE between the customer's current state and their aspirational state.

### Anti-Exemplar: The Everything-to-Everyone Brand

**What mediocre looks like**: A brand that posts educational explainers on Monday, entertainment skits on Wednesday, motivational quotes on Friday, and product photos on Saturday. No clear archetype, no consistent role in the customer's world. "Stop trying to be everything to everybody."

**Why it fails**: Without a single archetype commitment, the brand has no recognizable identity on social. The algorithm can't categorize it, the audience can't predict what they'll get, and the content team is stretched across incompatible formats. This is the exact problem the archetype exercise solves.

## Signature Moves

- **The Resource Audit Before Strategy**: Always maps what exists (people, skills, budget, access) BEFORE recommending what to do. Never starts with the "cool idea" → always starts with "what can you actually execute?" → **Deploy when**: First strategy session with any brand.

- **The Boring Industry Reframe**: When a client says their industry is boring, Oren doesn't argue — he goes DEEPER into specifics until the client discovers their own fascination. "There's a story behind every screw." → **Deploy when**: Client dismisses their content potential.

- **The Show Litmus Test**: "If your brand had a show, what would it be? Would people watch it without knowing it was yours?" Used as a kill switch for forced entertainment content. → **Deploy when**: Brand wants to do "fun" content without a narrative engine.

- **The Exemplar Flood**: Oren provides 3-5 real brand examples for EVERY archetype, across different verticals. He doesn't describe theory — he shows proof. → **Deploy when**: Presenting any archetype to a client or team.

- **The "Same Budget, Different Direction" Argument**: "If you keep trudging along in the same stuff and spending money on it, it goes nowhere. Use that same budget to do something interesting." → **Deploy when**: Budget objections to creative risk.

## Methodology

### Level 1: The Archetype Selection Process

1. **Present the 5 archetypes** with one-sentence descriptions
2. **Run the 4-question resource audit** (camera talent, design capability, showcasable assets, acquirable resources)
3. **Cross-reference**: Eliminate archetypes that don't match available resources
4. **Select one archetype** — commit to it fully
5. **Generate 15-20 content ideas** from that archetype's content types
6. **Validate with the Show Question** (Performer) or the Expert Question (Oracle) or the Aspiration/Practical split (Catalyst vs. Helper)

### Level 2: The 5 Archetypes — Decision Architecture

| Archetype | Best For | Requires | Content Types | Qualifying Question |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Oracle** | Brands with deep expertise to share | A core expert or expert team | Historical explainers, category comparisons, "why things are the way they are," predictions | "What does our customer need to understand about our world that nobody else is explaining properly?" |
| **Performer** | Brands where entertainment drives affinity | Creative talent willing to take risks; visual product | Skits, character series, entertainment-driven narratives with omnipresent product | "If our brand had a show, what would it be? Would people watch it without knowing it was ours?" |
| **World Builder** | Brands willing to take creative moonshots | Budget, creative director or creative team, risk tolerance | Short films, fictional character series, immersive intellectual content, curated cultural commentary | "Can we make something creative that people will love so much they love the brand right next to it?" |
| **Catalyst** | Brands helping people become better | Educational capacity (doesn't require deep expert) | Training documentation, inspirational content, challenges, community curation | "How can we be the bridge between who our customer is and who they want to be?" |
| **Helper** | Brands offering practical value | Relatable creator (not deep expert — more "on the journey with you") | Visual tutorials, carousel tips, story vlogs, interviews, curated comparisons | "What are little helpful things we can do that our customer will appreciate?" |

### Level 3: Archetype-to-Funnel Translation

Each archetype produces different funnel mechanics:

- **Oracle**: Education → trust → product credibility → purchase (Ken Sakata model)
- **Performer**: Entertainment → brand affinity → omnipresent product recognition → purchase
- **World Builder**: Cultural relevance → "they get us" brand affinity → organic brand love → purchase
- **Catalyst**: Aspiration → community belonging → brand as enabler → purchase
- **Helper**: Practical value → ambient brand recognition → paid ad performance lift → purchase
