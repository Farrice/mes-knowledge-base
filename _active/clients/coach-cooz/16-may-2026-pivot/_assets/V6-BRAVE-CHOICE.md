# §2 — THE 3 QUESTIONS

A note before we get into them. The questions are deliberately short. They aren't journaling prompts. They aren't designed to be sat with for 45 minutes with a candle and a notebook. They're designed to land in the 30 seconds you have between the alarm going off and your foot touching the floor. Each one points at a different layer of the same problem — the future you, the comfort you've been protecting, and the version of you in the room right now.

You can run them in any order. Most of my long-arc clients eventually run all three on themselves on the same day without realizing it. That's the gear shift I mentioned earlier. The questions stop being a tool you have to remember and become the way you process a decision.

Here they are, one at a time.

## Question 1

> ### *Will the version of me 5 years from now be glad I did this, or sad I didn't?*

This question pulls the decision out of right now and lands it 5 years downfield. Right now is loud. Right now is the meeting that ran long and the kid who needs help with homework and the third drink that's already poured. Five-years-from-now is quiet. Five-years-from-now is the version of you who already lives with the answer you're about to give.

The question is most useful when the comfortable choice is screaming at you in the present and you can't hear yourself think. You ask it, you wait, and the future version usually answers in one sentence.

**The scenario**: Mike — district manager 33 years at his company, 9-year client of mine, fired in January. Two days after he got the news, he was back in the gym. He didn't show up because he was disciplined that morning. He showed up because year-9-Mike already knew what year-1-Mike learned: the version of him 5 years from now would be glad he kept the practice when the rest of his life was getting torn down.

**The comfortable answer that doesn't move you**: *"I'll get back to it next Monday."*
**The answer the methodology surfaces**: *"Five-years-from-now me is sitting in the same chair, having the same conversation, with a body that's worse and a job that's still gone."*

The 47th Monday you didn't start is the data. Question 1 is what stops the 48th from looking like the 47th.

## Question 2

> ### *Is the comfortable choice the one that protects my future, or the one that protects my excuses?*

This question is a scalpel. It separates two things that look identical from the inside: protecting yourself, and protecting the story you tell about yourself. The comfortable choice almost always wears the costume of self-care. *I'm tired. I deserve a break. I'll start fresh.* When you ask the question, the costume usually slips.

It's most useful at the moment of decision — the 6:14 AM Tuesday moment. Alarm goes off. You have a choice. The body is tired. The schedule is full. The session is on the calendar. You ask, you answer honestly, and the costume comes off.

**The scenario**: Carron — #2-ranked national powerlifter in his weight class, holds the CA State Squat record. A few years ago, between meets, the comfortable choice was always to skip the nutrition work because *he was already strong enough.* The brave choice was to keep doing the boring protocol when nothing about it felt urgent. He chose the protocol. He's the strongest version of himself because he stopped letting comfort masquerade as recovery.

**The comfortable answer that doesn't move you**: *"I'll listen to my body."*
**The answer the methodology surfaces**: *"My body is fine. My excuse is the thing that needed listening to."*

I want to be honest about this one. *Listening to your body* is real and it matters — there are days when the brave choice is the rest day. The question isn't asking you to ignore signal. It's asking you to name the difference between a signal and a story. If you can't tell the difference, that's the work. That's what a coach is for.

## Question 3

> ### *Who am I being right now — the man I built the life for, or the man holding it all up?*

This is the identity question. The first two are about the choice in front of you. This one is about who's making the choice. There's a version of you the people in your life were promised when you started building. And there's a version of you that's been holding the whole thing up while the original version got quietly buried under the work.

This question is most useful when you can feel both versions in the same room. Usually that's late at night, or in the car after a meeting, or in front of the bathroom mirror for the 4 seconds before you look away.

**The scenario**: Corey — Hollywood writer, 4-year client of mine, "The Night Agent" and "Power Book II: Ghost." He travels for shows. He misses months at a time. Every time he comes back he trains 4–5 days a week and pushes intensity in every set. I asked him once why he doesn't ease back in. He said something like *the guy I want to be when this show wraps isn't the guy who eased back in.* That's the question, answered in his own words, in the gym, on a random Tuesday.

**The comfortable answer that doesn't move you**: *"I'm just trying to keep everything running."*
**The answer the methodology surfaces**: *"I'm being the man holding it all up. The man I built this for hasn't been in the room for a while."*

# §3 — HOW I DEPLOY BRAVE CHOICE ON A TRIAGE AUDIT

When somebody books a Triage Audit with me, the call is 90 minutes split into three 30-minute phases. Listen. Diagnose. Recommend one pathway.

The 3 questions show up in Phase 2.

In Phase 1 I shut up. I let the prospect talk for 30 minutes about what's actually breaking — body, schedule, life. I don't diagnose. I don't interrupt. I take notes on their verbatim language because that's the language we're going to build the work in.

Phase 2 is where the questions land. I read back what I heard in their words, not mine. Then somewhere in that 30 minutes — usually around minute 45 — I ask them one of the 3 questions. Not all three. The one their pattern is asking for. If they've been postponing for 3 years, it's Question 1. If they keep telling me they're "just being smart" about why they're not training, it's Question 2. If they keep describing a version of themselves their family used to know that doesn't show up anymore, it's Question 3.

Then I shut up again. The question does the work. Most of the time their answer to themselves is the diagnosis I was going to give them anyway — they just got there first. That's not a sales technique. That's the methodology. Brave Choice surfaces what they already know but haven't said out loud.

By the time we get to Phase 3 — the recommendation — they're not buying a program. They're choosing a pathway that confirms what the question already surfaced. The full call architecture lives in V6-TRIAGE-AUDIT-SCRIPT.md.

One thing worth saying directly: the questions aren't a sales technique. I'm not trying to manipulate someone into buying. The reason they work in a Triage Audit is the same reason they work in a kitchen at 11 PM — they surface the answer the person already knows but hasn't said out loud. If the answer they surface is *I'm not ready,* the right move is for them to walk away from the call, not to book a pathway. I've had Triage Audits end exactly that way and I think they were the right outcome. The methodology has to be honest before it's useful. If I bend it toward conversion, it stops being Brave Choice and starts being a sales script.

# §4 — HOW TO SELF-ADMINISTER THE QUESTIONS

You don't need me on a call to run this on yourself. The whole point of the methodology is that it lives in your pocket and you deploy it in the moment.

**Brave Choices aren't loud.** They're not the dramatic resolution at the end of a movie. They're the 6:14 AM Tuesday choices nobody is watching. Get up or stay in bed. Cook the meal or order. Train or don't. Tell the truth in the conversation or wait until next time. The methodology runs at *that* register, not at the New Year's resolution register.

Here's the protocol:

1. **At the moment of decision** — alarm goes off, end of the workday, between meetings, in the car before you walk in the door — pause for 30 seconds. Don't reach for the phone. The phone is the comfortable choice masquerading as input. The 30 seconds is the doorway.
2. **Pick the question your pattern is asking for.** If you've been postponing, run Question 1. If you keep dressing up comfort as self-care, run Question 2. If the version of you in the room isn't the version your people were promised, run Question 3. If you're not sure which one — run Question 1 first. It pulls the others up behind it.
3. **Answer it honestly.** Out loud if you're alone. In a note on your phone if you're not. The honest answer is usually the one you've been refusing to say. If the answer makes you wince a little, that's signal — the question landed. If it makes you feel righteous, run it again. You probably gave the comfortable answer in a costume.
4. **Make the choice the answer points to.** Not the choice your mood points to. Then move on. Don't sit with it. Don't make it a meditation. The methodology runs at the speed of life, not the speed of journaling.

Do this for 30 days and the questions stop being a tool you have to remember and start being the way you make decisions. That's the gear shift.

A note on what the methodology *isn't.* It isn't a willpower amplifier — discipline isn't your problem. It isn't a positive-thinking exercise — the question doesn't care how you feel, only what you choose. And it isn't permanent. You don't graduate from running the 3 questions. Robin still runs them. Mike still runs them. I still run them. The work isn't ever finished — it just changes register at the next layer.

# §4.5 — A SHORT NOTE ON HOW THIS LANDS WHEN IT'S WORKING

When the methodology starts running cleanly, two things happen that you can use as a check.

The first: the gap between the comfortable answer and the brave answer gets smaller. Not because the choice gets easier — the body still wants the bed, the mind still wants the takeout. The gap closes because you stop spending energy negotiating with yourself. The question makes the negotiation visible, and you stop having it.

The second: the questions start showing up unprompted. You'll be in a meeting and feel one of them surface. You'll be in the kitchen at 11 PM and Question 2 will land before you've reached for the cabinet. That's the methodology going from a conscious tool to a quiet pattern in the way you move. Robin describes it as "aging in reverse" — that's his language for it. He says he has more mental acuity, more energy, more confidence at 8 years in than he did when we started. I don't think it's the workouts. I think it's that he's been running these questions for so long that the man holding it all up doesn't get a vote anymore. Only the man he built the life for does.

That's compounding. That's why the work holds.

# §7 — FOUR MISREADINGS I WANT TO HEAD OFF

People hear *Brave Choice* and read it through a frame they already have. I want to name the four most common misreads, because if you're going to use this on yourself, the frame matters as much as the questions do.

**Misread 1: "This is a discipline framework."**
It isn't. Discipline is the muscle that makes the choice once it's identified. The methodology is what surfaces the choice in the first place. You can have all the discipline in the world and still spend 7 years protecting your excuses, because the question never got asked. Discipline executes. The question reveals.

**Misread 2: "This is positive thinking."**
It isn't. Positive thinking is what people reach for when they don't want to face the answer. The 3 questions don't care about your mood, your motivation, or your mindset. They care about what you choose. The brave answer is often the one that feels worse in the moment.

**Misread 3: "This works because I'll feel inspired."**
You won't. Most of the time the brave choice feels like nothing. It feels like getting up when the bed is warmer, eating the planned meal when the takeout would have been faster, telling the truth in a conversation when the deflection would have been smoother. There's no inspiration. There's just the moment, the question, and the answer. That's why it compounds — because it doesn't depend on how you feel.

**Misread 4: "I can run this without a coach."**
You can run the questions. You probably should — that's what this lead magnet is for. What a coach gives you that you can't give yourself is the diagnostic — the ability to name which question your specific pattern is asking for, and the practice partner who keeps the work honest when your pattern wants to dress comfort up as wisdom. Robin's been running these questions for 8 years. He still works with me. There's a reason for that.
