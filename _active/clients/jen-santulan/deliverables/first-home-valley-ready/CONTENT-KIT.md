## Reel 2: “The honest rent-versus-buy math”

**Cover:** I’m an agent, and renting is cheaper right now.

**Format:** 35 seconds, talking to camera or a notes-screen recording with voiceover.

**Spoken script**

Every agent tells you buying beats renting. I am not going to say that, because you have probably already run the numbers and know it is not true right now.

A two-bedroom in the Valley averages about $3,164 a month. An entry condo runs about $4,000 all-in with 20% down, and closer to $5,000 on a low-down-payment path. That means buying can cost roughly $900 to $1,900 more each month, depending on the down payment.

That premium changes what the payment does. Rent can rise at renewal. A fixed-rate mortgage holds principal and interest steady while principal repayment can build equity.

Rent is cheaper in this example today. The decision turns on whether the ownership path fits your life and your five-year plan.

**On-screen overlays**

- Renting is cheaper right now. Yes, really.
- 2BR rent: about $3,164/month
- Entry condo: about $4,000 or $5,000, depending on down payment
- Honest difference: about $900–$1,900/month
- What does that premium buy for your plan?

**Caption**

Buying is not automatically cheaper than renting in 2026. The honest Valley comparison is roughly $3,164 for a two-bedroom rental versus about $4,000 all-in with 20% down, or closer to $5,000 with a low down payment.

That difference is real. The next question is what the premium does for you if you plan to stay long enough for stability and equity to matter.

Want the side-by-side for your actual income? Send **MATH** and I will help you run it without pressure.

Figures: SRAR July 2026, Freddie Mac week ending August 28, 2026, and RentHop May 2026. Recheck before use.

## Reel 3: “You do not have to buy alone”

**Cover:** You don’t have to buy this alone. Most people aren’t.

**Format:** 30 seconds, talking to camera.

**Spoken script**

Here is one thing that might change your whole plan: you do not have to buy a home solo or with a spouse.

In a spring 2026 U.S. News survey, 37% of buyers said they were purchasing with someone who was not a spouse. That could be a sibling, friend, parent, or another co-buyer.

An entry Valley condo takes roughly $147,000 of household income to carry using the July benchmark. Two qualified incomes can reach that number faster than one.

For more than a third of buyers in that survey, co-buying was the working plan.

**On-screen overlays**

- You do not have to buy alone
- 37% reported buying with someone other than a spouse
- Sibling · friend · parent · co-buyer
- Two incomes can change the qualification math

**Caption**

One assumption keeps good buyers stuck: “I have to do this by myself.”

A spring 2026 U.S. News survey found that 37% of buyers planned to buy with someone other than a spouse. Co-buying is a real path, but it needs clear financing, ownership, exit, and legal agreements.

If there is someone you would consider buying with, let’s talk through the structure before you shop.

## Reel 4: “The 20% myth”

**Cover:** The number in your head is 20% down. It’s the wrong number.

**Format:** 30 seconds, talking to camera.

**Spoken script**

You have decent income and you are still renting. I think I know one reason why: the number in your head is 20% down.

On a $580,000 condo, 20% is $116,000 in cash. Most first-time buyers are not sitting on that.

About one in five first-time buyers used a gift or loan from family. CalHFA’s MyHome program can provide up to 3.5% on an FHA loan or 3% on a conventional loan for eligible buyers.

Twenty percent is one option among several. Calculate cash-to-close, the monthly payment, reserves, and qualification together.

**On-screen overlays**

- 20% down is not the only path
- $580K × 20% = $116K cash
- About 1 in 5 used family help
- MyHome: up to 3.5% FHA / 3% conventional for eligible buyers

**Caption**

If one number has quietly kept you renting, it may be 20%.

On a $580,000 condo, that is $116,000. But 20% is not a universal requirement. About one in five first-time buyers used a family gift or loan, and eligible buyers may have assistance options such as CalHFA MyHome.

Your workable number combines cash-to-close, payment, qualification, and reserves.

Send **MATH** if you want to run the honest version.

Sources: NAR 2025 and CalHFA program guidance. Program availability and eligibility must be rechecked.

## Reel 5: “Run the numbers before you decide”

**Cover:** Think you’ll rent forever? Run the numbers first.

**Format:** 25–30 seconds, keys/home B-roll with voiceover or talking to camera.

**Spoken script**

Maybe you have already decided you will rent forever. Before you make that a permanent story, run the actual numbers once.

Use your income, current rent, real cash-to-close, eligible programs, comfortable payment range, and expected time in the home. An online maximum cannot answer that for you.

The answer may be “not yet,” which gives you a planning target. It may also show that the door is closer than you thought.

Run the math before you decide.

**On-screen overlays**

- Think you will rent forever?
- Run your real numbers once
- Income · rent · cash · programs · timeline
- “Not yet” is useful. “Closer than I thought” is useful too.

**Caption**

“I’ll probably rent forever” sounds like a decision, but most of the time it is a story built without the full math.

Run the honest version once: income, rent, cash-to-close, eligible programs, payment comfort, and timeline. A clear “not yet” gives you a target. A workable scenario gives you something specific to verify. Both beat guessing.

Send **MATH** when you want to look.
