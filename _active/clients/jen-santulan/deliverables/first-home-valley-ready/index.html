Reel 0130 secondsTalking camera

You did not miss it

“You’re 40. You’ve rented for years. And somewhere along the way, you decided ownership was not for people like you.”

Open record-ready script

You’re 40. You’ve rented for years. And somewhere along the way, you quietly decided ownership was not for people like you. Can I show you something? When this data was first tracked in 1981, the typical first-time buyer was 29. Today, it is 40, a record high. At 40, you are standing where today’s typical first-time buyer stands. The timeline moved. The only thing that did not change was the old story in your head.

Reel 0235 secondsHonest math

Renting is cheaper right now

“I’m an agent, and I’m going to tell you renting is cheaper right now.”

Open record-ready script

Every agent tells you buying beats renting. I am not going to say that, because you have probably already run the numbers and know it is not true right now. A two-bedroom in the Valley averages about $3,164 a month. An entry condo runs about $4,000 all-in with 20% down, and closer to $5,000 on a low-down-payment path. That means buying can cost roughly $900 to $1,900 more each month, depending on the down payment. That premium changes what the payment does. Rent can rise at renewal. A fixed-rate mortgage holds principal and interest steady while principal repayment can build equity. Rent is cheaper in this example today. The decision turns on whether the ownership path fits your life and your five-year plan.

Reel 0330 secondsCo-buying

You do not have to buy alone

“A sibling. A friend. A parent. A co-buyer can change the qualification math.”

Open record-ready script

Here is one thing that might change your whole plan: you do not have to buy a home solo or with a spouse. In a spring 2026 U.S. News survey, 37% of buyers said they were purchasing with someone who was not a spouse. That could be a sibling, friend, parent, or another co-buyer. An entry Valley condo takes roughly $147,000 of household income to carry using the July benchmark. Two qualified incomes can reach that number faster than one. For more than a third of buyers in that survey, co-buying was the working plan.

Reel 0430 secondsDown payment

Twenty percent is not the only path

“On a $580,000 condo, 20% is $116,000. Most first-time buyers are not sitting on that.”

Open record-ready script

You have decent income and you are still renting. I think I know one reason why: the number in your head is 20% down. On a $580,000 condo, 20% is $116,000 in cash. Most first-time buyers are not sitting on that. About one in five first-time buyers used a gift or loan from family. CalHFA’s MyHome program can provide up to 3.5% on an FHA loan or 3% on a conventional loan for eligible buyers. Twenty percent is one option among several. Calculate cash-to-close, the monthly payment, reserves, and qualification together.

Reel 0525 secondsEvergreen close

Run the numbers before you decide

“Maybe you have already decided you will rent forever. Before that becomes permanent, run the actual numbers once.”

Open record-ready script

Maybe you have already decided you will rent forever. Before you make that a permanent story, run the actual numbers once. Use your income, current rent, real cash-to-close, eligible programs, comfortable payment range, and expected time in the home. An online maximum cannot answer that for you. The answer may be “not yet,” which gives you a planning target. It may also show that the door is closer than you thought. Run the math before you decide.
