Valley Native — the rulebook

The look for @_jiing carousels. Seven rules. Follow them and every future set looks like it came from the same person, without any two sets looking like the same post.

Why it's built this way: almost every agent doing "neighborhood guide" content runs white serif italic over a big warm photo. Sixteen of the eighteen covers we pulled did exactly that. So the serif italic here is one word, not the whole identity — the identity is the navy line drawing.

1. The stamp goes in the same spot every single time

Under the masthead rule, left side: the little navy roundel (mountains over a boulevard) plus VAN NUYS · 91401 and FROM THE VALLEY underneath it. Same size, same place, all seven slides, every set forever. It's the one thing a person recognizes at thumbnail size before they read a word.

The zip changes when the set is about a different place (Sherman Oaks · 91403, Reseda · 91335). Nothing else about it changes.

2. Everything that isn't a photo is drawn in one navy line

Buildings, maps, arrows, circles, pages. All of it stroke only — one weight, navy, cream inside. No filled icons, no emoji, no icon fonts, no shadows, no gradients, no rounded corners.

The building set is a small vocabulary you reuse: the flat-roof dingbat, the two-story courtyard with the outside stair, the mid-rise, the gabled bungalow. Four Valley buildings, drawn once, used forever. Tiling them (like slide 6, twenty buildings with three marked) is the house move for showing a share or a count.

3. Slide 2 is always the map

The second slide lays the whole set out: the drawn buildings sitting on a drawn street line, numbered 01–04, with a small label under each. Whatever the four things are that slides 3–6 cover, they get plotted here first. The reader knows what they're about to get, and it's the slide that makes the set feel like a guide rather than a list.

4. Photos are prints, not backgrounds

A photo never goes full-bleed behind type. It sits as a print: a thin navy border, a small margin, and a tiny tilt (one and a half degrees — enough to feel handled, not enough to look messy). Under it, a small caption in the sign typeface: the place and the zip, or the place and the year.

On the two navy slides the border is cream instead of navy. That's the only change.

If type ever has to sit on a photo, it gets a flat navy panel behind it at partial opacity. Never a fade.

5. One annotation per slide, and it has to point at something real

A drawn arrow or a drawn circle. One. It marks the actual subject — the building whose insurance we're talking about, the block in the old aerial. Never decoration in an empty corner. If there's nothing worth pointing at, leave it off.

6. Two navy slides, always slides 4 and 7

Slide 4 is the breath in the middle. Slide 7 is the close. Everything else is cream. That rhythm is what keeps her grid from turning into one long cream column — in a nine-square grid you see the dark beat land twice per set.

The close is always the same shape: the question in big type, the one-line answer, the cream box with the ask in serif italic, sources in small caps, her photo as a print on the right.

7. Rotate the subject per series, never the system

What changes set to set:

  • the neighborhood and zip in the stamp
  • which real places get photographed
  • the four numbered stops on slide 2
  • which of the four drawn buildings leads

What never changes: the masthead, the stamp position, the navy line weight, the two dark slides, the print treatment, the fonts, the colors.

Two things to never write

Never "safe," "family," "great schools," "quiet neighborhood," or anything about who a place is for. Fair housing, no exceptions.

Never a claim about what something will be worth later.
