## Profile 1: The Invite Man — "Who Makes the Room Work"

### 1. The Psychographic Core

#### What He Believes About Relationships, Dating, and Meeting People

He believes that the way most people meet now produces something he doesn't want. He has watched himself on apps becoming efficient, detached, and slightly cruel — not because he is cruel, but because the interface rewards selection over presence. He believes that evaluating someone in a 30-second scroll has changed something in him that he doesn't like. He believes there is a difference between knowing you are attracted to someone and *feeling* it, and that the second thing is harder to reach now. He believes that meeting someone should involve context — shared circumstance, mutual recognition — not just mutual selection.

He does not believe in "chemistry at first sight" as a reliable strategy. He has been to enough first dates where the coffee was good and the conversation was fine and nothing happened. He believes that attraction is something the body knows before the mouth can articulate it, and that most dating infrastructure is designed to keep people in verbal evaluation mode indefinitely.

He believes, specifically, that meeting someone while doing something real is different from meeting someone while doing something staged. He is not romantic about this. It is a practical observation from lived experience.

#### What He Has Tried and Rejected

**Apps (Hinge, Bumble, sometimes The League or Coffee Meets Bagel):** He used them for 1-3 years. He had some relationships from them, some situationships. He got good at the mechanics — the opening lines, the date scheduling, the text cadence. He noticed that getting good at the mechanics made him worse at the actual thing. He deleted them not in a dramatic gesture but in a gradual recognition that the ROI on his time and attention had turned negative. He may have reinstalled once or twice. He is currently not on apps, or he is on them half-heartedly and not checking.

**Bars and nightlife:** He went in his mid-to-late 20s. He found it loud, expensive, and built around performance. He either performed (the approach, the banter, the performance of confidence) and felt hollow afterward, or he didn't perform and stood against a wall drinking. He is not opposed to alcohol but does not trust the version of himself that navigates romantic possibility while drinking in a competitive environment. He stopped going to bars to meet people around age 30, give or take.

**Speed dating and organized singles events:** He may have tried one or two. He found them either too clinical (the timed rotations, the scorecards) or too forced-fun (the host with the microphone, the icebreakers). He is not someone who thrives in structured social performance, and these events felt like school but for something he already felt behind at. He left feeling worse about himself, even when he got matches.

**Ecstatic dance or conscious movement events:** If he tried these, he found the music not to his taste, or the verbal framing around the experience made him cringe, or he noticed that the social dynamics were actually quite similar to a bar (who performs openness best) just with different vocabulary. He did not find the demographic he was looking for. He is skeptical of anything that needs to explain why it is safe.

#### His Specific Relationship With His Own Body

This is the defining variable. The Invite Man does not necessarily trust his body. He may be awkward in it. He may not think of himself as someone who "has rhythm." What distinguishes him is not body confidence but body *willingness* — the capacity to move without knowing what he looks like.

He has some history of physical discipline. He lifts, runs, boxes, does BJJ, plays pickup basketball, yoga, cycles. Not as identity performance but as maintenance — he knows what it is to be in his body doing something difficult. This matters because it means he has experienced the sensation of looking foolish while learning, of being the worst person in the room, and continuing anyway. He has body humility.

He does not use his body to dominate space. He does not puff his chest, spread his legs wide on the train, or turn his physical presence into a claim on territory. He has learned, often through feedback from women he trusts, that his size (whatever it is) can be read as threatening, and he modulates accordingly. This modulation is not weakness. It is social intelligence.

His relationship to dancing specifically: he may not dance. He may have danced only at weddings, and only after enough drinks. The idea of dancing sober, in daylight, in front of people he doesn't know, produces a specific feeling — not quite fear, more like anticipated shame. He is willing to feel this because he has some evidence that the other side of it is worth something. He does not need to be a good dancer. He needs to be a man who moves while uncertain.

#### What He Wants That He Hasn't Been Able to Find

He wants to recognize someone. Not evaluate them — recognize them. He wants the feeling of "oh, you too?" that comes from seeing how someone moves through a room, how they respond to music, whether they laugh at the same moment in a song, whether they are kind to the person handing them water.

He wants a relationship where he doesn't have to perform a version of himself. He has performed — at work, on dates, in social settings — and he is good at it. He wants someone who gets the version he is when he is not trying. He doesn't know exactly how to produce that encounter. He suspects it happens in contexts where performance is not the currency.

He wants to be chosen by someone he respects. This is a specific want — not just "a partner" but the feeling of being seen and selected by someone whose judgment he trusts. He has been on enough dates where he did the selecting and it felt hollow. He wants mutuality.

He wants, practically, to stop feeling like meeting someone is a second job. The administrative labor of dating — the scheduling, the text maintenance, the profile updating — has made something that should feel alive feel like project management. He wants to outsource the filtering to something other than his own critical faculties.

#### His Relationship With Vulnerability

Not the wellness version. The practical version: can he be seen not knowing what he is doing?

He has some evidence that he can. He has started jobs where he didn't know the systems. He has moved to neighborhoods where he didn't know anyone. He has been the new person in a gym, a class, a team. He knows the sensation of competence arriving gradually. He is not someone who needs to be good at something immediately to continue doing it.

He has been told, at some point, by someone who mattered, that he is guarded. He may not agree with this assessment, but he has heard it enough to consider it. He has some access to the part of him that holds back, that evaluates before engaging, that treats social situations as tests to pass. He is not fully free of this. But he knows it is there.

The specific vulnerability this room asks of him — moving his body to music while sober and visible — is one he has not practiced. He may have told no one that he is coming. He may have talked himself out of it twice before buying a ticket. The fact that he shows up anyway is the vulnerability. Not what he does on the floor. The showing up.

### 2. The Social Profile

#### What His Social Circle Looks Like

His close friends are mostly coupled. This is not a coincidence — it is the condition that produces him. He has watched his friends pair off over the last 3-5 years, not dramatically but gradually, the way sediment settles. He is still invited to everything. He is the single person at the dinner party, and he has learned to navigate this without bitterness. He genuinely likes his friends' partners. He is tired of being the only one without a plus-one to plan around.

He has 2-4 single friends, mostly men. They talk about dating with a specific tone — not conquest narrative, more shared resignation. They send each other screenshots. They have debated whether apps are the problem or they are. They do not have answers.

His female friends are mostly coupled too. He may have one or two single female friends with whom there is no romantic possibility (established long ago, or mutual disinterest). These women are critical to his story — they are the ones who might tell him about Resonance, and his willingness to take a recommendation from a woman he trusts is itself a signal.

He is not socially isolated. He is socially situated in a way that makes his singleness visible to himself.

#### How He Spends Saturday Afternoons Normally

He does something physical in the morning — workout, run, class. He runs errands. He meets a friend for coffee or lunch. He might work on a side project, read, go to a museum, walk along the lake, bike the 606, hit the farmers market, cook dinner for the week. He has a life. He is not sitting at home waiting for this.

The Saturday afternoon timing of Resonance is either a slight inconvenience (he rearranges his workout) or a relief (it doesn't take his whole night, he can still have dinner with friends after). He does not need his Saturday to be "filled" by this event. He has enough going on that committing an afternoon is a real choice, not an act of desperation.

#### What Music He Actually Listens To When No One Is Watching

This is a diagnostic question. The Invite Man has music he listens to alone that he doesn't necessarily share. It might be 70s soul, or Nigerian Afrobeat, or Brazilian music, or house music from a specific era, or disco deep cuts, or the music his parents played when he was growing up. He has a relationship with music that is not about what it signals to others. He may not talk about this music much. He may not even know that other people are listening to the same things.

He may not have a "dance music" identity at all. He may think of himself as someone who "listens to everything." What matters is not genre knowledge but emotional availability — does music move him, and is he willing to admit that?

#### His Relationship With Chicago

He has been here 3+ years. He knows his neighborhood well — the coffee shop where they know his order, the running route, the places that closed during COVID and what replaced them. He has opinions about the CTA that are specific enough to be diagnostic (the Red Line at midnight, the Metra schedule, the bike lane situation).

He is not a recent transplant still establishing. He is not a lifer who never considered leaving. He chose Chicago or chose to stay, and he has some relationship to the city that goes deeper than his apartment. He may volunteer somewhere, play in a rec league, serve on a neighborhood board, or simply know his neighbors. He has roots, even if shallow.

He is aware of Chicago's segregation and has some relationship to it — not performative awareness but lived. He knows which neighborhoods are whose, where he belongs and where he doesn't, what it means to move through the city as his specific racial self.

#### How He Heard About Resonance

**Warm referral (primary channel, ~50-60%):** A female friend — likely someone he has known 2+ years, possibly a friend's partner — mentioned it. The way she mentioned it matters. If she said "you should go, there will be single women there," he may have resisted. If she said "I went to something like this and it was actually not terrible," he listened. The referrer's judgment is a proxy vetting mechanism. He trusts her taste, and that trust transfers.

**Content/Instagram (secondary, ~20-30%):** He saw a post or story. The copy was sharp enough to cut through — not aspirational lifestyle photography but something that named a problem he recognized (app fatigue, the difficulty of meeting someone real, the strangeness of evaluating humans like products). He followed the account, watched for a few weeks, decided it wasn't performative. The content did not try to sell him a better version of himself. It described a condition he was living.

**Colleague or loose acquaintance (tertiary, ~10-15%):** Someone at work, someone from his gym, someone he knows through a shared activity mentioned it casually. Not a close friend — the casualness of the mention made it feel discoverable rather than staged.

**He does not come from:** targeted ads, dating app cross-promotion, event listing sites, or "singles events" directories. These channels signal the wrong thing. If he found it through a channel that feels commercial or desperate, he arrives with the wrong frame.

### 3. What He Brings to the Room

#### What His Presence Signals to Other Men

This is the most important function of the Invite Man. He makes other men feel safe by being slightly uncertain.

When a man who is not obviously a dancer walks onto the floor and moves anyway — not well, not performatively, just genuinely — he broadcasts a message to every other man in the room: "This is possible." Men read other men constantly in social spaces. They are assessing threat, competition, and possibility. The Invite Man reduces threat and competition simultaneously by being neither dominant nor defeated. He is simply present, willing, and slightly awkward. This gives other men permission to be slightly awkward too.

The specific sequence matters: he arrives, maybe stands at the edge for a few minutes, maybe watches. Then he moves. Not dramatically. Just starts shifting weight, maybe nodding. The moment he stops evaluating and starts participating, the room shifts slightly. Other men notice. They may not consciously register it, but their bodies relax marginally.

He does not approach women aggressively in the first 30 minutes. He does not stand in a corner with his arms crossed assessing the "talent." He does not form a pod with other men and stay there. He participates in the thing that is happening. This behavior is contagious.

#### What His Presence Signals to Women

Women notice him because he is not performing for them. This is the critical distinction. He is not scanning the room to see who is watching. He is not adjusting his behavior based on female attention. He is doing something — moving to music — that has nothing to do with evaluation or selection. This makes him safe to be near.

Women also notice that he is not drunk. This changes the threat calculus entirely. A sober man in a room of moving people is not the same safety category as a man at a bar. He can read social cues. He can stop. He can be approached and can also not approach. His sobriety is legible in his posture, his gait, his eyes.

The specific moment women register him: when he smiles at something in the music — not at a person, at a song. When he catches someone's eye and looks away without it being a tactic. When he is handed water and says thank you like he means it. These are small signals of someone who is having an experience rather than executing a strategy.

#### What Specific Behavior on the Floor Unlocks Other People

**The first non-dancer who dances:** This man — who clearly did not come to dance, who may have come reluctantly, who is not good at it — starts moving. Not performing. Just moving. This unlocks other non-dancers. They see that the floor does not require competence, only participation.

**The man who dances alone without scanning:** He is in his own experience. Not checking to see if women are watching. Not positioning himself near someone attractive. Just in the music. This gives women permission to be in their own experience too — they don't have to manage being watched.

**The man who makes eye contact without immediately moving toward someone:** He sees someone. They see each other. He smiles or nods. He doesn't close the distance immediately. He lets the moment breathe. This creates the possibility of approach without the pressure of pursuit.

**The man who changes his energy when the music changes:** He notices the DJ. He responds to the shift from disco to Afrobeat to house. This signals that he is listening, that he is responsive, that he is not just executing a plan.

**The man who helps without performing helpfulness:** He picks up a cup someone dropped, moves a bag out of the dance area, hands someone water because he is already getting some. Not as a pickup move. Just as someone who sees the room as shared space.

#### What His Post-Event Description Sounds Like

This is the success signal — what he says the next day, what he texts his friend who asked about it.

> *"It was weird. Good weird. Not what I expected."*

> *"I didn't really talk to anyone for the first hour. Just danced. Then I ended up talking to this woman during the break and it was... easy? Like we didn't have to do the interview."*

> *"It felt like the opposite of a singles event. I don't know how to explain it. No one was trying to meet anyone but everyone kind of... did?"*

> *"The music was incredible. I haven't danced like that since I was a kid."*

> *"I'm going back. And I'm bringing Marcus."*

The language is slightly surprised. He did not expect it to work. He is not trying to convince himself it was good — he is reporting that it was. The music comes up. The feeling of ease comes up. What does not come up: "the women were hot," "I got numbers," "the ratio was good." If he uses those phrases, he is not the Invite Man. He is the Screen-Out Man who happened to get in.

### 4. The Sub-Types

#### Sub-Type A: "The Warm Referral Man"

**Who he is:** Marcus-adjacent. 30-36. Black, Latino, or mixed. Creative-class professional — designer, engineer, creative director, architect, photographer, trainer, teacher. Income $75-130K. Lives in Bronzeville, Hyde Park, Pilsen, Logan Square, or Wicker Park. Has a therapist. Trains BJJ or lifts. Has a small group of coupled friends who worry about him.

**What brings him:** A woman he trusts — his friend's partner, his sister, his female creative colleague — told him about it specifically. She knew he was struggling to meet people. She knew the apps weren't working. She may have had to mention it twice before he followed up.

**What he needs to see in the room:** Men who look like him or are comfortable around men who look like him. Music he recognizes or can move to. A woman at the door (Andrea) who greets him like a person, not a customer. No performative wellness language.

**What he brings:** Depth of presence. He is not there to perform. He is there because he trusts the woman who told him about it, and that trust makes him willing to try. When he decides to participate, he participates fully. His breakthrough moment — the moment he stops watching and starts moving — is one of the most powerful signals in the room.

**What might push him away:** Feeling like the only Black or Latino man. Walking into a room that reads as white and not knowing if he belongs. Music that is too niche or too aggressively "conscious." Any language that sounds like yoga studio copy. Men who evaluate him as competition. The sensation that this is a space for a certain kind of person and he is not that kind.

**How to spot him during vetting:** He applies after being referred by someone Andrea knows. In any brief exchange, he is slightly reserved, not selling himself. He may ask "what should I expect?" not as anxiety but as genuine unknown. He does not overshare about his fitness routine or his professional success. He may mention that a friend told him about it and name the friend.

#### Sub-Type B: "The Self-Selecting Skeptic"

**Who he is:** Daniel-adjacent. 32-40. White or Asian. Professional — engineer, lawyer, consultant, product manager, finance, tech, operations. Income $100-200K+. App-fatigued, event-skeptical. Found Resonance through sharp copy or a colleague's mention. Did not tell anyone he was coming. May have almost talked himself out of it the morning of.

**What brings him:** He read something that named his condition accurately. Not aspirational marketing — diagnostic prose. He recognized himself in the description of someone who is good at everything except meeting someone in a way that feels real. He is skeptical by nature and was surprised to find himself buying a ticket.

**What he needs to see in the room:** Competence without performance. He needs to see other men who look like they have their lives together but are not performing having their lives together. He needs the event to not be embarrassing — not lame, not desperate, not try-hard. He needs to see that the women are interesting, self-possessed, not there because they couldn't do better. He needs the music to be actually good, not playlist-good but *curated* good.

**What he brings:** Analytical rigor turned toward genuine curiosity. When he decides something is worth his time, he commits fully. He is likely to be one of the men who starts moving early, not because he is a dancer but because he has decided that standing at the edge is worse than participating. His participation gives other skeptics permission.

**What might push him away:** Anything that reads as corny, forced, or inauthentic. Icebreakers. A host with a microphone trying to "bring the energy." Music that sounds like a Spotify algorithm. Women who seem like they are performing being open. The sensation that he has paid to be in a room with people who couldn't meet anyone either.

**How to spot him during vetting:** He found Resonance through content, not referral. His application is concise, not oversold. He may express mild skepticism — "this seems interesting, I'm not sure what to expect" — without being hostile. He has specific questions about the format. He does not use words like "vibe" or "energy" or "community." He may mention that he deleted apps recently.

#### Sub-Type C: "The Returning Man Who Brings Someone"

**Who he is:** 34-42. Any racial/ethnic background. Was at a previous Resonance event. Did not necessarily meet his partner there — may still be single — but had an experience that he wants someone else to have. This is the man who texts his friend afterward: "I found it. This is the thing."

**What brings him back:** The first event worked in some way. Maybe he met someone. Maybe he didn't meet anyone but felt something shift. Maybe he just felt normal in a room of single people for the first time. He wants to share that with someone he knows is in the same position he was.

**What he needs to see in the room:** The same quality he experienced the first time. Consistency matters enormously — if the second event feels different (more commercial, more performative, different demographic), he will not come back and he will not refer. He needs to recognize the feeling.

**What he brings:** Social proof of the most powerful kind — not a review, not a post, but a personal invitation to a specific person. He is also, by his presence, a signal to newcomers that the room is safe. He knows the ritual (phones in basket, greet Andrea, the floor is the protocol) and his comfort with the ritual makes newcomers more comfortable.

**What might push him away:** Change in quality, tone, or demographic. Commercialization. A shift toward the performative. If he brings a friend and the friend has a bad time, he will not bring another.

**How to spot him during vetting:** He mentions attending before. He may apply with someone else ("my friend and I are coming"). His language is specific — he references details from the previous event (the music, the light, a moment). He does not need to be sold. He needs to be welcomed back.

#### Sub-Type D: "The Skilled Trades / Non-Creative-Class Man"

**Who he is:** 30-40. White, Latino, or Black. Electrician, carpenter, plumber, mechanic, firefighter, nurse, EMT, small business owner, contractor, chef. Income $60-100K. Not in the creative or tech professional circles that most of the room moves in. May feel slightly out of place in creative-class spaces but is not intimidated by them. Has a physical relationship to work — uses his body professionally, comes home tired, showers before going out.

**What brings him:** He heard about it through a gym connection, a colleague, or his sister. He is less likely to be app-fatigued (he may never have used apps seriously) and more likely to be socially fatigued — his social world has contracted, he works with mostly men, he doesn't meet women organically. He is practical about wanting a partner. He may have been married before or in a long relationship that ended.

**What he needs to see in the room:** Men who work with their hands or have physical presence. He does not need them to be the majority, but he needs to know he is not the only one. He needs the space to not feel pretentious — not too "designed," not too Instagram. He needs to feel that being a plumber or a carpenter is not a social demerit in this room.

**What he brings:** Groundedness. He is less likely to perform introspection because he is not in an introspection-performing social class. His presence adds socioeconomic texture to the room. When he moves, he moves with physical confidence — not dance confidence, but body confidence. This is legible to women. He is likely to be direct in conversation, not verbally agile but present. He may be the man who ends up in a long conversation during the break because he asked one real question.

**What might push him away:** Pretension. Overly aestheticized space. Conversations that feel like networking. The sense that everyone works in tech or design and talks about their "practice." Men who talk down to him or perform intellectual dominance.

**How to spot him during vetting:** He may not use the application language fluently — not because he is less intelligent but because he does not live in a world where people write paragraphs about themselves. His answers may be shorter, more direct. He may say what he does plainly without elaboration. He may have been referred by someone unexpected (his sister, his gym coach, his coworker). His questions are practical — "what should I wear," "is there parking," "how long does it go."

### 5. What He Needs From the Room

#### What He Needs to See in the First 5 Minutes

He needs to see that this is not a singles event. The absence of name tags, the absence of a host with a microphone, the absence of structured mingling — these absences are signals. He needs to see Andrea at the door, warm and specific, not "welcoming" in a hospitality-industry sense but greeting him like a person who is expected. He needs to see the phone basket and feel relief, not resistance — the structure is doing something for him, not to him.

He needs to see other men who look like they are also figuring it out. A room full of confident dancers would make him feel like he made a mistake. A room with some edge-standing, some hesitation, some looking around — that makes it possible for him to be hesitant too.

He needs to see women who look like women he would want to talk to — not in a "hot enough" sense but in a "seems like she has a life" sense. Self-possessed, in their bodies, not performing availability.

He needs the music to be good immediately. Not good in a "this is interesting" sense. Good in a "I know this song" or "I want to move to this" sense. The first track sets the frame. If it is apologetic, tentative, "wellness," or too niche, he will feel confirmed in his skepticism.

#### What He Needs to See in the Women

He needs to see women who are not performing being approachable. This is a specific and critical need. Women who are genuinely having their own experience — dancing, listening, talking with a friend — signal that this is a space where people are real. Women who are performing openness (scanning the room, positioned to be seen, reactive to male attention) make him feel like he is in a marketplace.

He needs to see women around his age, not a decade younger. He needs to see women who look like they have some history — not hardened, not bitter, but not naive. Women who have also tried things that didn't work.

He needs to see at least one woman he finds intriguing before he has talked to her. Not beautiful — intriguing. Something in how she moves, what she wears, how she responds to the music. This gives him a reason to stay that is specific and embodied, not abstract ("maybe I'll meet someone").

#### What He Needs From the Music

He needs the music to do emotional labor he does not know how to do. He needs it to create the conditions where moving feels natural, where being near someone does not require explanation, where a shift from one song to another creates a shared feeling without anyone having to manufacture it.

He needs recognizable moments — a song he knows, or a genre he recognizes, or a rhythm his body understands. These moments anchor him. They say "you belong here" without words.

He needs the music to change. Stasis kills participation. If the set is one genre at one tempo for too long, his mind takes over. Variation — in era, geography, energy, mood — keeps him in his body.

He needs the occasional ballad or slow moment. Not as a forced "now connect with someone" prompt. Just as breathing room. The man who stands still during a slow song, drink in hand, watching others, is processing something. That processing matters.

#### What He Needs From Andrea

He needs Andrea to see him. Not as a demographic, not as a ticket sale. As a person arriving with some uncertainty. Her greeting — specific, warm, not effusive — tells him that he is in a space where people are recognized individually.

He needs her to not try to make him comfortable. That would be suspicious. He needs her to be comfortable herself, to treat the space as normal, to not perform host-energy. Her ease is contagious.

If he is someone who needs a small orientation — "there's water, the floor is open, there's no structure" — he needs her to offer it without him having to ask, but without condescension. The way a friend of a friend would show you around a party, not the way a host manages a guest.

#### What Would Make Him Come Back

He met someone. Even just one conversation that continued past the event. Even just the possibility of one conversation. Even just the feeling that there were multiple people he could have talked to and didn't, which means there is more to discover.

Or: he didn't meet anyone but felt something he hasn't felt in a long time. Alive in his body. Present with strangers. Not performing. This is enough for a return if the feeling was strong enough and the music was good enough.

#### What Would Make Him Bring Someone

The experience being difficult to describe but impossible not to mention. When someone asks "how was your weekend" and he says "I went to this thing... I don't really know how to explain it. It was a dance thing, but not really. It was... good." And the other person says "that sounds weird" and he says "it was, but you should come next time."

That exchange — the inability to categorize it combined with the certainty that someone else should experience it — is the referral engine. He will not bring someone if it was "fine" or "interesting." He will bring someone if it was *notably different from anything else*.

## Profile 2: The Screen-Out Man — "Who Seems Right but Poisons the Room"

### 1. The Types to Screen Out

#### Screen-Out Type 1: "The Performer"

**What he looks like on paper:** Confident, socially adept, attractive. May have a creative job or a job that involves public presence. Describes himself as "loving to dance" or "always the first one on the dance floor." Has been to "similar events" and enjoyed them. References his social life, his travels, his interesting experiences. Seems like exactly the kind of man who would make a room more dynamic.

**What he does in the room:** He performs confidence. He enters with energy that fills the space. He is the first to dance, but his dancing is display — he is watching himself be watched. He positions himself centrally. He approaches women with practiced ease, the ease of someone who has done this many times. He uses the music as soundtrack to his social performance rather than as the thing he is doing.

**What women experience:** Initially, attention. He is handsome, confident, present. Then, gradually, the sensation of being an audience. He asks questions but doesn't listen to answers. He touches the small of the back, guides with a hand on the elbow — small territorial gestures that establish physical claim without consent. He is the man who makes other men feel small by comparison and then uses that smallness as opportunity.

**What other men experience:** Deflation. He takes up too much space. They find themselves standing farther from the center, moving less, feeling evaluated. The Performer does not need to do anything hostile. His presence alone establishes a hierarchy that makes other men's participation feel like failure.

**The red flag during vetting:** He describes himself as "the life of the party" or "always dancing." He asks about the "crowd" or the "scene" before committing. His application photo is professionally shot or obviously curated. He mentions being "good at meeting people" as a skill. He has been to multiple "conscious" or "wellness" events and describes them enthusiastically. He asks about "who else is coming" — not from social anxiety but from strategic planning.

**How he talks about dating/women:** "I love women." "I connect easily with people." "I'm looking for someone who can keep up." He talks about dating as something he is good at, not something he finds difficult. He describes past partners as having been lucky to be with him, or as having failed to appreciate him.

#### Screen-Out Type 2: "The Spiritual Pickup Artist"

**What he looks like on paper:** Introspective, emotionally articulate. Has done "work on himself." Therapy, maybe coaching. Describes wanting "depth" and "authentic connection." Interested in "alternative ways of meeting people." May reference meditation, breathwork, somatic practice, or movement as part of his life. Seems evolved. Seems safe.

**What he does in the room:** He performs emotional availability. He makes eye contact that lasts slightly too long — "soul gazing" as a pickup technique. He interprets shared movement as spiritual connection. He uses the absence of verbal structure as an opportunity to create intense non-verbal intimacy that has no accountability. He is the man who, during a slow song, positions himself near a woman and "breathes with her" without asking if she wants to breathe with him.

**What women experience:** Initially, relief. A man who is emotionally present, who doesn't perform machismo, who seems to understand something about being in a body. Then, claustrophobia. His presence is sticky. He creates intimacy without consent — not physical assault but atmospheric pressure. He makes it hard to disengage without feeling like you are rejecting something sacred. He uses the event's frame (heart encounters, body-first) as a pretext for bypassing normal boundaries.

**What other men experience:** Alienation. The Spiritual Pickup Artist has weaponized the event's language and values. He makes genuine emotional availability suspect. Other men who are actually trying to be open find themselves wondering if they look like him. He poisons the well for authentic vulnerability by performing it.

**The red flag during vetting:** He uses the event's language back at you — "heart encounters," "being in the body" — with a fluency that suggests practice. He references other "somatic" or "conscious" events he has attended. He asks if there will be "guided connection practices" or seems disappointed there aren't. His emotional articulacy is too polished — he has been coached or has coached himself. He talks about "holding space" or "being present" as things he does.

**How he talks about dating/women:** "I'm looking for a conscious partner." "I want depth, not surface-level connection." "I'm drawn to your energy." He uses the language of emotional intimacy as a seduction strategy. He may reference feminine or masculine polarity, embodiment, or "sacred union" concepts.

#### Screen-Out Type 3: "The Efficiency Hunter"

**What he looks like on paper:** Successful, busy, direct. He values his time. He is on apps but finds them inefficient. He likes that this event is "curated" and "vetted" — it signals quality control. He is not interested in wasting time. He may come across as refreshingly honest about what he wants.

**What he does in the room:** He treats it as a target-rich environment with better filters. He scans the room systematically. He may move quickly from interaction to interaction — dance near one woman, assess, move to another. He takes out his phone the moment the event's structure loosens. He asks women what they do, where they live, whether they want kids — the interview questions that apps formalize but he deploys in person. He is not rude. He is efficient. Efficiency in a room built for presence is violence.

**What women experience:** Evaluation. They can feel themselves being processed. He makes eye contact but it is scanning, not seeing. He asks questions but does not respond to the answers in a way that suggests curiosity — only data collection. Women who sense they are being interviewed for a position become guarded, which he may interpret as them being closed off, confirming his bias that dating is a numbers game.

**What other men experience:** Competitive pressure. The Efficiency Hunter creates an implicit frame: this is a marketplace, and the men are products. Other men start to wonder if they should be more strategic, more aggressive, more systematic. The room's non-competitive structure starts to feel naive.

**The red flag during vetting:** He asks about the "selection process" or "criteria" with a tone that suggests he is evaluating whether it is rigorous enough. He references his time as valuable and wants to know "what he is getting." He asks about the female-to-male ratio directly or indirectly. He describes himself as "knowing what he wants" with a certainty that does not include room for surprise. He may try to get information about who else is coming — names, profiles, photos.

**How he talks about dating/women:** "I don't have time for games." "I know what I'm looking for." "I'm looking for someone who has their life together." He talks about relationships as transactions that need to be optimized. He may describe past dating as "wasted time" with people who "didn't know what they wanted."

#### Screen-Out Type 4: "The Guy's Guy"

**What he looks like on paper:** Fun, normal, easy to talk to. Not too intense about any of this. Works in sales, finance, tech, or something similarly mainstream. Likes sports, going out, "having a good time." His buddy told him about this or he saw it somewhere. Seems like a regular dude.

**What he does in the room:** He arrives with a friend or finds another man to stand with. They form a pod. They talk through the music. They evaluate the room in audible whispers. They drink (or wish they could). When they do dance, it is performatively silly — ironic distance as defense. They are at a "thing" and the quote marks are audible. When they approach women, it is in a pack or with the energy of someone approaching from a position of social safety in numbers.

**What women experience:** Being scoped. The Guy's Guy does not create immediate threat but he creates immediate category: this is a man who is here with his boys, which means he is not a man who is having his own experience. Women who are approached by men in pods face a social geometry that is hard to navigate — engaging with one means engaging with the group. Disengaging means disengaging from the group. The cognitive load is higher. The safety is lower.

**What other men experience:** Recruitment pressure. If they are not in the pod, they feel excluded. If they are near the pod, they may be absorbed or evaluated. The Guy's Guy converts individual participation into group observation, which changes the room's social physics.

**The red flag during vetting:** He applies with a friend and emphasizes that they are "coming together." His application is minimal — "sounds fun," "my buddy told me about this." He asks if there will be alcohol. He wants to know "what kind of music" not from genuine musical interest but from social positioning. He seems to think the event is "cool" or "interesting" in a way that suggests he is slumming it.

**How he talks about dating/women:** "Just seeing what's out there." "Looking for someone cool." "No drama." He talks about women as categories — "cool girls," "crazy girls," "wife material." He may reference "the ratio" or ask if there will be "enough women."

#### Screen-Out Type 5: "The Comparison Shopper"

**What he looks like on paper:** Experienced, discerning. Has been to Daybreaker, Morning Gloryville, Ecstatic Dance, various conscious events, singles mixers, maybe Tantra-adjacent workshops. He knows this space. He has opinions about what works and what doesn't. He seems like an asset — he knows the culture, he can help other people feel comfortable.

**What he does in the room:** He compares. Not out loud, but in his posture, his evaluations, his suggestions. He mentions how "at the last event I went to" they did something differently. He positions himself as someone who knows how this is supposed to go. He may try to be helpful to Andrea or the DJ — offering feedback, making suggestions. His help is a way of establishing himself as someone with status in this type of space. He creates a subtle hierarchy of experience that places newcomers below him.

**What women experience:** Being evaluated by someone who thinks he is an expert at meeting women. The Comparison Shopper has been to enough events to have developed patterns — which types of women respond to which approaches, which music creates which openings. His interactions are rehearsed, even if they appear spontaneous. Women feel the script.

**What other men experience:** Displacement. The Comparison Shopper occupies the "experienced regular" role that should belong to returning Invite Men. He makes being new feel like being behind. He may offer unsolicited guidance to other men — "how it works here" — that is actually about establishing dominance.

**The red flag during vetting:** He lists multiple similar events he has attended. He has opinions about format — "I prefer when there's a sharing circle at the end" or "the last event I went to had a better energy." He asks about the "community" or the "regulars." He seems to be interviewing Resonance for fit rather than applying to participate. He may mention people he knows in "the scene."

**How he talks about dating/women:** "I've done a lot of work around relationships." "I'm really intentional about who I spend time with." "I'm looking for someone who is also on a growth path." He uses the language of self-development as social positioning. He talks about women as mirrors or teachers or reflections of his own journey.

#### Screen-Out Type 6: "The Man Who Is Too Good at This"

**What he looks like on paper:** Charming, attractive, successful. Articulate about what he is looking for. Has clearly had success with women — may mention past relationships with a tone that suggests they ended because the women couldn't meet his standards. Seems like exactly the kind of man you would want more of.

**What he does in the room:** He thrives. This is the critical problem. He does not just participate — he excels. Women are drawn to him. Men want to be near him. He creates connection easily, naturally, in a way that seems to exemplify what the event is for. But something is wrong. The connections he creates do not last past the event — not because they didn't work but because he does not want them to. He is not looking for a partner. He is looking for the experience of being chosen, repeatedly. The event becomes a validation engine.

He may leave with someone's number. He may see her again, once or twice. He will not commit. He will not be cruel about it — he will be honest, or honestly confused, or honestly "not feeling it." The woman he leaves behind is more skeptical of the next event, less willing to be open, more guarded.

**What women experience:** Being chosen and then not kept. The particular pain of being selected by someone who seemed to have options and picked you — and then didn't. This is not ghosting from an app. This is a deeper betrayal because the context promised something different. Women who have this experience talk about it. They warn their friends.

**What other men experience:** Invisible damage. The Man Who Is Too Good at This does not directly harm other men. He harms the environment. He depletes the trust that makes the room work. Other men may feel — correctly or not — that the women they are interested in are interested in him. They may not understand why the room feels harder than it should.

**The red flag during vetting:** He is suspiciously fluent in the event's values. He has the right answers too readily. He mentions wanting "something real" with a conviction that seems rehearsed. He has been to multiple "curated" dating events and speaks about them positively. When asked what didn't work about past events, he blames the format or the crowd, never himself. He does not express uncertainty about whether he will find what he is looking for — he expresses certainty that he will, because he is a good fit.

**How he talks about dating/women:** "I want a partner, not a project." "I'm looking for someone who is already complete." "I don't do drama or baggage." He talks about women as finished products — either they have their lives together or they don't. He does not express curiosity about a woman's complexity. He expresses interest in her competence.

### 2. The Subtle Screen-Outs

#### The Man Who Needs to Be the Most Interesting Person in the Room

He is not the Performer — he is subtler. He may be genuinely interesting. Well-traveled, well-read, good at what he does. The problem is his need for this to be recognized. He enters conversations with contributions that shift focus to himself. He tells stories that establish his uniqueness. He is not malicious — he may be unaware. But the effect is that conversations with him become performances of his interestingness.

In a room where the goal is mutual recognition, he makes recognition impossible. He occupies too much verbal space. Women leave conversations with him feeling exhausted rather than seen. Other men feel like they cannot contribute.

**How to spot him during vetting:** His application is a resume of interesting experiences. He may mention where he has traveled, what he has built, who he knows. Not as bragging but as self-description — he genuinely thinks this is who he is. When asked why he wants to come, he describes what he will bring rather than what he hopes to find.

---

#### The Man Who Is "There for the Music"

He makes clear, upon arrival or in application, that he is not there to meet anyone. He is there for the music, the scene, the dancing. This seems fine — the event does not require romantic intent. But his disengagement from the social purpose is not neutral. It is a position-taking. He is establishing that he is above the need, that his participation is purer than others'.

In the room, he dances with his eyes closed or facing away from people. He does not make eye contact. When approached, he is polite but closed — "just here for the music." This creates a rejection dynamic in a space that is trying to avoid exactly that. Women who approach him (or try to make eye contact) feel rebuffed. Men who see him feel like engagement is optional, which makes their own engagement feel vulnerable by comparison.

He is not doing anything wrong. That is what makes him subtle. He is just wrong for this room.

**How to spot him during vetting:** He emphasizes the music over the social aspect. He asks about the DJ, the sound system, the vinyl collection. He mentions being a "music person." He does not mention being single, wanting partnership, or struggling to meet people. His interest in the event is purely as an experience, not as a context.

---

#### The Man Who Has Been to 12 Similar Events

Not the Comparison Shopper — this man is more defeated. He has been to everything. He is not optimistic but he is not willing to give up. He arrives with the weight of all those previous events. He is watching to see if this one will be different, but his watching is itself a defense — he is not participating fully because he has learned not to invest fully.

His presence in the room is a low-grade depressant. He does not poison actively. He just makes the air slightly heavier. Other men sense his fatigue. Women sense his half-presence. He may leave early. He may stay but not fully arrive.

He is not malicious. He may be a good person who has been hurt. But a room of ~50 people cannot absorb much of this energy without feeling it.

**How to spot him during vetting:** He mentions having tried "everything" — apps, events, matchmakers, "you name it." His tone is weary. He asks questions that suggest he is looking for proof that this is different. He may express hope but it sounds like habit rather than belief. His application reads like someone applying for a job they don't expect to get.

---

#### The Man Who Is "Just Checking It Out"

One foot out the door before he walks in. He wants to keep his options open. He may have bought a ticket but he is not committed to attending — "I'll see how I feel that day." If he comes, he stands near the exit. He does not fully put his phone in the basket. He is ready to leave at any moment.

His presence is not poisonous directly. It is just dilute. He takes a spot that could have gone to someone who would participate. He adds to the count without adding to the room. Women sense his unavailability and steer clear. Men sense that someone is standing apart and wonder if they should be too.

**How to spot him during vetting:** Language of non-commitment — "thinking about coming," "sounds interesting, I'll see," "want to check it out." Questions about refund policy. Questions about whether he can leave early. No sense of anticipation or planning. May have been referred but by someone he is not close to.

### 3. The "Almost Right" Man

The Almost Right Man is the most dangerous category because he will pass vetting. He is 80% Invite Man and 20% Screen-Out. The 20% does not make him a bad person. It makes him wrong for this specific room at this specific moment.

#### What He Looks Like

He genuinely wants partnership. He is not the Performer or the Efficiency Hunter. He has done some work on himself. He is socially capable. He likes music. He would probably enjoy the event. He would probably be respectful to women. The problem is not his character. The problem is what he carries with him into the room.

#### The 20% That Breaks the Room

**He is just slightly too practiced.** He has been to enough events, enough dates, enough situations that his social behavior is optimized. He knows how to make eye contact, how to ask the right questions, how to create intimacy at the right pace. It all works. And it all feels slightly like technique rather than impulse. Women may not notice immediately. But they notice. The difference between being seen and being handled is subtle but real.

**He is just slightly too eager.** Not desperate-eager — that would be easy to screen. Competent-eager. He wants this to work. He is trying. His trying is visible. He is having a good time and making sure people know he is having a good time. His effort creates a low-grade pressure — if you are not also having a good time, you are letting the room down.

**He is just slightly too verbal.** He processes experience through narration. He comments on the music, the atmosphere, the concept. Not critically — appreciatively. But his appreciation is constant, verbal, performance-adjacent. He is co-creating the experience by narrating it rather than living it. People near him feel like they are in his story.

**He is just slightly too sure this is a good idea.** His confidence in the concept, in Andrea, in the room, is not grounded in experience. It is grounded in wanting to believe. This certainty makes him slightly evangelical — he wants other people to know how good this is, which creates a subtle social pressure to agree.

#### How to Spot the 20% During Vetting

**Look for the edge of performance in self-description.** The Almost Right Man is self-aware but his self-awareness is itself a performance. He knows the right things to say and says them with a fluency that suggests rehearsal, not reflection. Ask him something unexpected. If his answer still comes out polished, that is the tell.

**Look for absence of uncertainty.** The Invite Man is uncertain and admits it. The Almost Right Man has resolved his uncertainty into enthusiasm. Ask: "What feels strange about this to you?" The Invite Man has an answer. The Almost Right Man says "nothing really, it seems great" or makes uncertainty into a positive ("I'm only uncertain because I wish I'd found this sooner").

**Look for how he talks about past events.** The Almost Right Man has been to things that were "almost right" — and his description of why they were almost right will mirror his own almost-rightness. "It was good, just not quite the right crowd" or "great concept, the execution was a little off." He sees the pattern but not that he is part of it.

**Look for his relationship to women in his description.** Does he describe women he has dated as having been fortunate to know him? Does he describe what he wants in a partner as a list of qualities? Does he use the word "finally" — as in "I finally found something real" or "I'm finally ready"? These are not red flags in a dating profile. They are red flags in this room because they suggest that his participation is about completing a narrative rather than having an experience.

**Look for how he heard about Resonance.** If it was through a channel that required no personal recommendation — he found it, he decided, he applied — that is not disqualifying. But combined with the above signals, it suggests a man who makes decisions based on his own evaluation without the moderating influence of trusted input. The warm referral is not just a distribution channel. It is a vetting mechanism that says: someone who knows this man thinks he belongs here. The absence of that signal, combined with the 20%, is the pattern.

#### Why the 20% Matters

In a room of 50 people, one Almost Right Man is absorbable. Two or three, and the room starts to feel slightly off without anyone knowing why. The women are not quite as open. The men are not quite as relaxed. The music has to work harder. Andrea has to compensate more. The event succeeds on paper — people came, people danced, some people met — but the feeling is not what it could have been. The Almost Right Man does not poison the room. He just makes it 15% less than it should be. Over time, those 15%s accumulate.

### Vetting Principles: Operationalizing These Profiles

#### The Three-Question Diagnostic

During any vetting exchange (application review, brief message, or arrival conversation), these three questions cut to the distinction:

1. **"What made you decide to apply?"** — The Invite Man names a specific feeling, a trusted person's recommendation, or a moment of recognition. The Screen-Out Man names a strategic advantage ("curated crowd," "better than apps," "I like the concept").

2. **"What feels uncertain about this for you?"** — The Invite Man has a real answer. The Screen-Out Man has none, or his uncertainty is performative.

3. **"What would make it worth your afternoon?"** — The Invite Man names a feeling — ease, surprise, recognition, not feeling like he is performing. The Screen-Out Man names an outcome — meeting someone, a good time, "seeing what it's about."

#### The Application Review Checklist

| Signal | Invite | Screen-Out |
|--------|--------|------------|
| How they found it | Named person, specific context | "Saw it online," "looked interesting" |
| Self-description | What they do, where they are | What they are good at, what they want |
| Relationship to music | Specific, sometimes private | "Love all music," "there for the music" |
| Past event experience | Brief, non-judgmental | Extensive list, evaluative |
| Questions they ask | Practical, personal | Strategic, comparative |
| Tone | Slightly uncertain, real | Polished, certain, or weary |
| Photo | Looks like a person | Looks like a profile |

#### The Arrival Moment

Andrea's greeting is the final vetting gate. In the first 10 seconds:

- Does he make eye contact before checking his phone?
- Does he respond to being seen as a person, or does he treat the greeting as transaction?
- Does he have a question that is real, or is he performing having a question?
- Does his body relax marginally when welcomed, or does it stay armored?

The man who relaxes when seen is the Invite Man. The man who stays armored is not necessarily a Screen-Out — he may just need time. But the man who performs relaxation, who shows you that he is comfortable, who uses the greeting as an opportunity to establish himself — that is the Screen-Out who made it past the gate.
