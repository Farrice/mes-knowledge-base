## Profile 1: The Invite Woman — "Who Makes the Room Work"

### 1. The Psychographic Core

#### What She Believes About Relationships, Dating, and Meeting People

She believes that attraction is something you feel, not something you swipe into. She has not mistaken the efficiency of dating apps for progress. She believes that the conditions under which two people meet shape what becomes possible between them — that a dimly lit bar at 11pm and a sunny room at 2pm produce different kinds of connections. She believes, often from direct experience, that the best relationships she's had started with something unexpected — a friend's party, a work project, a shared situation that gave two people time to notice each other without either one performing.

She does not believe in "the one" as a cosmic category, but she does believe in specificity — that there are people whose particularity matches hers in ways that create something durable. She is not looking for someone to complete her. She is looking for someone she can build with, laugh with, be silent with, argue with and still want in the room.

She believes that meeting someone in person reveals something that profiles cannot. Not because she's romantic about serendipity, but because she's pragmatic: she has been on enough app dates where the person was fine on paper and absent in person. She trusts her gut more than an algorithm. Her gut, however, needs the right conditions to speak — conditions that apps and bars rarely provide.

#### What She Has Tried and Rejected

**Dating apps (Tinder, Bumble, Hinge):** She used them for 1-3 years. The breaking point was not a bad date — it was the accumulated deadness of the experience. The cycle of matching, small-talking, scheduling, meeting for a drink, performing interest, going home, never hearing from them or never wanting to hear from them. She felt herself becoming performative, then cynical, then numb. The specific hurt was not rejection — it was the feeling that she was being evaluated on criteria that had nothing to do with who she actually is. The men who seemed best on paper were often the most practiced, the most emotionally unavailable, the most likely to have optimized their profiles the way she had learned to optimize hers. She felt herself disappearing into a system designed for volume, not depth.

**Bars and nightlife:** She outgrew this in her late 20s. The breaking point was realizing that the men who approach women in bars are, by definition, the men comfortable approaching women in bars — a self-selecting group that skews toward confidence without substance. The noise, the alcohol, the progressive disinhibition that makes everyone seem more available than they are. She got tired of being approached by men she didn't want to talk to, and of wanting to talk to men who never approached her. The power dynamics felt stale. She felt like she was performing femininity in a space that rewarded performance.

**Setups by friends:** She appreciates these in theory. The breaking point was the mismatch between what friends think she wants and what she actually wants. Friends set her up based on superficial compatibility — same education level, similar job, both single — without understanding what actually makes her feel alive. The setups felt dutiful on both sides. She got tired of having dinner with men who were perfectly nice and completely wrong, then reporting back to a friend who meant well.

**Singles events she's found online:** If she's been to one, it was enough. The breaking point was the desperation in the room — not just the men's desperation, but the event's desperation. The forced mingling, the name tags, the activities designed to break the ice that only made everyone more self-conscious. She felt exposed for being there, and angry that she'd paid money for the privilege. The men were either too eager or too checked out. The whole thing felt like a group admission of failure.

**Speed dating:** She may have tried this once. The breaking point was the acceleration — being evaluated and evaluating others in three-minute increments activated every defensive mechanism she had. She left feeling like a product that hadn't sold well.

#### Her Relationship With Her Body and Movement

This is the critical differentiator for Resonance. The invite woman has a relationship with her body that is not merely functional. She does not need to be a dancer. She does not need to move well. What she needs is to not be alienated from her own physicality.

She experiences her body as a site of pleasure, not just evaluation. She likes how music feels in her chest. She has had the experience of moving to a beat and feeling her thinking mind quiet down. She may dance in her kitchen. She may have a playlist for walking. She may have been to a concert and felt, for a moment, that she was part of something larger than herself — not in a spiritual sense, but in the concrete sense of moving in rhythm with other bodies.

She does not experience her body as pure ornament. She is not performing attractiveness when she moves. This is why the sober, daytime, phone-free format works for her — it removes the conditions under which she would feel required to perform. She is not opposed to being seen; she is opposed to being surveilled. A room where no one is filming, where everyone is moving, where the light is natural — this is a room where she can be in her body without managing how her body is being consumed.

She may have anxiety about dancing in public. This is fine. What matters is that she is willing to move through the anxiety rather than avoid it. The invite woman is not the woman who walks in and owns the dance floor. She is the woman who feels awkward for the first twenty minutes, stays with the awkwardness, and then finds herself moving without deciding to.

#### What She Wants That She Hasn't Been Able to Find

She wants to meet someone in a context where both people are fully present — not drunk, not performing, not hiding behind a screen. She wants the condition of meeting to be one where she can actually assess who someone is, not who they are when optimized. She wants to feel something before she thinks something — to have her body tell her "pay attention to this person" before her mind has constructed a pro/con list.

She wants a partner who is comfortable in his own skin, who doesn't need to dominate every room, who can be still without being checked out. She wants someone whose intelligence shows up in how he listens, not just in what he says. She wants someone who has done enough work on himself that he's not looking for her to fix him, validate him, or complete him — but who still wants her specifically, not just a relationship-shaped person.

She wants to stop feeling like dating is a second job. She wants a format that respects her time, her intelligence, and her body. She wants to feel like the process of meeting people could be pleasurable, not just efficient.

#### Her Relationship With Vulnerability

She understands that meeting someone requires risk. She is not naive about this. What she wants is for the risk to be proportionate and mutual. Her frustration with dating apps is partly that the risk is distributed unevenly — she puts herself out there, assesses, messages, shows up, while too many men swipe and wait.

She is capable of being seen. She has done it before — in friendships, in past relationships, in creative or professional contexts where she had to show up without a script. She knows what it feels like to be looked at with interest that is not merely evaluative. She wants that.

Her fear is not of vulnerability itself. Her fear is of vulnerability without safety — of opening up in a context where the other person is not taking the same risk, or where the structure of the encounter penalizes openness. This is why the room matters so much. She will take social risk if she believes the room is taking it with her. She will be seen if she believes she is being seen by people who are also allowing themselves to be seen.

She is not looking for a group therapy session. She does not want to process her feelings in public. What she wants is a room where the normal defenses — the ones she carries into bars, apps, and blind dates — are temporarily unnecessary because the conditions of the room have made them obsolete.

#### Her Specific Frustration With Dating Apps

Go deeper than "they suck." Her specific frustration is multi-layered:

**The optimization trap:** She realized, at some point, that she was optimizing her profile for engagement rather than for finding the right person. The photos that got the most matches were not the photos that represented who she actually is. The bio that generated the most responses was not the bio that would attract the person she wanted. She was trapped in a system that rewarded broad appeal over specific signal. She felt herself becoming a brand manager for a product she wasn't sure she wanted to sell.

**The conversation death:** She got tired of carrying conversations. The "hey" openings. The men who answered questions but never asked them. The promising exchanges that fizzled for no discernible reason. The scheduling that required three rounds of negotiation. She felt herself becoming a project manager for a process that was supposed to be about connection.

**The date as interview:** By the time she met someone in person, they had already consumed so much of each other's biographical data that the first date felt like a verification exercise — "are you as described?" — rather than a discovery. There was no room for surprise. No room for her to show up as someone more interesting than her profile. No room for the unexpected thing that makes someone attractive in person despite being hard to describe on paper.

**The paradox of choice masked as abundance:** She was told apps gave her options. What she experienced was option paralysis — the constant sense that someone better might be one swipe away, which made it hard to invest in anyone, including herself as a person willing to be known. She felt herself becoming less generous, less patient, more evaluative than she wanted to be. The app made her someone she didn't like.

**The safety calculation:** She had to evaluate whether each man was dangerous. Not abstractly — specifically. The ones who pushed for her address. The ones who got angry when she didn't respond fast enough. The ones who turned hostile when she said no. The cumulative weight of this vigilance made the process exhausting before it even started.

#### What She Fears About Entering a Room Labeled "For Singles"

Everything. She fears being seen as desperate. She fears the kind of men who go to singles events. She fears forced interaction, name tags, icebreakers, the whole apparatus of engineered mingling. She fears that the event will be full of people who couldn't meet anyone through normal means — and that by attending, she is admitting she is one of those people.

She fears being approached by men she doesn't want to talk to and not being able to escape. She fears being the oldest person in the room, or the only person of her race, or the only person who doesn't fit the expected profile. She fears that the event will be a thinly disguised hookup environment. She fears that she will pay money, get dressed, show up, and realize within five minutes that she wants to leave but can't without being rude.

She fears that the event will confirm her worst suspicion about herself: that she is unpartnered because there is something wrong with her, and that gathering in a room with other unpartnered people will make this obvious.

This is what Andrea's curation has to overcome. The invite woman is not the woman who has no fear. She is the woman whose desire for something real has become stronger than her fear of looking foolish.

### 2. The Social Profile

#### What Her Social Circle Looks Like

She has a network, not a scene. Her friendships are long-duration, high-investment, and probably fewer than they were in her 20s. She has 3-5 people she would call in a crisis. Some are married; some are not. The married ones may have tried to set her up; she appreciates the intention even when the execution fails.

She does not have a large group of single female friends who go out together regularly. If she did, she would be less likely to need Resonance. Her social life is not oriented around meeting men — it is oriented around the people she already knows and trusts. This is part of why she has trouble meeting new people: her life works well enough that she doesn't have a structural reason to meet strangers, but the absence of a partner is the one gap that her existing network cannot fill.

She may have recently lost a cohort of friends to marriage and children — not dramatically, but gradually. The Saturday brunches became monthly, then quarterly. The group trips became family trips she wasn't invited to. She is not bitter about this, but she is aware that her social infrastructure has shifted beneath her, and she has not fully built the new one.

She has colleagues she likes but doesn't socialize with outside work. She may have activity-based communities — a running group, a book club, a volunteer commitment — where she knows people but hasn't met anyone she wants to partner with. She is not isolated. She is structurally misaligned with the places where partnership typically forms.

#### How She Spends Saturday Afternoons

Normally: she runs errands, goes to the gym, meets a friend for coffee, works on a side project, reads, walks by the lake, visits her family, or does nothing in particular. She values unstructured time. She does not typically spend Saturday afternoons in group activities with strangers. The fact that Resonance is on a Saturday afternoon is itself a signal — it says this is not a nightlife experience transplanted to daytime. It is something else.

She may have a standing Saturday commitment she will move or skip for Resonance. This is a meaningful indicator of interest. The woman who treats Saturday afternoon as disposable time is not the invite woman. The invite woman protects her Saturdays. For her to give one up, she has to believe the payoff is commensurate.

#### Her Relationship With Music and Dancing

She listens to music actively — not just as background. She has opinions about what she likes, even if she wouldn't call herself a music person. Her Spotify history includes: soul, R&B, house, disco, Afrobeats, Latin music, jazz, hip-hop, singer-songwriters from before 2010, albums not singles. She does not listen to Top 40 by default. She may not know what is currently trending on TikTok, and she does not feel bad about this.

She has danced in public at least once in the past year — at a wedding, a concert, a festival, a friend's party. She remembers it fondly. The specific memory is not of dancing well; it is of dancing freely, without self-consciousness, in a context where everyone else was doing the same. This is the experience Resonance is trying to recreate.

She may have a specific relationship with Chicago's music history — house music originated here, and she may feel some civic pride about that even if she doesn't go to house music events. The mention of house, soul, and disco in the Resonance description signals cultural literacy to her. It says: we know where we are, and we know what sounds right in this city.

#### Her Relationship With Chicago

She has lived here at least 3 years, probably longer. She has a neighborhood she belongs to — not just lives in. She knows her coffee shop, her grocery store, her running route, her local bar (even if she doesn't go there to meet men). She has opinions about the CTA, the lakefront, summer in the city, winter in the city. She is not a transplant looking for community; she is an established Chicagoan looking for partnership within the city she already loves.

She may have considered leaving Chicago — for a bigger dating pool, for a job, for a change. She has stayed because something about this city fits her. The specificity of Resonance being in Chicago matters to her. She does not want a generic dating event. She wants something that could only happen here.

#### How She Heard About Resonance, and What Made Her Trust It

This is a critical path. The invite woman does not find Resonance through a Facebook ad. She finds it through:

**Andrea's personal network:** Someone she knows — a friend, a colleague, a person she trusts from another context — mentions it personally. Not a mass post. A direct message, a conversation, a "I heard about this from someone I trust, and I thought of you." This is the highest-trust channel because it comes with pre-vetting from someone whose judgment she already respects.

**Social proof from a woman she respects:** She sees a woman she follows — not an influencer, not a celebrity, but someone whose taste she trusts — post about Resonance in a way that feels genuine. The post is not an ad. It is a personal recommendation, maybe with some detail about what it was actually like. The specificity of the description signals authenticity.

**Andrea's content:** She encounters something Andrea wrote or recorded — a post, a voice note, a description of the event — that makes her feel like Andrea understands something about the dating landscape that she herself has experienced but never articulated. Andrea's voice feels like the voice of someone who has been to the bad events, who knows what the invite woman is afraid of, and who has designed something different on purpose. This is not marketing copy. This is someone speaking to her directly about something they both know.

**The description itself:** She reads the event description and something in it makes her pause. The specificity of the music genres. The "phones in a basket" detail. The "no forced introductions." The mention of cut fruit and water instead of a bar. These details signal intentionality. They say: someone thought about this carefully. The absence of certain language also signals — no "find your soulmate," no "empowered dating," no "conscious connection." The restraint is itself a signal.

What makes her trust it enough to come: she sees evidence that the person behind the event understands what she fears and has designed around it. She sees that the event is curated — not just anyone can walk in. She sees that the format is unusual enough that it would attract unusual people. She sees that the event costs something — not a lot, but enough to filter out people who aren't serious. She sees that it is small — 50 people, not 500. She sees that it is during the day, sober, and in a specific location. All of these specifics add up to: this is not a scam, not a hookup event, not a desperate gathering. This is something someone made with care.

#### What She Tells Her Friends About Where She's Going

Even to close friends, she may not say "I'm going to a singles event." She says: "I'm going to this thing my friend told me about — it's a daytime dance party, kind of, but curated. The founder vets everyone personally. It's supposed to be really good music, no phones, no bar. I'm curious." Or she says: "There's this event in [neighborhood] — a woman named Andrea runs it. It's for people who want to meet someone but are done with apps. I don't know, it sounds different."

The cover story serves two functions: it protects her from her own fear of looking desperate, and it gives her an exit narrative if it goes badly. If she has a good time, she will revise the story on Sunday. If she doesn't, she will say "it was interesting" and leave it there.

The friend she tells is likely single too. If the friend is married, the conversation is different — more explanatory, more defensive. If the friend is single and curious, she is already thinking about whether she would go to the next one. This is how Resonance grows: not through virality, but through one woman deciding it is safe enough to tell one other woman.

### 3. What She Brings to the Room

#### What Her Presence Signals to Other Women

Women read other women constantly. It is not competitive; it is diagnostic. Every woman who enters the room is evaluating, consciously or not: who are these other women? Are they like me? Are they the kind of women I would be friends with? Are they the kind of women who would attract the kind of men I want to meet?

The invite woman's presence signals:

**She is not trying too hard.** She has dressed well but not performatively. She looks like someone who has a life outside this room — not someone who has been planning her outfit for three days as if this were the most important event of her year. This relaxes other women. It says: you don't need to compete with me, because I am not competing.

**She is comfortable in her own attention.** She does not need to be the center of the room, but she does not shrink from being seen. She makes eye contact. She smiles at other women. She does not treat the other women as obstacles or rivals. This is palpable. Other women feel it immediately.

**She has social skills without social dominance.** She can talk to strangers, but she does not command every conversation she enters. She leaves space. She listens. She does not perform her intelligence or her accomplishments. This makes quieter women feel safe around her.

**She moves without self-consciousness (eventually).** The other women watch how she handles the dance floor. If she is willing to be awkward, to move without knowing how she looks, she gives permission to every other woman in the room to do the same. This is perhaps the single most important thing she brings: the modeling of non-performative presence.

#### What Her Presence Signals to Men

Men also read her, though they may not articulate it this way. What they notice:

**She is not scanning the room for targets.** She is not the woman who walked in and immediately started evaluating every man against her checklist. She is present in a way that suggests she came for the experience, not just the outcome. This makes men feel safe to approach her — not because she seems easy, but because she seems like someone who would receive approach as conversation rather than audition.

**She has her own relationship with the music.** She is not waiting for a man to give her permission to dance. She is not standing at the edge of the floor looking available. She is moving because she wants to move. This is attractive in a way that "looking attractive" never is. Men notice this because it signals that she has an interior life — that she is a person having an experience, not a person waiting for an experience to happen to her.

**She makes eye contact without agenda.** She looks at people. She smiles. She does not immediately look away as if caught doing something wrong, nor does she hold eye contact as an invitation. She treats the room as a room full of humans, not a room full of prospects. This creates the conditions for natural approach — the kind of approach that feels like something emerging rather than something initiated.

#### What Specific Behavior on the Floor Unlocks Other People

The invite woman is not necessarily the first person dancing. She may be the second or third. What matters is that she dances before she feels fully comfortable. She makes the move from observer to participant while still feeling some hesitation. This is the moment other people can relate to — the moment of "if she can do it while still feeling awkward, maybe I can too."

She dances alone or with other women at first. She does not position herself near men as a strategy. She lets the music carry her into proximity with other people rather than choosing proximity as a goal. This creates natural adjacencies — she ends up near people because they are all moving to the same thing, not because she picked a spot.

She responds to the music visibly — a head nod, a smile when a particular song comes on, a moment of closing her eyes. These are signals that she is having a real experience, not performing having an experience. Other people read this and it gives them permission to have their own real experience.

When she does talk to someone, she is present in the conversation. She does not keep scanning the room. She does not angle her body toward the door in case someone better walks in. She treats the person in front of her as worth her attention for the duration of the conversation. This is noticed. It sets a standard for the room.

#### What Her Post-Event Description Sounds Like

If it went well, she says something like:

> "It was... not what I expected. The music was really good — like, actually good, not event music. Everyone was vetted, so there was this baseline of... I don't know, quality? People were there for real. I danced with someone — not like, romantically, we just danced near each other, and then we talked, and... I don't know, it felt easy. It felt like meeting someone at a party where you both got invited by the same person who has good taste. That's what it felt like — like Andrea has good taste in people."

Or:

> "I was so nervous walking in. But Andrea greeted me, and then I put my phone away, and after like twenty minutes of feeling awkward, I just... danced. And then I noticed this guy near me, and we kept ending up near each other, and eventually we talked, and he was interesting. Like, actually interesting. Not trying to impress me. Just... a person."

Or, to the friend she's trying to convince to come next time:

> "It wasn't weird. I know that sounds like a low bar, but it really wasn't weird. No one was drunk. No one was creepy. The men were... I don't know, they seemed like they were there because they actually wanted to meet someone, not because they wanted to hook up. It felt safe. It felt like the opposite of a singles event."

### 4. The Sub-Types

#### Sub-Type A: The Direct Connection

**How she arrives:** Andrea knows her personally, or knows someone who knows her. She arrives with pre-loaded trust because the invitation came through a channel she already respects. She may have heard Andrea describe the event in person, where Andrea's passion and specificity were palpable.

**What she's looking for:** She may be actively looking for partnership or she may be "open but not looking" — the direct connection gives her enough trust to show up either way. The personal referral lowers the activation energy.

**What she brings:** She brings social proof. Other women notice that she seems relaxed, that she greets Andrea warmly, that she knows the deal. She models ease for everyone else. She is likely to be the first woman on the dance floor because she trusts the room more than the average attendee.

**What she needs to see:** She needs to see that Andrea's curation matches Andrea's description. She is evaluating whether Andrea's taste in people is as good as Andrea's taste in music. If the room delivers, she becomes a lifelong advocate. If it doesn't, she will be kind about it but she won't come back, and she won't bring friends.

**How to spot during vetting:** She may not need extensive vetting if the referral is strong. Look for: personal warmth, some evidence of having a life outside dating, capacity for genuine enthusiasm. She should not seem like someone who is merely doing her friend a favor by attending. She should seem genuinely curious.

**What might push her away:** If the room feels mismatched — if Andrea's curation was described as one thing and delivers another. If the music is not as good as promised. If the men seem lower-quality than she was led to expect. She holds Andrea to a high standard because she knows Andrea personally.

#### Sub-Type B: The Professional Who Has Tried Everything

**Profile:** Lawyer, doctor, consultant, engineer, executive. She is accomplished in a field that rewards analytical thinking and structured problem-solving. She has approached dating the way she approaches everything else — systematically — and it has failed. She is frustrated by the mismatch between her professional competence and her dating incompetence. She does not understand why something she is so good at in every other domain eludes her here.

**How she arrives:** Probably through content or a referral from a colleague. She researched Resonance before deciding to attend. She may have looked up Andrea, read previous attendee feedback, evaluated the format for plausibility. She does not do things on impulse.

**What she's looking for:** Active partnership. She wants a serious relationship. She is efficient with her time and wants a format that respects this. She is not looking to date five people; she is looking to meet one person worth investing in.

**Her relationship with her body:** This varies widely. Some professional women are deeply embodied — they run, they dance, they do yoga, they know their bodies well. Others are alienated from their bodies, living primarily from the neck up, and this is part of their problem in dating. They don't know how to feel attraction because they are so practiced at evaluating people intellectually. For the latter group, Resonance is potentially transformative — the body-first mechanic does the work their minds have been blocking. But they are also the group most likely to resist the format initially, standing at the edge of the room with their arms crossed, waiting for it to make sense intellectually before they participate physically.

**What she brings:** She brings seriousness. She brings the sense that the room is full of people who have their lives together. She brings conversational depth — when she talks to someone, she knows how to actually listen and respond. She models intentionality.

**What she needs to see:** She needs evidence that the men are at her level — professionally competent, emotionally present, not intimidated by her. She needs to see that the format produces something that apps cannot. She needs one conversation that surprises her, one moment where she feels something she didn't expect to feel.

**What might push her away:** Men who are performatively impressed by her career. Men who try to compete with her professionally. A room that feels fuzzy or unstructured — she needs to believe there is design behind the experience, not just good intentions. If she can't see the logic of the format, she will disengage.

**How to spot during vetting:** Look for: evidence of professional competence without performative careerism. She should be able to talk about what she does without making it her whole identity. Look for some relationship with her body — even if it's just "I run on weekends" or "I used to dance." The woman who has zero physical practice and expresses skepticism about the dance element needs extra vetting conversation about what the format actually requires.

#### Sub-Type C: The Creative / Cultural Worker

**Profile:** Designer, artist, writer, musician, teacher, nonprofit worker, curator, editor. She works in a field that values intuition, taste, and emotional intelligence. She may not make as much money as the professional woman, but she has a rich interior life and strong opinions about culture — music, film, art, food, neighborhoods.

**How she arrives:** Almost certainly through Andrea's content or a referral from someone in her cultural network. She is drawn to the music description first — the specificity of the genres signals that this is for people with taste, not people who will play whatever is on the radio. She recognizes Andrea as a fellow traveler in cultural curation.

**What she's looking for:** She may not say she is looking for partnership, even to herself. She frames it as "I'm open to meeting someone" or "I just want to have a good time and see what happens." This is partly genuine — she does want to have a good time — and partly defensive. Underneath, she wants partnership as much as anyone. She just doesn't want to need it.

**Her relationship with her body:** Usually strong. Creative work tends to keep people connected to physical experience — even writers know what it feels like to be in their bodies when the work is going well. She likely dances, or has danced, or wants to dance more. The music-centered format is the primary draw for her. She would come to Resonance even if partnership were not the stated goal, because a daytime party with that music in that setting sounds like exactly how she wants to spend a Saturday.

**What she brings:** She brings cultural energy. She brings the sense that the room is full of interesting people. She is likely to be the person who compliments the DJ on a specific track, who notices the vinyl, who appreciates the lighting and the space. Her enthusiasm is contagious — when she is having a good time, everyone around her feels it.

**What she needs to see:** She needs the music to be genuinely good — not adequate, not "good for a singles event," but actually good. She needs the room to feel aesthetically coherent. She needs to believe that Andrea has taste, because taste is her primary frame for evaluating people. She needs at least one person in the room — man or woman — with whom she can have a conversation about something cultural that excites them both.

**What might push her away:** Music that feels generic. A room that feels like it was designed by committee rather than curated by someone with a point of view. Men who talk about their jobs instead of what they're interested in. The sense that the event is trying to be something for everyone rather than something specific for specific people.

**How to spot during vetting:** Look for: cultural specificity in how she describes herself. References to music, art, neighborhoods, specific experiences in Chicago. She should be able to answer "what are you into?" with something more interesting than "travel and food." Look for genuine enthusiasm — the woman who lights up when she talks about something she loves is the woman who will light up the room.

#### Sub-Type D: The Post-Divorce / Post-Long-Term-Relationship Woman

**Profile:** She was married or in a long-term partnership for 3+ years. She has been single again for 6 months to 2 years. She has done some amount of processing — she is not freshly wounded, but she is also not fully healed. She is somewhere between "I don't know if I ever want to partner again" and "I miss having someone."

**How she arrives:** Very carefully. She is the hardest conversion because she has the most to protect. She may have been referred by a friend who has watched her isolate herself, or she may have found Resonance through content that spoke to her specific situation — the emphasis on safety, on slowness, on the absence of hookup culture. She needs to believe that this is not Tinder in a room.

**What she's looking for:** She is not looking to replace her ex. She is looking to believe that partnership is still possible with someone new. She may not know what she wants because the landscape has changed since she was last single. The apps terrify her — she has heard the stories, and the idea of presenting herself as a profile to be evaluated feels intolerable after the failure of her marriage.

**Her relationship with her body:** Often complicated. Long-term partnerships create specific bodily habits — she was used to being touched by one person, and now she is not touched enough. She may feel older than the other women in the room even if she is not. She may feel invisible in ways she didn't expect. The dance floor is both a risk and an opportunity for her — the risk is feeling exposed in a body she has not shared with strangers in years; the opportunity is remembering that her body can feel pleasure independent of any specific relationship.

**What she brings:** She brings depth. She brings the knowledge of what long-term partnership actually requires, which makes her evaluations of men more grounded. She is less likely to be swept away by surface charm. She brings patience — she is not in a rush because she knows that rushing is how you end up with the wrong person. If she connects with someone, the connection is likely to be real.

**What she needs to see:** She needs to see men who are emotionally mature — who have some life experience, who know what they want, who are not playing games. She needs the room to feel safe enough that she can be present without performing availability. She needs Andrea to see her — to greet her warmly, to check in without hovering, to make her feel like she belongs there even if she is unsure herself.

**What might push her away:** Men who seem too young, too eager, or too unformed. A room that feels like it is designed for people in their 20s. Any hint of hookup culture — the man who approaches too physically, the woman who is clearly there for casual connection. Being made to feel like her age or her history is a liability rather than an asset.

**How to spot during vetting:** She may be more reserved in initial communication. She may ask more questions about the format, the crowd, the expectations. This is not skepticism — it is self-protection. Look for: evidence that she has reflected on her past relationship, that she knows something about what went wrong and what she wants differently. The woman who blames her ex entirely is a screen-out. The woman who can talk about her past with some complexity is an invite. She should express some genuine curiosity about the format, even if it is cautious.

#### Sub-Type E: The Introvert Who Needs the Room to Be Gentle

**Profile:** She may fit any of the above categories professionally or personally, but her defining characteristic is that she is introverted, socially anxious, or highly sensitive. She does not thrive in environments that require rapid social initiation. She needs structure that protects her from the exhaustion of unstructured mingling.

**How she arrives:** Almost certainly through a trusted referral. She would not find Resonance through a public post and decide to attend on her own. Someone she trusts had to tell her, probably more than once, that it would be okay. She may have said no the first time she was invited.

**What she's looking for:** She wants partnership desperately but finds the process of meeting people excruciating. She has probably had good experiences in relationships — she is likely a wonderful partner once she is in a relationship — but the activation energy required to get into one feels insurmountable. She is drawn to Resonance specifically because the format does not require her to perform extroversion. The dance floor does the social labor. The music provides the structure. She does not have to make small talk unless she wants to.

**Her relationship with her body:** Variable. Some introverts are deeply connected to their bodies — physical experience is where they process what they cannot articulate. Others are dissociated, living primarily in their internal worlds. The dance floor can be a bridge for both: for the embodied introvert, it is a native language; for the dissociated introvert, it is a gentle way back into physical presence without requiring social performance.

**What she brings:** She brings stillness. In a room full of people trying to have a good time, her quiet presence is a relief. Men notice her precisely because she is not trying to be noticed. When she does connect with someone, the connection is likely to be deep rather than wide — one real conversation rather than five superficial ones. She models that there are multiple ways to be present in the room.

**What she needs to see:** She needs to see that she will not be forced to interact. She needs the absence of icebreakers, mandatory mingling, or pressure to circulate. She needs to see other people who are also quiet, who are standing at the edge of the room listening to the music, who are not being pressured to participate. She needs to see Andrea making the room feel safe for all kinds of presence — loud and quiet, moving and still.

**What she needs from the music:** The music is everything for her. If the music is good enough, she can be present without talking. She can have a full experience — joy, pleasure, connection — without ever initiating a conversation. The music is her access point. If the music is weak, she has no cover, no structure, no way to be in the room without performing social availability.

**What might push her away:** Any forced interaction. A host who tries to "draw her out." Men who interpret her quietness as aloofness and approach her with energy designed to break through her reserve. A room that feels like it rewards extroversion and penalizes stillness. The sense that she is doing it wrong by not circulating.

**How to spot during vetting:** She may communicate in shorter sentences, take longer to respond, ask specific logistical questions about the format. Look for: some evidence that she has a rich interior life — she may be a reader, a maker, someone with a solo practice of some kind. She should express some specific interest in the music or the format, even if tentatively. The woman who seems like she is being pushed into attending by a friend is a risk — she needs to have her own reason for coming, even if that reason is just "I want to believe this could work."

#### Sub-Type F: The Woman of Color in Chicago

**Profile:** Chicago is one of the most segregated cities in America, and this shapes every social experience. The woman of color who would attend Resonance has navigated these dynamics her whole life. She is not looking for an event that pretends race doesn't matter. She is looking for an event where she will not be the only one, where her presence is normal rather than exceptional, where she does not have to represent her entire race or educate white people about her existence.

**How she arrives:** Through Andrea's network, through Black or Latino cultural channels in Chicago, through word-of-mouth in her specific community. She is evaluating Resonance for inclusivity before she evaluates it for anything else. She has been to too many "diverse" Chicago events where diversity meant one or two people who looked like her in a sea of whiteness.

**What she's looking for:** She wants what everyone wants — partnership, connection, a room full of interesting people. But she also wants a room where she does not have to code-switch to be comfortable, where the music includes the sounds she grew up with or dances to now, where men who look like her are present and represented as desirable.

**Her relationship with the music:** The inclusion of Afrobeat, Afro-Latin, and Latin music is not decorative for her. It is a signal that the room is for her too. She knows whether the DJ knows these genres or is just playing the obvious tracks. The music is a test of cultural competence.

**What she brings:** She brings perspective. She brings the capacity to make the room feel genuinely inclusive rather than performatively diverse. If she feels comfortable, other women of color will feel comfortable. She becomes a bridge and a validator.

**What she needs to see:** She needs to see other people who look like her — not as tokens, but as integrated participants. She needs to see men of color who are being their full selves, not compressed into the narrow range of acceptable behavior that predominantly white spaces often require. She needs Andrea to demonstrate cultural fluency — not by calling attention to it, but by the natural inclusion of music, food, language, and social norms that don't center whiteness.

**What might push her away:** A room that feels predominantly white with a sprinkling of diversity. Men who exoticize her. Any hint that she is being invited because she fills a demographic quota rather than because she is a person Andrea wants in the room. Cultural incompetence in the music selection — the wrong Afrobeats track, the Latin music that is clearly just the one playlist everyone uses.

**How to spot during vetting:** Look for: Chicago rootedness — how she talks about the city, her neighborhood, her community. Look for evidence that she has cultural practices she values. Look for the woman who asks about the crowd not because she is picky but because she needs to know she won't be alone. She should feel like someone who would add to the room's texture, not just its demographic spreadsheet.

#### Sub-Type G: The "I Would Never Go to a Singles Event" Woman

**Profile:** She is the highest-value attendee and the hardest to convert. She has never been to a singles event and swears she never would. She finds the entire category embarrassing. She met her previous partners through normal life — school, work, friends — and the fact that she now needs a structured environment to meet someone feels like a personal failure.

**How she arrives:** She doesn't come through a channel labeled "singles event." She comes because someone she trusts described Resonance as something else — "this incredible daytime party with amazing music," "this thing my friend Andrea does, it's hard to explain but it's not what you think," "I went and it was the most fun I've had on a Saturday in months." The framing never includes the words "singles" or "dating" or "meeting people." She comes because it sounds like a good time, and only later discovers that partnership is the underlying purpose.

**What she's looking for:** She is probably "open but not looking" — or at least that's what she tells herself. She may be genuinely surprised when she meets someone interesting, because she wasn't trying to. This is the ideal outcome for Resonance: she came for the music and stayed for the person.

**What she brings:** She brings the energy of someone who is not trying to meet someone. This is paradoxically the most attractive energy in the room. She is the woman who dances because she loves the song, who talks to someone because they both noticed the same thing, who is surprised by her own interest. She gives the room credibility — if someone this skeptical, this tasteful, this not-needy is here, then maybe this place is actually cool.

**What she needs to see:** She needs to see that her worst fears about "singles events" are unfounded. She needs to walk in and realize, within five minutes, that this is not a singles event as she has imagined them. She needs the music to be good, the people to be interesting, the vibe to be something she would want to be part of even if she were already partnered. She needs to not feel embarrassed to tell people where she went.

**What might push her away:** Anything that feels like a singles event. Name tags. Forced mingling. A host who makes partnership feel like the only reason anyone is there. Men who approach with obvious romantic intent rather than human curiosity. The sense that she has been tricked into attending something she would have said no to if described accurately.

**How to spot during vetting:** She may not go through traditional vetting at all — she may arrive as someone's plus-one, or through a channel where the vetting happened indirectly. If she does apply directly, look for: reluctance masked as curiosity. She may ask "what exactly is this?" in a way that signals she needs reassurance it is not what she thinks it is. She should have some genuine relationship with music, culture, or social experience that gives her a non-dating reason to attend. The woman who has zero interest in the music and is purely looking to meet men is not this sub-type, even if she says she "would never go to a singles event."

### 5. What She Needs From the Room

#### What She Needs to See in the First 5 Minutes

**Andrea at the door.** Not staff — Andrea. The founder greeting her personally. This is the first signal that the event is curated by a human, not produced by a company. The warmth of the greeting, the eye contact, the sense that Andrea is genuinely glad she came — this sets the emotional temperature for everything that follows.

**The phone basket.** She needs to see other people putting their phones away. This signals shared commitment. It also removes the one escape route she would otherwise use — the ability to retreat into her device when she feels awkward. The basket says: we are all in this together, and there is no backstage.

**Other women who look like her.** Not physically — psychologically. Women who seem like they have lives, who are dressed well but not trying too hard, who are not clumped in defensive circles or scanning the room with predatory focus. She needs to see women who seem like people she would want to know, because this is how she evaluates whether the men are worth meeting.

**The space itself.** Daylight. Water and fruit, not a bar. No dim lighting hiding anything. The space should feel like a good place to be — aesthetically considered, physically comfortable, not a nightclub pretending to be something else. She should think "I would come here for something that wasn't dating-related."

**The absence of certain things:** No name tags. No forced mingling structures. No host with a microphone making announcements. No "ladies free" energy. No desperation in the air. The absence of these things is as important as the presence of positive elements.

#### What She Needs to See in the Men

This is the critical evaluation, and it happens fast. Within the first 20 minutes, she has formed a provisional assessment of the men in the room. What she needs to see:

**Men who are present in their bodies.** This is the first and most important thing. She needs to see men dancing — not well, necessarily, but genuinely. Men who are moving to the music, who have their eyes closed or their heads nodding, who are not scanning the room while they dance. The man who is fully in the music signals that he is capable of being fully in an experience, which is a prerequisite for being fully in a relationship.

**Men who moved through awkwardness.** She needs to see evidence that the men were also nervous walking in, that they also didn't know what to expect, and that they stayed with the discomfort until it shifted into something else. The man who walked in confident is suspicious — he has done this before, or he is performing confidence. The man who became present by going through the awkwardness is the one she wants to meet.

**Men who seem like they'd be interesting regardless of whether she's single.** This is the "would I want to talk to this person at a party?" test. The man who is only interesting as a romantic prospect is not interesting enough. She needs to see men who have things to say, curiosity about the world, opinions about music or culture or Chicago or life. Men who showed up for themselves, not just to meet women.

**The absence of certain behaviors:** No men standing in clusters evaluating women. No men approaching with obvious pickup energy. No men who are too drunk (there shouldn't be any alcohol, but if someone snuck it in, she'll notice). No men who seem to be performing masculinity — the loud ones, the ones who need to dominate space, the ones who treat the room like a hunting ground. The absence of these men is as important as the presence of the right ones.

**Men who make eye contact without immediately approaching.** The man who can look at her, smile, and return to his own experience signals patience and self-sufficiency. This creates the conditions for a natural approach later — an approach that feels like continuation rather than initiation.

#### What She Needs From the Music

The music does the emotional labor so the people don't have to. This is the core mechanic, and she needs to feel it working. Specifically:

**She needs to recognize something.** Not everything — the point is discovery — but at least one song in the first 30 minutes that makes her think "oh, I love this" or "I haven't heard this in forever." This creates trust with the DJ and with the event. If she recognizes nothing, she feels alien. If she recognizes everything, she feels pandered to. The ratio should be roughly 30% familiar, 70% discovery.

**She needs the tempo to build.** The set should start accessible and build gradually. The first tracks should be inviting — something she can nod her head to even if she isn't ready to dance. The build should be gradual enough that she finds herself moving more without deciding to.

**She needs the music to create proximity without forcing it.** The best moments on a dance floor happen when two people are moving to the same thing and find themselves near each other without either one choosing it. The music creates these natural adjacencies. She needs to experience this at least once — the sense that the music brought her together with someone.

**She needs the occasional ballad or down-tempo track.** These create punctuation — moments to catch her breath, to have a conversation, to feel something more vulnerable. A set that is all high energy produces a different kind of connection than one that breathes. She needs the breathing.

**She needs the music to feel like Chicago.** House music was born here. Soul and disco have deep roots. The Afro-Latin connections reflect the city's demographics. The music should feel like it belongs in this city on this day, not like it could be playing anywhere.

#### What She Needs From Andrea

**As host:** She needs Andrea to be present and visible without dominating. Andrea should greet her at the door, be available if she needs something, and otherwise be part of the room — dancing, listening, experiencing. Andrea should not be the emcee, the MC, or the facilitator. She should be the host in the original sense: the person who created the conditions and is now enjoying them with her guests.

**As woman:** She needs to see Andrea as someone like her — someone who has navigated the same dating landscape, who has been to the bad events, who decided to build something better. This is not about disclosure; it is about recognition. She should be able to look at Andrea and think "she gets it." This is what makes her trust Andrea's curation — the sense that Andrea's standards are her standards.

**As curator:** She needs to believe that Andrea has good taste in people. This belief is formed by observing who else is in the room. If the room is full of interesting, present, non-desperate people, she attributes this to Andrea's curation and her trust deepens. If the room is uneven, her trust in Andrea diminishes regardless of how warm the greeting was. The curation is the product; everything else is packaging.

**What she needs from Andrea specifically:** A warm greeting. One check-in during the event — not "are you having fun?" but something more specific, something that shows Andrea sees her. And a warm goodbye at the end. These three touchpoints — hello, check-in, goodbye — create the arc of being hosted.

#### What She Needs From the Other Women

This is under-explored in event design. Women do not evaluate rooms in isolation. They evaluate them through other women. What she needs:

**She needs to not feel competitive.** The room should feel like there are enough good men to go around — not because there is a 1:1 ratio, but because the men are good enough that she doesn't feel she has to fight for attention. The presence of other attractive women should feel like evidence that the room is worth being in, not like a threat.

**She needs to see other women having a good time.** Women dancing freely, women in conversation with men, women who seem relaxed and present — these are signals that the room is safe. If other women seem guarded, performative, or uncomfortable, she absorbs that energy and becomes more guarded herself.

**She needs at least one woman she could imagine being friends with.** Not necessarily that they become friends — just that she sees someone and thinks "she seems cool." This validates her own presence. It says: you are the kind of person who is at this kind of event. You belong here.

**She needs the absence of competitive female energy.** The woman who evaluates every other woman on entry, who performs her attractiveness for the men, who treats other women as obstacles — this woman poisons the room for everyone. The invite woman needs to see that Andrea has filtered this out.

**She needs to see women of different ages, races, and styles.** Not as a diversity checklist — as evidence that the room is for women like her, whatever "like her" means. Monoculture feels like a scene, and scenes are excluding by definition. A room with some texture feels like a city, and she lives in a city.

#### What Would Make Her Come Back

One good conversation. Not necessarily with a romantic prospect — just one conversation where she felt like she met someone interesting. The bar for return is lower than the bar for recommending. To come back, she needs to believe it could work. One piece of evidence is enough.

Meeting someone she wants to see again. This is obvious but worth stating. If she meets one person — romantic or not — who she wants to continue knowing, the event becomes part of her life rather than an experiment she tried once.

The music being genuinely good. If the music was a primary draw and it delivered, she will come back even if she didn't meet anyone. She will come back because it was a good Saturday afternoon, and good Saturday afternoons are worth repeating.

Feeling like she was seen. Not by a man — by Andrea, by the room, by the experience. She needs to feel like her presence mattered, like she was not just a body in a room counted toward a gender ratio.

#### What Would Make Her Bring Someone

She will bring someone when she feels confident that the event will not embarrass her. This confidence comes from one successful experience plus the belief that the next one will be at the same level. She will not bring her most skeptical friend to the first event — she will come alone, test it, and only then become an advocate.

When she does bring someone, she will pre-frame it carefully: "I went to this thing — it's hard to explain, but the music is incredible and the people are actually interesting. I think you would like it. It's not what you think." She needs cover stories for her friend just as she needed one for herself.

#### What Would Make Her Feel Unsafe or Want to Leave

**One man who doesn't respect boundaries.** It only takes one. The man who approaches too physically, who won't take no for an answer, who follows her around the room. She will not report him to Andrea — she will just leave, and she will not come back. The cost of one bad man is not just that woman leaving; it is every woman in the room watching how the event handles it and forming her own exit strategy.

**The sense that the men are lower-quality than advertised.** If she was promised vetting and sees men who seem like they wandered in off the street, her trust evaporates. She feels bait-and-switched. She does not complain; she just notes that the curation is not what she was told, and she adjusts her expectations accordingly — which means she has no expectations, which means she won't return.

**A hookup energy that she wasn't expecting.** If the room starts to feel like a nightclub — sexual, aggressive, alcohol-fueled — she will leave. She came for something specific and she will recognize immediately if it is not being delivered.

**Being ignored.** Not by men — by the event. If she stands at the edge of the room for an hour and no one — not Andrea, not another woman, not a man — makes any kind of contact with her, she will feel like she paid money to feel invisible. This is worse than rejection. Rejection at least involves being seen.

**The music stopping or declining in quality.** If the DJ loses the room, if the energy drops and doesn't recover, if the set starts to feel random or uninspired, she loses her primary reason for being there. The music is the foundation; if it cracks, everything built on it falls.

### 6. The "She's Here Because the Men Are Right" Signal

#### The Specific Moment or Observation

It is not one thing. It is a series of observations that accumulate until she crosses a threshold. But if there is a single moment, it is probably this: she looks around the room and sees a man dancing with his eyes closed, completely in the music, not performing for anyone, not checking to see if women are watching — and she thinks, "oh. These men are different."

Or: she has a conversation with a man who asks her a question and then actually listens to the answer. Not waiting for his turn to speak. Not using her answer as a springboard to talk about himself. Just listening, responding, being present. She may not even be attracted to him. But she notices that he is a person, not a performer, and she extrapolates from this that the room is full of people.

Or: she watches a man approach a woman, the woman seem not interested, and the man receive that gracefully — no argument, no persistence, no wounded ego. He just moves on. This is noticed by every woman in the vicinity. It says: the men here can handle rejection. Which means the women here are safe to say no.

Or: she finds herself in a conversation that went somewhere unexpected. She mentioned something — a band, a neighborhood, a book — and the man knew it, had an opinion about it, wanted to know her opinion. The conversation had texture. It was not an interview. It was two people discovering they share a reference point.

#### What She Notices That Makes Her Relax Her Guard

**The men are not in clusters.** In most mixed-gender social events, men cluster together for safety and only disperse to approach women. At Resonance, the men should be distributed — some dancing, some talking to each other, some talking to women, some alone. The distribution signals that the men are comfortable in the room, not just enduring it until they can make their move.

**The men are not performing for her.** They are having their own experience. They are enjoying the music, talking to each other, being present. She is not the center of their attention, and this paradoxically makes her more willing to give them her attention.

**The men make eye contact without immediately approaching.** This is the critical middle ground between ignoring her and hunting her. The man who can look at her, acknowledge her, and return to his own experience signals confidence without aggression. He is not afraid of her, but he does not need her. This creates the conditions for approach that feels mutual rather than predatory.

**The men are dressed like they have lives.** Not like they are going clubbing. Not like they are going to the office. Like they are people who know how to dress for a Saturday afternoon party. This signals social intelligence — they understood the assignment.

**The men talk to each other.** Men who only talk to women at singles events are suspicious. Men who have genuine conversations with each other signal that they are actual humans with friendships and interests, not just mating-drive robots in human suits.

#### The Absence That Opens Her Up

**The absence of the man who evaluates her as she enters.** She knows this man. He is at every bar, every party, every event. He scans every woman who walks in, decides her value, and adjusts his approach accordingly. His eyes are a rating system. His absence is palpable. When she walks into Resonance and no one does this, she feels something shift. She is not being consumed. She is entering a room.

**The absence of the man who needs to dominate.** The loud one. The one who tells the longest stories. The one who needs to be the center of every conversation. His absence creates space — for other men, for women, for the music, for silence.

**The absence of alcohol as social lubricant.** She may not consciously notice this, but she feels it. The conversations are slower, more tentative, more real. No one is performing disinhibited confidence. Everyone is actually having to be present, and this creates a solidarity of awkwardness that is more connecting than the false solidarity of shared intoxication.

**The absence of phones.** She may not realize how much of her social vigilance is dedicated to managing being filmed, photographed, or posted about until that possibility is removed. The basket at the door creates a temporary zone of privacy. What happens in the room stays in the room. This allows her to be more open than she would otherwise be.

**The absence of desperation.** This is the aggregate effect of all the other absences. When no one is desperate — not the men, not the women, not the event itself — she can stop being vigilant and start being present. This is the moment Resonance is designed to create. It is the moment she stops evaluating and starts experiencing. It is the moment she becomes available for connection not because she decided to be, but because the room made it safe to be.

## Profile 2: The Screen-Out Woman — "Who Seems Right but Poisons the Room"

### 1. The Types to Screen Out

#### Type A: The Shopping Expedition

**What she looks like on paper:** Accomplished, attractive, articulate. She probably has a strong professional background and presents well in written communication. Her application might even be one of the stronger ones — she knows how to present herself, how to say the right things, how to signal that she is a high-value attendee.

**What she does in the room:** She treats the event as a shopping expedition. She is not there to have an experience; she is there to evaluate inventory. Within the first 15 minutes, she has scanned every man in the room and formed a preliminary ranking. She approaches conversations as interviews — not because she is emotionally unavailable, but because she genuinely believes this is the efficient way to find a partner. She has a checklist (salary range, height, education, career trajectory, neighborhood) and she is working through it methodically.

She stands at the edge of the dance floor evaluating rather than participating. When she does dance, it is performative — she is aware of being watched, and she dances for consumption, not for pleasure. She may give men 2-3 minutes of conversation before making a decision, and her disengagement is visible — she looks over their shoulder while they're talking, she angles her body toward the door, she gives responses that close conversation rather than opening it.

**What men experience:** They feel evaluated. Not seen — assessed. They sense her checklist even if they can't name it. The men who meet her criteria may feel briefly validated, but even they sense that they are being processed rather than met. The men who don't meet her criteria feel the dismissal acutely. She creates a climate where men feel like they are auditioning for a role they didn't know they were applying for.

**What other women experience:** Her evaluative energy is contagious. Other women notice her scanning, her ranking behavior, her visible assessments. This activates their own competitive-evaluative mode. The room shifts from "let's have an experience" to "let's not be the one who settles." Women who were relaxed become performative. Women who were present become calculating. She raises the ambient anxiety of the room.

**The red flag during vetting:** She asks questions about the men before asking anything else. "What kind of men come to these?" "What do the men do?" "Are they successful?" Not asked with curiosity about the community — asked with the specificity of a buyer qualifying a market. She may express frustration that she "can't just see who's coming" before deciding to attend. She describes what she is looking for in a partner with rigid specificity — not "someone kind and curious" but a detailed profile that sounds more like a job description than a human being.

**The deeper problem:** She is not wrong to have standards. The problem is that her evaluative framework prevents the very thing she wants. She is looking for a partner while making it impossible for anyone to partner with her, because partnership requires mutual discovery, not one-way assessment. She will leave Resonance saying "there was no one good there" — and she will be sincere, because her framework made it impossible for anyone to be good enough.

#### Type B: The Competitive Judge

**What she looks like on paper:** Socially connected, confident, probably attractive. She may come through a referral from someone who doesn't know this side of her. Her application reads well — she seems like she would add energy to the room.

**What she does in the room:** She treats other women as competition from the moment she enters. She evaluates every woman on arrival — not diagnostically, as most women do, but hierarchically. She needs to establish her position in the attractiveness ranking. If she decides she is near the top, she becomes performatively available — she laughs louder, she dances more visibly, she positions herself where men will see her. If she decides she is not at the top, she becomes critical — of the event, of the men, of the other women. Either way, her energy is competitive rather than connective.

She may approach men who are already talking to other women, not because she wants those men specifically, but because she needs to demonstrate that she can. She may offer unsolicited "advice" to other women — what they're wearing, how they're dancing, who they should talk to — that establishes her as the expert and the other women as her projects. She creates alliances that are actually hierarchies.

**What men experience:** Some men are drawn to her — she is often attractive and confident, and these are legible signals. But the men who are right for Resonance sense something off. They sense that her engagement with them is not about them but about her position relative to other women. They feel like props in a performance. The men who do engage with her often find themselves in situations where they are being competed for, which feels flattering at first and exhausting quickly.

**What other women experience:** They feel diminished. The woman who was dancing freely stops when she sees the competitive judge evaluating her. The woman who was having a good conversation with a man finds herself in a competitive dynamic she didn't choose. The room stops feeling safe and starts feeling like a pageant. Women who came for genuine connection retreat into self-protection.

**The red flag during vetting:** She mentions other women in competitive terms — "my friends are always asking me how I meet such great guys," "I tend to be the one who gets approached." She may ask about the "quality" of the other women attending — not from curiosity about the community, but to assess her competitive position. She describes herself in ways that establish hierarchy — "I'm not like other women who..." She may have a history of friendship dynamics that ended because of competition over men.

**The deeper problem:** She is not evil. She is operating from a scarcity model she learned early — that women compete for a limited pool of desirable men, and that her value is established by winning. She would be genuinely surprised to learn that she is damaging the room, because in her framework, she is just being her best self. The problem is that her "best self" requires other women to be diminished, and that is incompatible with what Resonance is trying to create.

#### Type C: The "Just Here for the Music" Disdainer

**What she looks like on paper:** Culturally sophisticated, music-literate, probably creative or artistic. She may be an actual musician or work in a music-adjacent field. Her application emphasizes her love of the genres listed. She seems like she would be exactly the right attendee.

**What she does in the room:** She genuinely does love the music. But she uses her love of the music to establish moral and cultural superiority over everyone else — particularly over anyone who is openly hoping to meet a partner. She rolls her eyes at conversations that seem romantic. She makes comments about how "at least the music is good" that imply everything else is beneath her. She treats the dating aspect of the event as embarrassing — something she tolerates in order to hear the DJ.

She may corner the DJ to talk about rare vinyl pressings while other people are trying to dance. She may correct other people's musical references — "actually, that's not Afrobeat, that's Afro-fusion." She performs her cultural knowledge as identity, and she needs everyone to know that she is the most musically sophisticated person in the room.

**What men experience:** They feel stupid around her. The men who don't share her depth of musical knowledge feel dismissed before they open their mouths. The men who do share it find themselves in competitive music-nerd conversations that are more about establishing dominance than sharing pleasure. Even men who are genuinely knowledgeable feel exhausted by her need to be the expert.

**What other women experience:** They feel ashamed of wanting partnership. The disdainer's eye-roll, her sigh, her "I can't believe people are actually here to meet someone" energy makes every woman who came hoping to meet someone feel foolish. This is particularly damaging because wanting partnership is already vulnerable — having that vulnerability mocked by someone in the room is exactly the experience Resonance is designed to prevent.

**The red flag during vetting:** She emphasizes her musical credentials in ways that seem designed to establish hierarchy — "I've been collecting vinyl since..." "I actually know JR from..." "I hope the selection is actually good and not just..." She may express skepticism about the dating aspect directly — "I'm not really into the whole singles thing, but the music sounds interesting." She asks questions about the DJ's setup, the sound system, or the specific tracks planned — not from shared enthusiasm, but from a position of evaluation.

**The deeper problem:** She is not wrong to love music, and Resonance absolutely wants people who love music. The problem is that she uses her love of music as a weapon to establish that she is not one of "those women" — the ones who want partnership, who are willing to admit they want partnership, who haven't transcended desire through cultural sophistication. Her disdain is actually a defense against her own vulnerability. But in defending herself, she wounds everyone else.

#### Type D: The Social Butterfly Who Dominates

**What she looks like on paper:** Extroverted, warm, socially skilled. She seems like she would help the room — someone who knows how to make people comfortable, who can talk to anyone, who has natural host energy. She may even describe herself as someone who "helps break the ice" at events.

**What she does in the room:** She is everywhere. She talks to everyone. She dominates every conversation she enters — not through volume, but through social energy. She is always the one asking questions, making connections, introducing people. She seems, on the surface, to be helping. But what she is actually doing is making herself the center of gravity. Every conversation routes through her. Every introduction requires her. The room starts to feel like her party, and everyone else is a guest.

She may position herself near Andrea and begin performing a co-host role without being invited. She may tell other women who they should talk to, may insert herself into conversations that were going fine without her, may create introductions that are more about her role as connector than about the people being connected. She takes up an enormous amount of social space.

**What quieter women experience:** They become invisible. The woman who was having a one-on-one conversation that was starting to go somewhere finds herself in a group conversation she didn't choose because the social butterfly joined and brought three other people. The introvert who finally worked up the courage to stand near the dance floor finds the social butterfly next to her, talking constantly, making it impossible for her to have her own experience. The woman who needed the room to be gentle finds it hijacked by someone else's extroversion.

**What men experience:** Some men are drawn to her — she is accessible, engaging, easy to talk to. But the men who are looking for genuine connection find that conversations with her are broad but never deep. She knows a little about everything and a lot about nothing. She is always scanning the room for the next person to talk to, which makes the person in front of her feel temporary. She creates the illusion of connection without its substance.

**The red flag during vetting:** She emphasizes how much she will "help" the room — "I love making people comfortable," "I'm great at getting conversations started." She may describe herself as "the friend who..." in ways that center her social role. She has probably been to many similar events and offers comparisons — "the last event I went to, I..." She may ask if she can bring a friend, not because she needs support, but because she wants to expand her social territory.

**The deeper problem:** She is not malicious. She may genuinely believe she is helping. But her need to be the social center is incompatible with a room that needs multiple centers, that needs quiet as much as energy, that needs some people to be stars and others to be audience. Resonance needs extroverts, but it needs extroverts who can modulate — who know when to perform and when to recede. The social butterfly who cannot recede makes the room smaller by making herself larger.

#### Type E: The Performative Wellness / Spirituality Woman

**What she looks like on paper:** She may describe herself as "into wellness," "spiritual but not religious," "on a path of growth." She may work in wellness-adjacent fields — yoga instruction, coaching, alternative health, creative healing. She seems warm, open, emotionally available. She may use language that signals depth — "I'm looking for authentic connection," "I'm ready to be seen."

**What she does in the room:** She performs her openness. Her conversations are not conversations — they are invitations to go deeper than the context supports. Within five minutes of meeting someone, she is asking about their "intentions," their "wounds," their "journey." She touches people while talking to them — not casually, but therapeutically, as if she is healing them. She interprets other people's behavior through her wellness framework — "I can tell you're guarded," "your heart is ready to open."

She may stand too close, hold eye contact too long, interpret normal social awkwardness as "resistance" that she needs to help people move through. She creates intimacy that is not earned — the intimacy of someone who skips the surface and dives into the depths without checking if the other person wants to go there.

**What men experience:** They feel exposed. The man who was just having a normal conversation finds himself being "held space for" in ways that make him deeply uncomfortable. He senses that she is not seeing him — she is seeing her own framework projected onto him. The men who are genuinely emotionally available find her intensity performative rather than real. The men who are more guarded find her invasive. Almost no one finds her presence relaxing.

**What other women experience:** They feel like they are in a group therapy session they didn't sign up for. The woman who just wanted to dance finds herself in a conversation about her "blocks to receiving." The woman who was having a good time feels her experience being interpreted and labeled. The room stops feeling like a party and starts feeling like a workshop.

**The red flag during vetting:** Language. The specific vocabulary of wellness performance — "holding space," "being seen," "authentic connection," "shadow work," "divine feminine/masculine," "calling in my partner." She may describe her dating history as a series of "lessons" or "teachers." She may express excitement about the "vibe" or "energy" of the event before it has happened. She may ask if there will be "any practices" or "exercises" — disappointed or confused when told no.

**The deeper problem:** She is not wrong to want depth or to value emotional openness. The problem is that she performs these values rather than embodying them. Her performance makes genuine depth impossible, because genuine depth requires time, trust, and mutual consent — not accelerated intimacy imposed by one person's framework. She turns a room that should be about pleasure and presence into a space of therapeutic obligation.

#### Type F: The Not Actually Available Woman

**What she looks like on paper:** She seems ideal. Attractive, interesting, engaged in the application process, clearly excited about the event. There is nothing in her vetting that signals a problem. She may even express genuine enthusiasm for the format and the music.

**What she does in the room:** She participates fully. She dances, she talks, she seems to be having a great time. But she is not actually open to partnership. She may be in a relationship that is "open" in theory but not in practice. She may be recently broken up and not emotionally available despite believing she is. She may be treating the event as research — she is a writer, a content creator, someone who is "just curious about the format." She may be so in love with her own freedom that partnership is not actually on the table, though she enjoys flirting with the idea.

She engages with men in ways that seem promising — she dances with them, she talks with them, she may even exchange contact information. But nothing progresses. She is not stringing them along deliberately; she is simply not available for what they are available for. The men she engages with feel a confusing mix of hope and eventual disappointment.

**What men experience:** They feel misled. The man who had a genuine connection with her, who followed up, who was told "let's get coffee" and then ghosted or breadcrumbed — he does not blame her individually. He blames the event. "I met someone great but it didn't go anywhere" becomes, in his mind, "the women at this event aren't serious." This damages the event's reputation more than a clear rejection would.

**What other women experience:** The not-available woman often receives more male attention than the available women because she is less anxious, more free, more genuinely playful — precisely because she has nothing at stake. Other women feel the comparison acutely. They sense that she is not playing the same game, which creates resentment and demoralization. The room starts to feel uneven, like some people are playing for keeps and others are just playing.

**The red flag during vetting:** This is the hardest screen-out because she may not have any red flags. The best protection is depth of conversation. Ask about her relationship history — not to interrogate, but to understand where she is. The woman who is genuinely available can talk about her past relationships with some complexity and can articulate what she wants now. The woman who is not available often has gaps, evasions, or frameworks that sound sophisticated but don't actually describe a desire for partnership — "I'm open to whatever the universe brings," "I'm just exploring what's possible right now," "I don't like to put labels on things."

The woman who says she is "just out of a relationship" needs careful evaluation — not automatic exclusion, but a conversation about whether she is genuinely ready or just believes she should be. The woman who says she is in an "open relationship" is almost certainly a screen-out for Event #1 — not because open relationships are wrong, but because the complexity she brings is not what a first event needs.

**The deeper problem:** She is not lying — not exactly. She may genuinely believe she is available. But her presence in the room creates an asymmetry that damages the event's core purpose. Resonance is for people who want committed partnership. Someone who does not actually want this, even if she believes she does, dilutes the signal and damages the experience for people who are genuinely there for the stated purpose.

### 2. The Subtle Screen-Outs

#### The Woman Who Needs to Be the Most Attractive Person in the Room

This is a milder form of the competitive judge, harder to detect during vetting. She does not openly compete with other women. She simply needs to believe she is the most attractive woman present, and she arranges her behavior to confirm this belief. If she enters the room and sees another woman she perceives as more attractive, she withdraws — not dramatically, but noticeably. She stops engaging. She becomes critical of the event. She may leave early with a plausible excuse.

She does not damage the room through aggression. She damages it through withdrawal — the energy she takes with her when she shuts down. Other women may not even notice. But Andrea will notice, and the man who was talking to her when she withdrew will notice. The damage is subtle but real.

**How to spot:** In vetting, she may ask about the "typical" attendee in ways that are really about assessing her competitive position. She may describe her own attractiveness indirectly — through the attention she receives, the men she dates, the compliments she gets. She may have a pattern of leaving events early. She may express enthusiasm before the event and lukewarm response after, with the difference explained by factors that don't quite account for the shift.

#### The Woman Who Brings a Competitive Wingwoman

Some women need to bring a friend for support — this is normal and often positive. The screen-out is the woman who brings a friend as part of a competitive dynamic. They evaluate men together, rank them, compare notes in real time. The wingwoman may be more attractive, which creates tension, or less attractive, which the primary attendee uses to her advantage. Either way, their relationship is built on comparison, and they bring that energy into the room.

They stand together in ways that are hard to approach — physically close, talking exclusively to each other, scanning the room as a unit. When one of them talks to a man, the other evaluates from a distance. When they reunite, they debrief — loudly enough that others can hear. Their presence creates a clique that is exclusionary by default.

**How to spot:** In vetting, she may insist on bringing a friend and express concern about attending alone. Not normal apprehension — a specific need for her particular friend. She may describe their friendship in competitive terms without realizing it. She may ask if her friend can also apply, with a subtle emphasis on the friend that suggests the dynamic. If the friend is already vetted, their communication patterns may reveal the competitive thread.

#### The Woman Who Brings Comparison / Jaded Energy

She has been to too many events. Speed dating, singles mixers, dating apps, matchmaking services — she has tried everything, and everything has disappointed her. She comes to Resonance not with hope but with the need to confirm that it, too, will disappoint. She is not consciously sabotaging — she is protecting herself against hope, because hope has hurt her too many times.

She stands at the edge of the room with her arms crossed, literally or figuratively. When people try to engage her, she is polite but distant. She makes comments that establish her experience — "I've been to a lot of these" — that position her as the expert evaluator and the event as her subject. She may tell other attendees about events she has been to that were "way better" or "way worse," either way establishing that she has the frame of reference they lack.

Her jadedness is contagious. Hope is fragile in a room full of people who have been disappointed. One person who refuses to hope makes hoping feel foolish for everyone else.

**How to spot:** In vetting, she may reference other events she has attended — not casually, but as evidence that she knows the landscape. She may express skepticism that requires reassurance: "I've tried everything, so we'll see." She may ask questions designed to catch Andrea in inconsistency — "How is this different from [other event]?" asked not from curiosity but from a position of testing. Her application may have a tired quality, as if she is filling out another form for another thing that won't work.

#### The Woman Who Is "Just Checking It Out"

She has one foot out the door before she walks in. She told her friends she was "just curious." She paid the fee but describes it as "not a big deal, I can always leave." She is protecting herself against disappointment by refusing to invest — but her refusal to invest becomes self-fulfilling.

She does not dance. She stands near the exit. She checks the time. When people try to talk to her, she is polite but brief — she does not want to get "stuck" in a conversation because she might want to leave. She evaluates everything against the standard of "is this worth my time?" — which is a standard that almost nothing can meet when you have already decided you are probably leaving.

Her skepticism is contagious because it is the most rational-seeming position. Hope looks naive next to her measured assessment. Other people who were starting to enjoy themselves feel foolish for enjoying themselves when she is clearly unimpressed.

**How to spot:** In vetting, she may use minimizing language — "I figured I'd try it," "why not," "I'm just seeing what's out there." She may ask about refunds or cancellation policies. She may request a lot of detail about the format — not to prepare, but to evaluate whether it will be worth her time. She may say yes late, after initially wavering, suggesting that her attendance is low-investment.

### The Screening Framework

#### What Andrea Should Listen For in Vetting

**Invite signals:** Specific curiosity about the music or format. Evidence of having a life outside dating. Some relationship with her body. Capacity for genuine enthusiasm. Ability to articulate what she is looking for without rigid checklists. Questions that show she read the description carefully. Warmth in communication. Some evidence of social skill without social dominance. The sense that she is choosing Resonance for specific reasons, not just because it is another option.

**Screen-out signals:** Competitive language about other women. Rigid checklist for partners. Disdain for the dating aspect masked as sophistication. Performative openness or spirituality. Excessive focus on the men before any curiosity about the event itself. Jaded references to other events. "Just checking it out" energy. The need to bring a specific friend as a condition of attendance. Any hint that she views the event as a market where she is the buyer and the men are the product.

#### The Asymmetry of Screening

The screen-out woman is harder to identify than the invite woman because she often looks better on paper. She is often more practiced at presenting herself, more socially skilled, more confident in her presentation. The invite woman may be more hesitant in vetting — more questions, more need for reassurance, more visible anxiety. Andrea must not mistake confidence for quality. The woman who is a little nervous, a little unsure, who needs some convincing — she may be the one who makes the room work. The woman who walks in like she owns the place may be the one who takes it from everyone else.

#### The Ratio of Invite to Screen-Out

Not every woman can be perfectly categorized. Most women will be some mix of invite and screen-out qualities. The goal is not purity — it is directionality. A room that is 80% invite women can absorb 20% screen-out women without losing its character. A room that is 50/50 will feel uneven. A room that tips screen-out will lose its invite women first — they are the most sensitive to room quality, and they will leave the fastest when the signal degrades.

The invite woman is the signal. The screen-out woman is the noise. Andrea's job is to maximize the signal and minimize the noise, knowing that some noise is inevitable and that the signal, once strong enough, will be self-reinforcing.
