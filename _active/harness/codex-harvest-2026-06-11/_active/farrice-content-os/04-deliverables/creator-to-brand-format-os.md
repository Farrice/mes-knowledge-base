## The Core Model

Every card in this OS should produce four things:

1. **A friction transfer:** What burden does the creator take off the viewer?
2. **A desire setup:** What does the viewer want before the product, method, or idea appears?
3. **A proof event:** What can be tested, contrasted, shown, or demonstrated?
4. **A business bridge:** How does this become a post, brief, offer asset, or campaign?

If a format cannot answer those four questions, it is decoration.

## The 12 Format Cards

### 1. Guinea Pig Content

**Use when:** The audience is curious but skeptical, and the creator can test something so the viewer does not have to risk money, time, or embarrassment.

**Source mechanic:** The speaker names guinea pig content around `00:00:53`, then frames the creator as the person who test-drives the product around `00:01:24`. Around `00:03:38`, the speaker says the format creates trust.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Product, workflow, method, or belief to test.
- Time period or test condition.
- Before state and after state.
- Honest result, including the caveat.

**Hook patterns:**

- "I tested [tool/process/product] for [time] so you do not have to."
- "Everyone keeps talking about [thing], so I ran the uncomfortable version."
- "I wanted to know if [claim] actually holds up."

**Structure:**

1. Name the popular claim or hype.
2. State the test and the stakes.
3. Show the time anchor.
4. Reveal what changed.
5. Say who should use it and who should skip it.

**Farrice use:** Test AI content workflows, prompt packs, no-code automations, content calendars, or AI image/video tools and show what actually gets better.

**Brand/business translation:** Give creators a controlled brief: test the product in a real scenario, document the before/after, and do not jump straight to the pitch.

**Monetization use:** Lead into a paid audit by showing the moment where the tool worked only after the process around it was fixed.

**Quality gate:** If the test has no visible or measurable before/after, do not use this format.

**Prompt:** "Turn this tool, workflow, or product into a Guinea Pig Content card. Define the risky claim, the test, the time anchor, the proof event, the honest caveat, and the business bridge."

### 2. Storytime Proof

**Use when:** The audience needs to feel seen before they will trust the solution.

**Source mechanic:** The source introduces storytime at `00:05:28`, then distinguishes it from a flat testimonial. Around `00:06:13`, it adds the failed-solution layer. Around `00:07:32`, it ties story to relatable problem and trust.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Lived moment or customer moment.
- Specific problem.
- What was tried first.
- Why it failed.
- How the real solution appeared.

**Hook patterns:**

- "I did not realize [problem] was really [deeper problem] until [moment]."
- "The first fix made it worse."
- "This is the part of the client story most case studies skip."

**Structure:**

1. Open with a concrete moment.
2. Name the problem in human language.
3. Show the failed attempt.
4. Explain the hidden reason it failed.
5. Introduce the solution as earned, not magical.

**Farrice use:** Tell stories about AI work that looked like a prompt problem but was actually a taste, context, or workflow problem.

**Brand/business translation:** Replace generic testimonials with stories that show the old belief, failed fix, hidden constraint, and new operating method.

**Monetization use:** Strong entry point for case-study posts, audit sales pages, and founder interviews.

**Quality gate:** If the story starts with the product, rewrite it. The audience has to care before the solution appears.

**Prompt:** "Turn this customer, founder, or personal moment into Storytime Proof. Include the relatable problem, failed solution, hidden mechanism, earned solution, and one line that makes the audience feel recognized."

### 3. Ungatekeeping

**Use when:** The audience believes the real answer has been hidden, oversimplified, or made needlessly inaccessible.

**Source mechanic:** The source names ungatekeeping at `00:09:13`, says it works because people feel misled around `00:09:56`, warns not to go straight into the product around `00:10:44`, and shows the guilt-relief mechanic around `00:14:42`.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- The secret, missing step, or overlooked constraint.
- What people have been told instead.
- Why that advice fails.
- The practical reveal.

**Hook patterns:**

- "I cannot keep watching people blame [surface problem]."
- "The thing nobody explains about [desired result] is [hidden mechanism]."
- "You were not bad at this. You were missing [specific constraint]."

**Structure:**

1. Name the thing people feel locked out of.
2. Validate the frustration.
3. Reveal what is actually happening.
4. Give the practical method.
5. Show how to apply it now.

**Farrice use:** Explain why AI content sounds generic, why prompts fail, why content systems stall, and why teams keep rescuing AI outputs manually.

**Brand/business translation:** Use this for educational products, services, audits, SaaS onboarding, and category education where the buyer needs a better mental model.

**Monetization use:** Use as a lead-in to AI Slop Cleanup, AI Misfire Audit, or a workshop.

**Quality gate:** Do not fake secrecy. The reveal must be specific enough that the viewer can immediately use or recognize it.

**Prompt:** "Create an Ungatekeeping card from this source. Identify what people have been denied, what wrong explanation they were given, the hidden mechanism, the practical reveal, and the first action."

### 4. Competitor Callout / Trust Jacking

**Use when:** A familiar incumbent, category norm, or default method can be contrasted fairly with a better alternative.

**Source mechanic:** The source introduces competitor callout at `00:15:33`, emphasizes demonstrated effectiveness around `00:16:44`, and describes trust jacking around `00:17:58`.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Known incumbent or category belief.
- Fair comparison criterion.
- Demonstrable contrast.
- Alternative method or product.
- Risk note for naming, fairness, and platform policy.

**Hook patterns:**

- "[Known option] vs [overlooked option]: I tested the part people never compare."
- "The popular solution wins attention. The better solution wins [result]."
- "Everyone trusts [category], but the actual proof says [specific contrast]."

**Structure:**

1. Name the familiar default or category.
2. Define the comparison fairly.
3. Demonstrate the contrast.
4. Transfer trust to the better mechanism.
5. Give a buyer-safe recommendation.

**Farrice use:** Brandjack recognizable companies or AI tools by studying their mechanics, not copying or attacking them.

**Brand/business translation:** Build creative briefs around "old way vs new way," "default tool vs actual workflow," or "category promise vs proof."

**Monetization use:** Strong for competitive audits, teardown posts, sales enablement, and client education.

**Quality gate:** Avoid defamatory claims. If the comparison is not fair, specific, and evidence-backed, turn it into a category contrast instead of a named competitor callout.

**Prompt:** "Turn this comparison into a Competitor Callout card. Define the recognizable default, fair test, contrast proof, trust transfer, buyer-safe language, and risk note."

### 5. Failed-Solution Prize

**Use when:** The audience keeps blaming themselves for a failure that was created by bad information, missing context, or the wrong mechanism.

**Source mechanic:** Around `00:14:28`, the source says the creator can relieve the viewer of guilt by showing they did not have the right information.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Common failure.
- Common advice.
- Why that advice fails.
- The missing mechanism.
- Relief statement.

**Hook patterns:**

- "It is not your fault [thing] keeps failing."
- "The obvious fix failed because it was solving the wrong layer."
- "You were told to improve [surface]. The real issue was [system]."

**Structure:**

1. Name the painful failure.
2. Remove blame.
3. Diagnose the false solution.
4. Reveal the better mechanism.
5. Give a next step.

**Farrice use:** "It is not your prompt. It is the missing context layer." "It is not your content calendar. It is the absence of a point of view."

**Brand/business translation:** Use in B2B education, onboarding, objection handling, and transformation content.

**Monetization use:** Perfect for audit CTAs because it creates relief before asking for action.

**Quality gate:** The relief has to be true. Do not absolve the viewer if the real issue is effort, fit, or execution.

**Prompt:** "Convert this pain point into a Failed-Solution Prize. Identify the false fix, the guilt relief, the real mechanism, and the paid or practical next step."

### 6. Before/After Proof Trial

**Use when:** The result can be shown through visible contrast, output comparison, or process delta.

**Source mechanic:** The source repeatedly returns to time anchors and visible contrast in guinea pig and competitor formats, especially `00:01:43` through `00:03:06` and `00:16:19` through `00:16:41`.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Before artifact.
- After artifact.
- Change made.
- Evaluation criterion.
- Caveat or constraint.

**Hook patterns:**

- "Before I changed [mechanism], this output looked fine but did not work."
- "Same idea, different system. Here is the difference."
- "This is what changed after I removed [bad assumption]."

**Structure:**

1. Show the before state.
2. Name the hidden flaw.
3. Apply one change.
4. Show the after state.
5. Explain the business result.

**Farrice use:** AI slop before/after, agent-readable workflow before/after, rough brand thought before/after.

**Brand/business translation:** Sales enablement, creative testing, SaaS onboarding, training content, and proof demos.

**Monetization use:** Turns abstract expertise into visible proof for paid audits and workshops.

**Quality gate:** The difference must be obvious without a lecture.

**Prompt:** "Build a Before/After Proof Trial from these two artifacts. Name the hidden flaw, the intervention, the visible difference, and the buyer meaning."

### 7. Creator Routine Lab

**Use when:** The audience wants the repeatable behind-the-scenes system, not just the result.

**Source mechanic:** Around `00:18:10`, the source says creators and brands should introduce the four formats into the routine, then rotate hooks and executions.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Repeatable routine.
- Daily or weekly cadence.
- Inputs.
- Output types.
- Feedback signal.

**Hook patterns:**

- "The routine behind this output is less glamorous and more useful."
- "Here is the weekly loop I would run if I needed [result]."
- "Most people post ideas. This is the system underneath the ideas."

**Structure:**

1. State the desired output.
2. Show the recurring inputs.
3. Map the weekly loop.
4. Add feedback metrics.
5. Give the first run.

**Farrice use:** Content OS routines, prompt-to-post loops, client audit sprints, daily idea capture, brandjack research.

**Brand/business translation:** Internal team SOPs, creator brief systems, content sprint operating docs.

**Monetization use:** Converts expertise into a productized service, workshop, or team training.

**Quality gate:** A routine without a feedback signal is just a checklist.

**Prompt:** "Turn this result into a Creator Routine Lab card. Define the cadence, inputs, format mix, feedback signal, and first sprint."

### 8. Format-Market Transplant

**Use when:** A proven format from one market can make a saturated or boring market feel fresh.

**Borrowed expert mechanic:** Tim Danilov's niche-bending method separates market from format. The market is what the content is about; the format is how attention is earned.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Source format.
- Original market.
- Target market.
- Shared psychological mechanism.
- Native examples and vocabulary.

**Hook patterns:**

- "What if [market] used the format that made [other market] addictive?"
- "This format is not about [source niche]. It is about [psychological mechanism]."
- "The empty square is [format] applied to [market]."

**Structure:**

1. Extract the format skeleton.
2. Strip away source-market details.
3. Keep the psychology.
4. Refill with target-market expertise.
5. Translate the visual and verbal language.

**Farrice use:** Move creator commerce formats into AI consulting, content strategy, local business, founder education, or client delivery.

**Brand/business translation:** Useful for saturated categories where topic innovation is hard but format innovation is still open.

**Monetization use:** High-value strategic audit: "Your market is crowded, but your format library is empty."

**Quality gate:** If the target market lacks a real audience pain or desire, the transplant is novelty only.

**Prompt:** "Use Format-Market Transplant on this source. Identify the proven format, the market it came from, the target market, the portable mechanism, and the new content brief."

### 9. AI Slop Cleanup

**Use when:** A piece of AI-generated content is technically correct but emotionally empty, generic, or untrustworthy.

**Farrice mechanic:** This card turns weak AI output into a creator-style trust asset using source fidelity, voice, lived tension, and proof.

**Raw inputs needed:**

- Bad AI draft or bland output.
- Intended audience.
- Business goal.
- Missing context.
- Proof or lived example.

**Hook patterns:**

- "This is why your AI content sounds expensive and empty."
- "The draft was not wrong. It was unowned."
- "AI did not ruin the idea. It exposed the missing judgment."

**Structure:**

1. Show the bland output or summarize its flaw.
2. Diagnose the missing human layer.
3. Add specific context, tension, and proof.
4. Rewrite through one source format.
5. Add buyer-relevant CTA.

**Brand/business translation:** Service offer, audit demo, before/after proof, workshop exercise.

**Monetization use:** Direct bridge into AI Slop Cleanup, AI Misfire Audit, or an agent-ready workflow sprint.

**Quality gate:** Do not merely make the draft sound cooler. It must become clearer, more specific, and more useful.

**Prompt:** "Run AI Slop Cleanup on this draft. Diagnose the missing judgment, choose the best creator format, add proof, rewrite the content argument, and state the buyer signal."

**Use when:** A source insight should become a campaign-ready creative brief for creators, UGC, paid ads, or organic content.

**Client-facing mechanic:** Convert strategy into creator-native execution without losing brand safety or business intent.

## Operating Loop

1. **Capture:** Save source, thought, interview, draft, trend, or client issue.
2. **Ground:** Separate observed source evidence from inferred mechanics.
3. **Choose trust job:** Risk transfer, identification, insider reveal, trust transfer, relief, proof, routine, or transplant.
4. **Select card:** Pick one primary format and one optional modifier.
5. **Translate:** Adapt to Farrice voice, client voice, or brand voice.
6. **Produce:** Draft post, script, creative brief, workshop section, or offer asset.
7. **Gate:** Run source, voice, buyer, format, specificity, trust, AI ethics, and reuse checks.
8. **Save:** Add the reusable mechanic to the content OS bank.
