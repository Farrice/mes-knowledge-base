## ICP SCOPE DECISION

**Your ICP is NOT "coaches, consultants, and founders."**

That's three different people with three different problems. Here's the actual scope:

### Primary ICP: The Expert-Practitioner
**Coaches, consultants, and course creators who are genuinely excellent at their craft but invisible online.**

This person:
- Has real expertise (5-20+ years)
- Gets results for clients through 1:1 or group work
- Knows LinkedIn would grow their business
- Has tried and stalled, quit, or never started
- Feels a specific kind of shame about this gap

### Who Is NOT Your ICP (Right Now):
| Not Your ICP | Why |
|--------------|-----|
| Brand-new coaches with no track record | They need coaching skills, not content strategy |
| Corporate executives building thought leadership | Different pain, different price point, different language |
| E-commerce founders | They need performance marketing, not ghostwriting |
| Coaches already posting 3-5x/week | They need optimization, not rescue |

**Your sweet spot:** The person who has 5+ years of real-world expertise, a roster of past clients who'd vouch for them, and a LinkedIn profile that looks like it was last updated in 2019.

## THE CONSUMER POSTURE PROFILE: MARCUS

> *Not a demographic. Not a persona. A specific human being.*

### The Individual

His name is Marcus. He's 41. He runs a leadership coaching practice that makes $150K-$250K mostly from referrals and one corporate contract he got through a friend. He's a former operations manager who became a coach after a burnout episode he doesn't talk about publicly but which is the real engine of everything he does.

He wears clean sneakers. His office has exactly one bookshelf, organized by color because his wife did it and he hasn't reorganized since. He drinks black coffee until noon, then switches to water because a client once told him his afternoon energy dropped.

Marcus is the kind of person who remembers details about people. He'll reference something you said three months ago in a way that makes you feel genuinely seen. Clients describe him as "the person who finally understood what I was actually saying."

He is not on LinkedIn. Or rather — he HAS a LinkedIn. It has a photo from 4 years ago, a headline that says "Leadership Coach | Helping Leaders Lead," and an About section that starts with "Experienced leadership coach with a passion for helping..."

He hates that About section. He wrote it in 12 minutes while eating lunch and has never gone back because every time he opens it, he feels a low hum of inadequacy that he can't explain.

### Occupation in Farrice's World

Marcus doesn't think he needs "content help." He thinks he needs to "finally figure out LinkedIn." There's a difference. The first implies he'd hire someone. The second implies it's a personal failing he should solve himself.

This is critical. **He sees his inability to post on LinkedIn as a character flaw, not a skills gap.**

He knows coaches with half his experience and a fraction of his results who are posting daily, getting engagement, landing speaking gigs. He tells himself "they're just better at marketing" — but what he actually feels is a quiet, simmering anger that the game rewards performance over substance.

He has tried:
- Writing posts himself → abandoned after 4 posts that got 2 likes each (one was his wife)
- Watching YouTube videos on "LinkedIn growth" → felt overwhelmed and slightly disgusted
- Using ChatGPT to write posts → read the output, felt his stomach clench, thought *"this sounds like everyone else"*
- Considering hiring a ghostwriter → looked at prices, felt it was "an expense, not an investment," closed the tab

### Activity in Farrice's World

**What Marcus does:**
- Scrolls LinkedIn for 15 minutes in the morning, reads posts from coaches who are "killing it," feels a mix of admiration and resentment, then closes the app
- Periodically googles "LinkedIn for coaches" and opens 3 tabs he never reads
- Keeps a note on his phone titled "Post Ideas" with 7 bullet points, none longer than 5 words, the oldest from 11 months ago
- Once every 2-3 months, sits down on a Sunday to "finally write some content," writes for 45 minutes, reads it back, hates it, saves it as a draft, never publishes
- Occasionally sends a DM to congratulate someone on a milestone, wishing he had something to promote too
- Refers business through word-of-mouth and waits for the phone to ring

**What Marcus refuses to do:**
- Post anything that feels like "bragging"
- Use hashtags (finds them performative)
- Write in a way that sounds like "a marketing person"
- Share client testimonials without being asked (feels like exploitation)
- "Be vulnerable on main" — he's seen the performative vulnerability posts and finds them manipulative

### Thought Process in Farrice's World

Marcus has a very specific internal logic:

**What feels authentic:** Sharing genuine insights from coaching sessions (without naming clients). Having real conversations. Writing something that would make a colleague say "that's actually useful."

**What feels inauthentic:** Growth hacking. Engagement pods. Hook formulas. Anything that smells like "playing the game." Content that exists to get likes rather than to help someone.

**The paradox that traps him:** He respects depth but the platform rewards performance. He values substance but can't figure out how to make substance visible. He's excellent at articulating his clients' problems — but when he turns that lens on himself, everything goes flat.

**His private belief (the one he'd never say out loud):** "If my work was really as good as I think it is, people would find me. The fact that I have to promote myself means maybe I'm not as good as I think."

**The deeper belief underneath that one:** "Marketing is for people who need to compensate for mediocrity. I shouldn't need it."

**The belief under THAT one:** "But I do need it. And that makes me feel like a fraud."

### Posture Synthesis

Marcus sits up straight in your brand's world. He doesn't lean in eagerly or lean back skeptically — he's **guarded but curious**. He wants what Farrice offers but will never ask for it directly. He'll frame his interest as "exploring options" or "just looking into this" because admitting he needs help with something as basic as "writing posts" feels like a humiliation.

His posture is: **"Convince me without selling me."**

He'll respond to someone who demonstrates understanding of his world — not his "pain points" (he'd reject that framing as manipulative), but his actual experience. If Farrice can describe what it's like to sit in that Sunday afternoon draft session and feel the gap between what you KNOW and what you can WRITE — Marcus will stop scrolling.

## STRATEGIC IMPLICATIONS

### For Farrice's Content Strategy
1. **Never say "pain points."** Marcus hates marketing language. Use "the thing you keep bumping into" or "that gap" instead.
2. **Never promise "virality" or "growth."** Promise clarity, voice, and the feeling of "that actually sounds like me."
3. **Lead with recognition, not solution.** The first 3 sentences of every post should describe Marcus's experience so accurately he screenshots it.
4. **Use humor carefully.** Marcus will respond to dry, self-aware humor about the absurdity of content marketing. He'll recoil from sarcasm about his industry.
5. **The "translate" metaphor is your moat.** You're not a ghostwriter. You're a translator. You translate depth into format. This metaphor sticks because it's exactly what Marcus can't do.

### For Outreach
1. **Never cold DM with "I noticed you're not posting consistently."** That's a judgment, not a connection. Marcus will block you.
2. **Instead**: Reference something specific about their expertise, then describe the gap you solve — without naming it as a gap.
3. **The qualification question**: "Do you ever have a great coaching session and think 'I should post about this' — and then just don't?" If they say yes, they're your client.

### For Pricing
1. Marcus will pay $1,500-2,500/month IF he believes you understand his craft. He won't pay $500 for "LinkedIn posts." The difference is entirely positioning, not deliverable.
2. Frame the investment as: "You spend 40+ hours a month coaching. I spend 8-10 hours making sure the internet knows you're as good as your clients already know."
