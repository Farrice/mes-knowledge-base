# 04 LinkedIn Publish Sprint And Current Drafts

## Role Of LinkedIn

LinkedIn is the conversion spine.

Instagram can support recognition and shareability. YouTube/Loom supports proof and depth. Newsletter/blog supports authority and durable IP. But LinkedIn is the first place to create signal because the target buyer is professional, service-based, and already exposed to AI noise.

## Sprint Objective

The first 14 days should not be a brand overhaul.

The sprint objective is:

> Collect signal around whether founder-led experts will submit real AI misfires and consider paying for diagnosis.

The content should do three jobs:

1. Make the pain legible.
2. Demonstrate Farrice's judgment.
3. Invite one low-friction submission.

## Flagship Post Direction

Best first post:

> Farrice read his own AI-assisted content and realized it had the exact failure he is warning buyers about.

Why this works:

- It is honest.
- It avoids fake case-study energy.
- It proves judgment.
- It makes the offer concrete.
- It creates a natural bridge to AI Misfire Express.

Core post thesis:

> The draft was structurally fine, but it did not have lived tension, proof, or human judgment. That is the gap AI creates when the work behind the work is invisible.

## Draft Flagship Post

I read one of my own AI-assisted LinkedIn drafts and had that little pause.

Not because it was terrible.

That would have been easier.

It was organized. The point was clear. The phrasing was clean enough. It had the bones of a useful post.

But I would not publish it.

It had the exact problem I keep noticing in AI work:

It did the task.

It did not understand the work.

The draft knew the topic, but it did not carry the tension. It did not show the lived moment. It did not have the judgment that tells you, "This is technically correct, but it will not land."

That is the part most people are still rescuing manually.

Not grammar.
Not formatting.
Not even the idea.

The invisible judgment.

The reason one sentence feels generic and another feels true.
The reason a proposal sounds polished but still misses the buyer.
The reason an SOP looks complete until a real edge case shows up.
The reason a follow-up email says all the right things and still feels like nobody was listening.

This is where AI gets dangerous in a quiet way.

It gives you something finished-looking.

So you stop interrogating the work too early.

The correction you keep making afterward is not just cleanup.

It is data.

It is showing you the rule, taste standard, customer language, example, or decision boundary that the system could not see.

That is what I am building around now:

Not "better prompts" as the whole answer.

Better work instructions.
Better source-of-truth rules.
Better examples and counterexamples.
Better quality tests.
Better ways to make human judgment reusable.

Because if AI keeps getting 70% of the way there and you keep fixing the same 30%, the question is not only:

"How do I prompt it better?"

The better question is:

"What part of my judgment is still trapped in my head?"

If you have an AI output, prompt, SOP, workflow, content draft, proposal, or follow-up that is close but still wrong, send me one.

I will show you where AI is being forced to guess.

## Supporting Post Angles

### Post 2: The Correction Is The Data

Thesis:

> The part you keep fixing is the missing operating rule.

Reader payoff:

- inspect repeated corrections
- stop treating cleanup as random
- convert corrections into rules/examples

CTA:

> Look at the last AI output you fixed. What did you correct that you never told the system?

### Post 3: The Founder Is Still The Operating System

Thesis:

> Many founder-led businesses use AI, but the founder's judgment still carries the real workflow.

Reader payoff:

- recognize hidden founder dependency
- understand why tools alone do not fix workflow quality

CTA:

> If the work gets worse when you step away, the system does not understand the work yet.

### Post 4: Close But Wrong Is The Most Expensive AI Output

Thesis:

> Bad output is easy to reject. Close-but-wrong output costs more because it tricks you into editing instead of diagnosing.

Reader payoff:

- understand why "almost there" can be dangerous
- identify hidden review costs

CTA:

> Track one close-but-wrong output this week and write down what it made you fix.

### Post 5: Do Not Start With Automation

Thesis:

> Automating unclear work creates faster cleanup.

Reader payoff:

- sequence diagnosis before automation
- inspect source-of-truth and decision boundaries

CTA:

> Before automating the workflow, ask what the system is allowed to decide.

### Bridge Post: Send One Misfire

Thesis:

> The fastest way to improve your AI workflow is to inspect one real failure.

Reader payoff:

- clear next step
- low-friction submission

CTA:

> Send one failed output, prompt, SOP, workflow, or content process. I will map where AI is guessing.

## Comment Prompts

Use these as real comments on relevant posts, not engagement bait:

1. "The tricky part is that AI cleanup often looks like editing, but the repeated correction is usually an uncaptured rule."
2. "The question I keep coming back to: what did the human know that the system never got to see?"
3. "A lot of AI output is not wrong enough to reject. It is just generic enough to quietly drain trust."
4. "Before I automate a workflow, I want to know where the current version depends on taste, judgment, or edge-case memory."
5. "The hidden cost is not the first draft. It is the review loop nobody measures."

## DM Follow-Ups

For someone who resonates:

> Appreciate you engaging with that. Curious: where is AI already close to useful in your business, but still wrong enough that you have to correct it?

For someone with a misfire:

> If you want, send one prompt, SOP, draft, workflow, or output. Redact anything sensitive. I can point out where the system is being forced to guess.

For someone asking what you do:

> I diagnose AI outputs and workflows that are close but still wrong. The goal is to find the missing judgment, examples, rules, or source-of-truth gaps so the work becomes clearer and more repeatable.

For paid audit bridge:

> If this is a repeated issue, I can do a paid Misfire Map: one workflow, one Loom, one written diagnosis, and the first recommended fix.

## Metrics

Track:

- comments from qualified buyers
- profile visits
- DMs started
- misfires submitted
- audit interest
- paid audit sales
- objections repeated
- buyer phrases worth saving

Do not overvalue likes without conversation.

## What To Challenge

External reviewer should evaluate:

- Is the flagship post too inside-baseball?
- Does the CTA feel clear and low-friction?
- Are the supporting posts too educational?
- Is there enough story and proof?
- Which post should go live first?
- What would make the reader trust Farrice faster?
