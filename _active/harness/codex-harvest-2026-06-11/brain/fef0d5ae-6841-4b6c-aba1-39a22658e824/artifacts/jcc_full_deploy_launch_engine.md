## Workstream 1: Featured Section Build-Out

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The Featured section is where Referral Visitors and Post Readers decide whether you're real. These 4 tiles must do three things: prove you can think (Tile 1), prove you have depth (Tile 2), prove you're consistent (Tile 3), and open the door (Tile 4).

### Tile 1: "The Unsolicited Brief"

**Title**: `I wrote a campaign brief for a brand that never hired me.`
**Type**: Link → Substack article (publish the mini-brief as an essay)
**Thumbnail direction**: Dark background. Serif text. Layout mimics a professional brief cover page. Brand name visible but not dominant. Accent: terracotta line at the bottom. Text reads: "Campaign Opportunity Brief" with the brand name below it.

**Description copy** (LinkedIn Featured tile subtitle):
```
This is how I think about marketing. Not a pitch.
Not a proposal. Just the thinking — identity word,
customer voice, campaign architecture — for a product
whose marketing doesn't match what its customers actually say.
```

**Why this tile works**:
- Lynch GP-5: Leads with the food (the brief itself), not the appliance (your services)
- Sanders: Featured tiles with professional document thumbnails convert 3x more than text links
- Acosta: Functions as a lead magnet without being one. Readers who click are self-qualifying

**How to create it**:
1. Run `/lynch-product-prequalify` on a brand you genuinely admire
2. Run `/lynch-customer-voice-mine` to extract real voice data
3. Write the mini-brief (2-3 pages) using `/lynch-creative-brief` in mini mode
4. Publish on Substack as an essay titled: "I Wrote a Campaign Brief for [Brand]. They Didn't Ask Me To."
5. Link the Substack URL in the Featured tile

### Tile 2: "The Thinking Piece"

**Title**: `Why your best customers don't sound anything like your ads`
**Type**: Link → Substack essay or LinkedIn article
**Thumbnail direction**: Same dark aesthetic. Large pull-quote in serif: "The gap between what customers say and what brands say is where every great campaign lives." Small attribution: Farrice Cain.

**Description copy**:
```
Your repeat customers describe your product with words
your marketing team has never used. That gap isn't a problem.
It's the entire campaign.
```

**Why this tile works**:
- Lynch GP-6: The title is written from inside the reader's head. Jordan already knows this is true.
- Kallaway: Passes the "5 Universal Inputs" test. It's specific (your customers vs. your ads), emotional (the frustration of mismatched messaging), and actionable (implies a fix exists)
- Jensen: The title is specific enough to pass the AI authenticity gradient. 50 other people's AI would NOT produce this exact framing.

**How to create it**:
Write a 1,000-1,500 word essay covering:
- The customer voice gap (what customers say vs. what brands say)
- One real example (mine Amazon/Reddit reviews for a brand you admire; show the language contrast)
- The identity-first alternative (Lynch GP-1: name the identity word, show how it changes the creative)
- Close with an observation, not a CTA. Let the quality do the selling.

## Workstream 3: Mini-Brief DM System

### Brand Pre-Qualification Checklist

Before investing 2-3 hours in a mini-brief, run these filters:

```
□ PRODUCT FIT
  - Does the product have a genuine USP? (Not just "better" — actually different)
  - Do real customers love it? (Check Amazon reviews, Reddit, social mentions)
  - Is the marketing visibly weaker than the product? (Good product, mediocre creative)

□ ECONOMICS FIT
  - Revenue range: $1M-$15M (big enough to afford you, small enough to need you)
  - They're spending on paid media (visible ads on Meta/Google = budget exists)
  - The founder or CMO is accessible on LinkedIn (you can actually reach the decision-maker)

□ IDENTITY FIT
  - Can you name the identity word in under 60 seconds? If yes, there's a campaign here.
  - Would the product's best customer post about it without being asked? (Identity potential)
  - Does the category allow for a customer-as-media play? (Visible usage, shareable moments)

If 7+ out of 9 boxes checked → Write the brief.
If 5-6 → Maybe. Depends on how interesting the product is to you.
If <5 → Skip. Find a better brand.
```

### DM Template A: Warm Prospect (They've Engaged With Your Content)

```
Hey [Name] — I've been following your posts on [specific topic].
Your point about [specific thing they said] stuck with me.

I actually work in a similar space — I'm a creative strategist
focused on identity-first campaigns. The short version: I help
brands figure out why their customers love them, then build campaigns
that sound like the customers instead of the brand.

I wrote a campaign brief for [brand name] recently that shows the
thinking. Not a pitch — just an example of how I approach this.
Would you want to see it?

Either way, enjoyed your content. Keep going.
```

**Why this works**: No ask. No "let's get on a call." The brief IS the pitch. Lynch GP-5 (food first). The reader sees the thinking before they're asked to buy anything.

### DM Template B: Cold Prospect (Brand Founder/CMO for Mini-Brief Send)

```
[Name] — I spent some time looking at [brand name] this week.
Your product is genuinely good. Your customers clearly love it.
(I read through about 40 of their reviews.)

I noticed something: the language your customers use to describe
why they love [product] sounds nothing like your current marketing.
That gap is where the best campaigns live.

I wrote a short campaign opportunity brief — 2 pages — showing
what I'd do differently. Not a pitch. Not a proposal. Just
the thinking.

Want me to send it over?
```

**Why this works**: Specific. "40 of their reviews" signals real work, not a spray-and-pray DM. The gap observation is something the founder already suspects but hasn't articulated. Lynch GP-6 (writing from inside their head).

### DM Template C: Follow-Up (5 Days After No Response)

```
Hey [Name] — just floating this back up.
I wrote that short campaign brief for [brand].
Happy to send it over if you're curious.
No strings. Just wanted you to see what I saw
in your customer reviews.
```

**Why this works**: Short. No pressure. "What I saw in your customer reviews" creates curiosity without being aggressive. The brevity itself signals confidence.

### Post-Response Conversation Flow

When someone says "Yes, send it":
1. Send the mini-brief as a PDF or Substack link
2. Wait 24-48 hours
3. Follow up: "Curious what stood out to you. Happy to walk through any of it if you want — 20 minutes, no pitch."
4. The call IS the close. On the call, the brief does the selling. You just answer questions and listen.

## Workstream 4: LinkedIn Content Series (10 Posts)

> [!NOTE]
> These are organized by Diandra's 4-Bucket system. Each post is written in the polished Parallax voice. All have been pre-audited for em-dash count (≤1 per post), AI slop (zero banned phrases), and rhythmic asymmetry (Alic).

### Bucket 1: Growth Posts (Reach)

**Post G1: "The Customer Voice Gap"**

```
Your best customers describe your product better than your marketing team does.

I spent a week reading Amazon reviews for a skincare brand. The customers
kept saying the same thing: "I stopped wearing foundation." Not "my skin
improved." Not "reduced pores by 40%." They said: "I stopped hiding."

The brand's ads said: "Clinically proven to reduce blemishes in 14 days."

Both statements are true. One of them is a campaign.

Most marketing is written from inside the conference room. The best
campaigns are written from inside the customer's mouth.

The gap between what your customers say about you and what your marketing
says about you is the entire opportunity.
```

*Hook mechanics (Alic)*: Opens with a claim the reader already believes but has never articulated. The Amazon review detail is the trapdoor — it pulls the reader from "obvious observation" into "oh, this person actually does the work."

---

**Post G2: "The Identity Test"**

```
Nobody puts "I own a 12-megapixel camera" in their Instagram bio.

They put "I surf Pipeline."

GoPro understood this. They didn't sell cameras. They named the product
"Hero" and sold bravery. The camera was invisible. The identity did the work.

Here's the test for your product: would your best customer include it
in the 5-6 things that define who they are on social media?

If yes, your marketing should be about the identity, not the product.

If no, you have a positioning problem that no amount of better copy
will fix.

Most brands fail this test. Not because the product is bad. Because
the marketing is talking about the wrong thing.
```

---

**Post G3: "The Cereal Box"**

```
The smartest customer acquisition strategy I've seen cost less
than $200 a day.

A camera company gave away one product daily. 365 units per year.

People entered the contest. Then they browsed the full product line
while they were there. Then they realized they weren't going to win.
Then they bought one anyway.

The contest was the ad. The data capture was the funnel.
The "I probably won't win" realization was the close.

Cost of goods on 365 cameras was nothing compared to the $6M
in first-year revenue it generated.

The best acquisition strategies don't look like ads.
They look like something the customer wanted to do anyway.
```

### Bucket 2: Authority Posts (Credibility)

**Post A1: "The 14-Page Brief"**

```
Most creative briefs are 1-2 pages. The ones I write are 14-20.

Not because length equals quality. Because a 2-page brief means
you skipped the hard parts.

A brief that actually works covers:
→ The identity word the product should own
→ Customer voice architecture (their actual phrases, not yours)
→ Competitive creative landscape (what everyone else sounds like)
→ Pricing mechanics and media economics
→ The specific transformation the customer undergoes

When you hand a 14-page brief to a creative team, they don't need
a follow-up meeting. They don't need to "interpret the vision."
The vision is on the page.

The brief is the strategy. If you can't fill 14 pages, you don't
understand the product well enough to sell it.
```

---

**Post A2: "Customer-as-Media"**

```
The goal isn't to reduce your customer acquisition cost.
The goal is to eliminate it.

When a customer buys your product and then posts about it
without being asked, without a referral code, without a brand
deal — that's not UGC. That's your customer becoming your
media channel.

The question is whether your campaign was designed for that
to happen, or whether it happened by accident.

Most brands get lucky once. A customer goes viral. A review
takes off. Then they try to manufacture it with influencer
partnerships and ambassador programs.

That's backwards. You don't incentivize the behavior.
You design the product experience so the behavior is inevitable.

The campaign that gets acquisition cost to zero isn't a better ad.
It's a system where customers can't help but tell people.
```

---

**Post A3: "The Two Columns"**

```
Most copywriters write one column. The words.

They hand the script to a designer or video team and say
"make it look good." The visual interpretation is somebody
else's problem.

The strategists who command the highest fees write both columns.
Left side: what the viewer hears. Right side: what the viewer sees.

When both columns are designed by the same person, the words
and visuals don't just coexist. They compound.

A viewer hears "find your inner Hawaiian" while seeing a couple
on a beach at sunset. Remove either column and the ad loses half
its power.

If you only write words, you're leaving the other half of
the campaign to chance.
```

**Post C1: "The Mini-Brief Reveal"**

```
Last week I wrote a campaign brief for a brand that never hired me.

I spent two hours reading their customer reviews. Then I wrote

I sent it to the founder. No pitch. No proposal. No ask.

I'm writing 2 more of these this week for products I genuinely

DM me the product. I'll tell you if I see a campaign in it.
```

---

**Post C2: "The 90-Minute Diagnostic"**

```
The first thing I do when I start a project isn't look at the brand deck.

I interview 5 of the brand's best customers. 90 minutes total.

Not surveys. Not NPS scores. Actual conversations. What made them
buy. What almost stopped them. How they describe the product to
a friend who's never heard of it.

What comes out of those conversations is almost always a surprise.
The brand thinks people buy for one reason. The customers reveal
a completely different one.

That gap becomes the campaign.

The customer's language, their metaphors, their hesitations,
their moment of "this is who I am now" — that's the raw material.
Everything else is built on top of it.

If you've never heard your best customers explain why they chose you,
in their words, not a testimonial you wrote for them, you're
marketing blind.
```

### Bucket 4: Personal Posts (Bond)

**Post P1: "The 18-Year Convergence"**

```
I didn't start in marketing.

For 18 years I sat across from people in the middle of becoming
someone new. Psychology. Coaching. Transformation. Addiction recovery.
Career pivots. The moment when someone realizes the life they built
was designed for a person they no longer are.

The pattern I kept seeing: people don't change when they hear
the right argument. They change when they see themselves differently.
Not over months. In a single conversation.

That's the same mechanic that powers every great campaign ever run.

Nike didn't argue that running was good for you. They made runners
see themselves as athletes. The product became invisible. The identity
did the selling.

I spent 18 years studying that mechanic up close. Now I deploy it
for brands.

The career pivot sounds dramatic. It wasn't. I've been doing
the same thing my entire life. I just changed who was sitting
across from me.
```

---

**Post P2: "Naptime"**

```
I write Parallax during my son's naps.

That's not a flex. It's a constraint that changed how I work.

When you have 90 minutes before someone wakes up, you don't
outline. You don't brainstorm. You don't research for 45 minutes
and write for 15.

You sit down knowing what you want to say and you say it.

The constraint killed every habit I had that looked like work
but wasn't. The extra research pass. The "let me think about
the structure." The third draft that was really just procrastination
in a different font.

Most of what I thought was my "creative process" was just stalling.

The nap window doesn't care about your process.
It cares about whether you have something to say.
```

## Workstream 5: Comment Templates (Alic Comment Laboratory)

> These are **archetypes**, not scripts. Adapt them to the specific post.

### The Expander (adds a new angle)

```
This reminds me of something I keep seeing in [related field] —
[specific parallel observation]. The overlap is [what it reveals].
```

### The Challenger (respectful counterpoint)

```
I agree with [specific part], but I'd push back on [specific claim].
In my experience, [alternative observation with supporting detail].
The distinction matters because [why].
```

### The Evidence Dropper (adds proof)

```
This lines up with what I found when [specific thing you did].
[1-2 sentence detail]. The result was [outcome].
```

### The Question (seeds curiosity)

```
Curious how this plays out in [adjacent context].
Have you seen [specific scenario] where it breaks down?
That's where it gets interesting.
```

> [!TIP]
> **Alic's Comment Laboratory rule**: Track which comments get profile views. The comments that generate the most views become next week's posts. You're testing ideas in the comments before investing in full posts.
