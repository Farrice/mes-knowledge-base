THE HERO'S JOURNEY
A lot of authors and seminar leaders talk about the hero's journey. The
phrase was popularized by scholar Joseph Campbell, who introduced it
in his 1949 book The Hero With A Thousand Faces.
The hero's journey is a very powerful concept. And when you're writing
a Story for Hollywood or a fiction publisher, the hero's journey is a
reliably winning formula. But there are a couple of problems when you
try to use it for persuasion:
• It's complicated and tricky to master.
• It has many moving parts and it takes great knowledge, practice
and skill to create a successful hero's journey.
Besides the difficulty, there's another problem. For many experts, the
hero's journey has become a deity. These experts worship it as though it
were the only type of story that worksfor anything.
But that's just not true.
For the vast majority of persuasion situations, using a hero's journey
story is the equivalent of wearing a tuxedo (or a fancy evening dress) to
go out in the woods and cut down some trees.
Yes, the fancy clothes the Hollywood in-crowd wears to attend the
Academy Awards look stunning. The makers of hero's journey stories

WORKHORSES AND SHOWHORSES
Persuasion stories, like the one in the Wall Street Journal subscription
letter, are workhorses.
Hero's journey stories—like, for example, the movies Wall Street: Money
Never Sleeps and Margin Call—are showhorses.
Workhorses get the job done (i.e., haul the money into the bank), but they
don't call a lot of attention to themselves and in fact avoid center stage.
Nevertheless-and this is worth repeating-they get the job done.
Showhorses are all about the show. They love attention and crave as much
of it as they can get. They put on a great show. But when push comes to
shove, they're not the most reliable results-getters. True, they make
money by selling tickets. But not all that often by selling products and
services other than themselves.
Let's face it: These workhorse persuasion stories, while interesting and
attention-getting, are really not as glamorous as hero's journey stories.
But then, they're not supposed to be. In theatrical terms, workhorse
stories play a supportive role. In business terms, though, workhorse
stories solve problems to move a prospect smoothly towards becoming a
customer.
This book is about the workhorses of the story world.

ORIGIN STORIES
People everywhere are curious about where other people, things, ideas—
even the universe itselt—all come trom.
The Old Testament's first chapter is called "Genesis," a Greek word
meaning... you guessed it... "origin." Indeed it does describe the origin
of the universe. And different cultures around the world have their own
versions, from long ago, of how the Earth came into being.
Here in the 21st century, things are not that different. I just finished
watching the third season of the Amazon streaming series "Jack Ryan."
After the last episode, Amazon suggested I might want to watch the 2014
movie "Jack Ryan, Shadow Recruit." It was Jack's origin story. (Of course
I watched it.)
In persuasion, origin stories have a special format to be as believable as
possible:
• They contain enough convincing detail and fascinating facts to begin
a persuasion process... which could end up in a closed sale.
• Origin stories nearly always contain proof. But even when they
don't, origin stories have enough concrete elements so you will be
in a perfect position to add convincing proof soon enough.
• So, origin stories are not merely sets of promising claims in
narrative form. They are carefully crafted sequences of facts and

events that get your overall sales message off to a very good start.
Once you start looking for them in real life-and you should—you'll

"about" pages on websites (there's a great example below).
In a spoken presentation, you'll find origin stories at crucial points where
the presenter — salesperson, or attorney, or someone pitching a potential

intrigue at the same time.

QUALIFICATIONS/TRACK RECORD STORY
This type of origin story is an interesting way to answer the inevitable

say) what they are promising?"
Your prospects are naturally skeptical. And one of the best ways to
convince them that something is legitimate today, is to show what you
have done in the past makes it so.
This story is from a sales letter I wrote for a course on new hypnosis
techniques presented by one of my best clients ever. I learned a lot from
him just watching him teach (as well as reviewing in detail his products

was helping his business partner sell). The letter sold hundreds of
thousands of dollars of courses.

record, or when you are that expert yourself. In either case, this works

mysterious, concepts and techniques - and making them so

Notice that this story does more than establish Igor's credentials as an
innovative instructor. You also know that he's smart and disciplined (he

There's a bit of storytelling romance in the description of his journey in

How to create a Qualifications/Track Record Origin Story
To create a story like this, first make a list of all the things you, or the
expert you're writing about, have done to get to this point. All of them—
don't skimp. You're not going to use everything you've written, but it's
better to have more than you need, so you'll have a good selection when
you end up with the few key elements you're going to eventually use in
the story itself.
Next, make a list of qualifications, awards, degrees and other indicators
of recognition by others that show why this person is an expert.
Finally, make a third list where you take the most important and
interesting items from the first and second lists. The idea here is to do the
most with the least. If you have 20 items, but you can write a convincing
story with only five of those items, just put those five on the list.
Now that you have your pared-down list of ingredients, use them to

2. Important touchstones along the way - What are the key points

3. What others say - If you have accolades or awards you can
mention that further certify this person's expertise, mention them.
4. The result - Where did this person end up, and what can you

You can use this kind of origin story to explain why you, or someone

after the lead and you're ready to introduce the expert.

Once you get your prospect actively anticipating getting that sense of
relief themselves, you have a much more interested prospect than you
did just moments ago.

How to Create A Failure-Success Story
Let's start with the fact that not everyone is going to have a matched set
of failures and contrasting successes... nor do you need one. All you

need, at a minimum, is one dramatically painful failure, and one success
that shows you turned things around.
So you'll need at least one big matching contrast, or a set of matching
contrasts (like Karbo did). Start out with a piece of paper in landscape
mode and a line drawn down the middle (or a page in your word
processor with two columns that cover the whole page, in landscape
mode).
In the left column, or on the left side of the page, write out a symptom of
your failure. For example, don't write "I was broke" or "I couldn't make
any money." Instead, a symptom would be "My wife phoned me, crying,
because the landlord had shown up at the house-and we didn't have
money to pay it."
What you want to do is write as many symptoms down on the left-hand
side as you can.
Then, see how many contrasting examples you can come up with. These
examples show that you had achieved success where you were failing
before. For not being able to afford to pay rent, the contrasting example is
owning multiple pieces of real estate outright. (Karbo actually had that
and a lot more in the ad, but quoting the whole ad would have taken up
way too much space.)
When you have put all the symptoms on the left you can think of, and
matched up as many contrasting examples as you have on the right, take
a look and see:
1. Would your story work better to have a series of examples, the
way Karbo did, or
2.
Would one, longer composite description of your failures,
followed but one longer, composite description of your successes,
work better?
Choose the one you think will work best, and write that story.
A Failure/Success Story is great to open a speech, to include in a pitch,
and of course to start an ad or sales letter.

CHAPTER 4
STORIES ABOUT WHAT YOUR
PROSPECT IS EXPERIENCING
There's something about a detailed description, when it rings true, that
creates a moment of bonding between two people. Stories about what
your prospect is experiencing in the present moment have an uncanny
way of making your prospect feel like you know them... and, in turn,
that they know you. The reaction is more instinctive than thought-out.
These stories help you and your prospect get familiar, emotionally.
And this is tremendously useful in persuasion. When U.S. President Bill
Clinton said, "I feel your pain," he was using this technique in the
general sense. And while it wasn't all he did or said, he did get elected
president twice, after all. Love him or hate him, you've got to admit, Bill
Clinton had some pretty good persuasion chops.
What we're talking about in this chapter goes deeper than Clinton dia.
We're moving beyond a single empathy statement—"I feel your pain."—
to a fleshed-out story, as you'll soon see. These stories are more like little
movies than single-frame pictures. They move through time. People are
taking action in them.
You create motion in these stories by zeroing-in on a few facts or events
at different points in time.
Here's how it works: At first, you say, you notice this—that's the first
point. Later, you say, this happens-that's the second point. After a
while, you say, this happens-that's the third point.
When you build your stories this way, as you'll notice in the examples in

this chapter, you create a sense of motion through time. But very
economically. Adding motion in this crisp way adds impact and depth to
your story.
And though there is motion, one thing is missing from these stories: an
ending.
I don't mean these are eternal stories, that go on for centuries. What I
mean is: they're open-ended. On purpose. Because when you have a story
about a problem without a fixed ending, your reader or listener really
wants to know what the ending is... and that gives you an opportunity
to present your solution later as an offer, which they will be more likely
to buy (since it's something they've been wanting).
So, to be clear: These stories are not self-contained ads or sales pitches.
But they are integral components of a larger persuasive message, and they
make the message that much stronger.
One more thing, before we get to the stories themselves. And that's
research.
Making stuff up without any specific knowledge or personal experience

Make sure this doesn't happen by doing some research ahead of time.
The best kind of research is simply talking to people suffering from the
problem. But that's not always possible or practical to do. Almost as
good is going onto sites that have customer comments, like Amazon. Or
discussion forums, like Reddit.
What you are looking for is what people describe and how they describe
it. Reading what they say will give you a lot of valuable information to

STORIES ABOUT YOUR PROSPECT'S PAIN
When you are selling a product that relieves pain, you get instant
credibility with your prospect by describing that pain in a story the
prospect takes in as realistic and knowledgeable.
People are naturally suspicious when you promise you have a solution
that works, especially after they've tried other solutions that didn't work
for them before.
Again, remember, story like this does not promise the solution. Instead, it
makes the prospect much more receptive to a solution you will offer later,
because it shows you know what they are experiencing and how they
feel about it. Get this right, and you've gone a long way to dissolve
resistance.
Here's a story about what it's like to suffer joint pain. This could be part
of a larger sales message for a supplement to provide relief from joint
pain.
After a while, every morning you hate to get up. Not because
you're sleepy. You're well-rested enough. The problem is the pain
in your knees. You don't feel it much at first, but after that first
cup of coffee, every time you move your legs, it's like sandpaper is
grinding against your bones, underneath your skin.
Notice how this example moves, point-by-point, through time. At first,
it's just general and global: "every morning." But then it zeroes in. The
feeling is hardly noticeable at first. Then you drink a cup of coffee. And
after that, every time you move, you feel the agonizing pain.
This works in a couple of ways. First, obviously, if you have this kind of
joint pain yourself, the story brings it into sharp focus. But just as
important is the progression of time. The short sequence of events—
waking up, having a cup of coffee, and finally, feeling the pain full-
throttle—makes it more like real life.
To write a story like this, it's important to know what people actually
experience when they have this kind of pain. And then, if your story
rings true, you've created a lot of credibility with any person who is

experiencing the same pain.
Let's say you haven't experienced this pain yourself. What's the best way
to find out how others describe it?
Again, the best way is to have conversations with people who do. When
that's possible, find them and ask them. And listen closely to what they
say.
Now, that's not always possible. So, next best is reading what people
write — in online forums, and in customer comments on sites like
Amazon. People are remarkably willing to share, in granular detail, what
the pain is like for them.
Find the phrases they use, and be willing to use them. Copying short

any single source. Combine material from several sources. See if you can
write a description that includes a sequence of events over time. Be sure
to include as much visual and physical description as you can.
Stories about your prospect's pain work well as a lead-in to the
presentation of a solution that promises to ease that pain.
STORIES ABOUT THE WORLD TODAY
The concept "top of mind" is very useful when it comes to writing this
kind of story.
"Top of mind" refers to thoughts so important that they
crowd out all (or most) of everything else someone is thinking about.
People sometimes think of these things because of topics that get
repeated over and over—in the news, on social media, and even in casual
conversations between people in a specific group or niche.
A story about the world today combines comments about a "top of
mind" topic with a particular worry—or opportunity—likely to be on the
mind of the prospect thinking about this topic. Of course, later on in your
pitch or sales copy, you'll want to present your solution to that worry, or
a specific offer to take advantage of the opportunity.
You can use this kind of story to create some mental/emotional

my mind, too." Here's a story that focuses on recent rapid advances in

Being there!

SOME TIPS
Where do you use these "getting familiar" stories? There's no single
place in your persuasion messaging—whether it's in a presentation, a
pitch or a written sales message—that you must always, or can only, use
them. A better way to look at the question of where to use them is:
Wherever it would help your prospect realize and possibly even
experience why they need what you are trying to persuade them to buy
or do.
People are motivated by emotion, not logic. And if there's one thing you
can always count on these "getting familiar" stories to do is create
emotion in your prospect. That's because they remind the prospect of
facts and events in their own lives that created emotion for them—or,
particularly with stories about the world today, they put in sharp focus
feelings your prospect is currently having.
With that in mind, it might be useful to use one of these stories near the
start of your message or presentation. There will be times when you'll
want to use one of these stories right before you introduce your solution
or offer. And at the end of your presentation, before your call to action, it
might be persuasive to remind your prospect one final time of the pain
they will continue to experience if they don't do something about it.
Notice these stories are concise-concentrated. They don't drone on and
on. They're not stuffed with flowery adjectives and emotional language.
Instead, they cut to the chase to make their point with facts and
sequences of events. So it's a good idea to rewrite stories like this until
they are compact and as bare-bones-free of hype and flourishes—as
possible. Sometimes, shorter really is better.

CHAPTER 5
STORIES THAT PREDICT THE FUTURE
A big part of persuasion involves making a promise for a better future.
Most copywriters, salespeople and other professional persuaders know
this instinctively. Promises of better outcomes for the prospect are a key
part of almost every persuasive message.
Some types of predictions work much better than others. In this chapter,
we'll look at four types of persuasion stories that work exceptionally
well.
As much as you may like all four, you can't use all of them in every

not all.

that can help a prospect improve their social standing, or status. And the
fourth type is mainly useful only with products that improve health.
Nevertheless, it's good to learn about all four types of stories. Why?
Because seeing a persuasion story in action-even one you can't use in
your current persuasion efforts-can spark your imagination for the
stories you are going to create and use.

UNEXPECTED BENEFIT STORY
Possibly the most persuasive person of the first half of the 19th century in
America was Daniel Webster, the attorney, orator, and politician. I
wouldn't be a bit surprised if he knew, intuitively, every single kind of
story we talk about in this book.
One of Webster's most fascinating persuasive stories has survived intact
into the 21st century, courtesy of author and copywriter extraordinaire
Victor Schwab. This was not from a political speech. In this case, Webster
was asked by a New Hampshire friend to run the auction for his farm.
Here is the story Webster used to open the auction:
"Neighbors, we're not auctioning Tom Brown's good milk cows...
or 80 acres of fine land... or a sturdy home that's seen 20 winters.
"No. I'm offering you the chance of biting into a red apple with
the juicy sap running over your lips... the smell of new mown
hay... clear mountain stream water on your table... the crunch of
snow under your feet... and the best neighbors in the world..."
Legend has it that Webster hadn't even finished speaking before
ferocious bidding started. The farm sold for a much higher price than
anyone expected.
What was so unusual here: Webster didn't just present the offer-the
farm up for auction—in typical feature-and-benefit terms. If he had, he
might have said something like, "80 acres... twice as big as any other
farm in sight. And the milk from these cows is the very milk the

Governor demands for his own children!"
He didn't stop there (or even go there). Instead, he went above and

house-as necessary to mention but hardly that important, in his first
sentence.
But then, he did something really imaginative, something the people
gathered at the auction surely had not anticipated. Webster figured out
and very elegantly described a future unexpected benefit of living at this
farm. Namely, that the experiences you would get—at least for people
who like cold winters—would be about as close to heaven on earth as
you could get.
Unexpected benefits have a tremendous power in moving your case
forward when you are making an effort to persuade. In fact, it's fair to
say that one such benefit can do a lot of the heavy lifting for you-just as
it did for Daniel Webster's auction pitch.
Now, let's look at how this kind of story works in the 21st century. We
can look at an unexpected future benefit of an online course, by an

The sole purpose of the course is to show the student how to get through

problems most people assume they have to hire a lawyer to solve, but
actually don't, because they can solve them on their own.
The whole course is about solving problems without hiring a lawyer. But
that idea suddenly moves in an exciting new direction with this story:
In the final session, Donnie takes a welcome U-turn to clue you in
on what you need to know if-after you've looked at all the
alternatives—you've realized you must hire an attorney.
And there's no shame in that. Yes, in previous sessions, you
learned how to avoid getting a lawyer 95% of the time. But there
are certain situations in life where hiring an attorney is absolutely
necessary.
Even when you do, though, overpaying is not necessary, Donnie says. So

he will give you the same expert advice he would give you in a
$250/hour consultation. He'll let you in on how to prepare and present
your information so the attorney will realize right away they should
charge you as little as reasonably possible.
Lawyers don't work for free (most of the time), and they can get
expensive, fast. But what's important to you is: The same service can cost

This Unexpected Benefit Story works because common sense tells people

What this story does is validate that belief. Plus, it deftly gives in to the

Notice how this story is a great example of showing a future unexpected

Not only does the story make that doubt go away. It also turns hiring a
lawyer into a money-saving event. Two problems solved at the same
time.
How To Create An Unexpected Benefit Story
When you set out to persuade someone, whether it's in writing, in a
video, or in a personal conversation, you first typically make a list of the
features and the benefits of what you are suggesting or selling.
Features, of course, are essentially facts: What the product is. Benefits, by
comparison, are performances: What the product does.
After you've got your two lists, you can create an Unexpected Benefit
Story. First, step back, mentally. Ask yourself: What is the overall
promise of what I'm selling? What's the main idea?
(In the example above, the main idea is: Solve problems without hiring a


--- PAGE 55 ---

lawyer, and save lots of money as a result.)
Then, look through all your benefits and see which one is as different
from the main idea as possible, as well as valuable and maybe even
startling. It should not directly contradict the main idea, but it can stand
in sharp contrast to it.
(The story above shows a way to hire a lawyer when you absolutely have
to, but still save money.)
Once you have your unexpected benefit, build your Unexpected Benefit
Story using these questions as guides:
1. What's the unexpected benefit?
2. How is it like the other benefits, but also, what makes it different?
3. What are the implications or spinoffs of this benefit? That is, by
having this benefit, what else will the prospect be able to get, to
do, or to be?
4. What does the prospect risk missing out on by not having this
benefit?
PREDICTED TRANSFORMATION STORY
Comic books and Marvel Studios brought a wonderful idea into our
minds: superpowers.
At first, only fantasy heroes had superpowers. Wonder Woman can fly,
and she has superhuman strength and speed. Ant-Man can enlarge or
shrink objects and enlist ants to help him in his campaigns. Wolverine
has claws that could cut through anything, and he can see in the dark.
Spider-Man can shoot webs from his wrist and bind up enemies with
them.
And of course the original superhero, Kal-El from Krypton (also known
as Superman), has x-ray vision and can travel through time. Among
many other things.
Something happened, though, and it became popular to think everyone
has a superpower. People talk about their greatest talent or skill that
way. But I'd like to take it one step further, and say a superpower is not


--- PAGE 56 ---

just something you're good at, but something amazing. Something rare.
Something awe-inspiring. For the Predicted Transformation Story, a
superpower means you can do something that most people could barely
even imagine being able to do.
Simple example: A person who has become a millionaire might say that
making money is their superpower. They may be lucky or they may be
skilled-or both-but I wouldn't say that making money is their
superpower.
Sure, becoming a millionaire is a respectable accomplishment. Most
people aren't millionaires, but being one is hardly super-unusual.
Worldwide, according to the money-maven bank Credit Suisse, there are
62 million people who are millionaires (in U.S. dollars or equivalent
amounts). That's nearly 1% of the world population. A select group, sure.
But hardly a unique demonstration of a rare ability. Not exactly awe-
inspiring.
So what would be a superpower when it comes to making money? How
about having an uncanny ability to pick stocks that go up and up and
up? Much rarer, and outside the reach, it often seems, of ordinary
mortals.
Now that's a superpower.
So we can say Warren Buffett has that money-making superpower. He
was once considered the richest man in the world by Forbes magazine,
and it all came about because of the stocks he picked. Remember, he
didn't inherit the money or make it by building a mammoth business. It
all came from stock-picking.
Superpowers don't have to be famous and legendary. Something simple
that seems exceptionally rare will do.
You may have a friend who has a secret recipe for mixing mojitos that no
one else can even come close to. Mojitos are their superpower.
Maybe you have a friend who can always find a parking spot when no
one else can. Or, you might think of how Steph Curry plays basketball.
So, people with superpowers like I'm talking about might be world-
famous. But they don't have to be famous at all
They just have to be able to do something most people think they could


--- PAGE 57 ---

never have themselves DL1] and may not even personally know anyone who
has this ability themselves. (After all, not everyone knows your friend
with the mojito superpower.)
And that's what a Predicted Transformation Story is about. Promising to
deliver that kind of superpower to your prospect.
To put one of these stories together, you need to figure out something
that is true... plausible... something that you/your product/ your service
can actually deliver... and, most important of all, will be perceived as a
real, true, no-kidding superpower by your prospect. Something unique
and awe-inspiring. Previously thought impossible to obtain.
Let's create one right now.
Suppose you offered a service called "Automatic Rapport Coaching."
Your main promise would be that, by learning and practicing certain
special skills, you could get into rapport with anyone, anywhere,
anytime. A pretty big promise, but, by itself, hardly the promise of a
superpower.
Let's look at the ordinary powers your course will deliver first. As you
attempt to persuade someone to engage your Automatic Rapport
Coaching services, you might talk about features and benefits. Learning
to hold non-threatening eye contact (feature), which would create trust
(benefit). Learning to listen better (feature), which would make other
people relax when they talk to you (benefit). Learning mirroring and
matching behavior (feature), which puts you and the other person "in
synch" (benefit).
All of these are good, but none is rare and unusual enough to come
across as a superpower.
What would be? How about this:
After only a few coaching sessions, and sometimes after just one,
you will notice a stunning change in the way people respond to
you.
People who used to ignore you will seek you out, and many will
ask for your opinion and try to get your approval.
Those who previously resisted your ideas and argue constantly,


--- PAGE 58 ---

will suddenly start to agree with you and follow your lead.
Best of all, you'll feel a renewed surge of confidence in all your
interactions with people, because life will be generally more
pleasant and rewarding.
Now we've got something that barely seems possible for most people,
although they probably imagine there are some people walking this Earth
who have these special qualities and skills.
Even if it's more common than most people think, it seems like a
superpower to most of your prospects.
What you have done in this story is, you have put together the combined
effect of learning and using the individual skills from your coaching into
a single astounding ability most people don't have-and few can
imagine they would ever have.
By the way, you don't need to call it a "superpower" in your story. In
fact, probably better that you don't, because too many people associate
the new, diluted meaning with that word.
My point here is that a Predicted Transformation Story should tell of
something really special, exotic and desirable. If you can't wrap your
mind around calling what you come up with "a superpower," that's OK.
Just make sure the story you tell is about something above and beyond
what your prospect initially would expect, and, that it describes a
transformation that will make a meaningful difference in their life.
How To Create A Predicted Transformation Story
These stories are great. But what if you're selling something that can't
legitimately deliver a big transformation? One that a prospect could
conceivably see as giving them a superpower?
If you can't do that, then don't force this kind of story. Only use it when
the change you can promise is awe-inspiring.
And if that's the case, then use these questions to guide you in writing
one.
1. What is the one standout skill or ability the prospect can get from

your product or service that they would consider rare or nearly
unattainable?
2. What are the most impressive and desirable results someone who
has this skill could get?
3. Because of these results, how would your prospect's overall life
and sense of themself change for the better?

UCLA basketball coach, was called "the greatest coach of them all" by
Sports Illustrated magazine. Wooden emphasized in a TED Talk that the
best path to success was not to compare yourself to others. The path was
only to be the best that you could be.
Many other experts agree with Wooden. Only one problem: It's not what
people do. Regardless of the advice, we constantly compare ourselves to
others.
We have Nobel Prize Winners-and then we have all the lesser mortals.

Baseball has its major leaguers. They count for more in sports
conversations than the baseball players in the one-step-lower AAA
leagues.
Hollywood has its A-Listers. You read about those people on the gossip
sites, and see them more often than not in blockbuster movies.
We seem to like to pretend that, aside from superstars like Nobel Prize
Winners and Hollywood A-Listers, "everyone else" is equal. As Stevie
Wonder sings, "No one is better than I, and I know I'm just an average
guy." Most people don't like to admit they constantly measure the worth
of others and conclude they are superior or inferior to other people.
Unfortunately, there's something in the human brain that is constantly
preoccupied with status. Seems it can't be trained out of us. There's even
a phrase for this phenomenon at the everyday level: "Keeping up with
the Joneses."
And, when it comes to persuasion, it's important to note: Regardless of
what we're "supposed" to think and believe..
People do respond eagerly to otters to improve their status. Whether it s
fancy cars, or degrees from prestigious schools, or expensive watches, or
simply being recognized as Employee of the Month, we all crave
recognition-and the opportunity to rank higher in how we are regarded
by others.
In other words—improved status.
Advertising man Max Sackheim understood this human craving so very
well. An immigrant from Eastern Europe in the early 1900s, he knew
first-hand how not speaking English well would handicap a person in
America. Particularly, how poor English would crush their hopes of
upward mobility in society.
Today, it's less of an issue than it was at the start of the last century. But
at that time, for millions of recent immigrants to America who wanted
social acceptance in their new country, learning to speak "educated"
English was an urgent priority.
In the 1910s, Sackheim wrote a mail-order ad that ended up lasting 40
years. No one else could write an ad for this home-study course that
would sell more than Sackheim's-and many of the best tried.


--- PAGE 61 ---

Among copywriters, the ad is well known today. The headline: Do You
Make These Mistakes In English?
In a personal letter to author J.L. Watkins, Sackheim admitted, "Having
no formal education, I was concerned about the mistakes in English I
might have been making in my own writing. I was, therefore, a good
prospect—and I wrote what I thought would sell me."
The ad was interesting because the print is small and it covers a full
newspaper page. Much of the ad is about mistakes people make and
what it costs them in their lives. It introduces Sherwin Cody, a teacher
who had unusual abilities. Cody had developed a method that has
helped more than 90,000 people correct their mistakes in English.
Towards the end of the ad is a special piece of persuasion. When you
read it carefully you can see that the ad is not only promising improved
English. This last part of the ad is an Improved Status Story:
Sherwin Cody has placed an excellent command of the English
language within the grasp of everyone. Those who take advantage
of his method gain something so priceless that it can not be
measured in terms of money. They gain a trademark of breeding
that cannot be erased as long as they live. They gain a facility in
speech that marks them as educated people in whatever society
they find themselves. They gain the self-confidence and self-
respect which this ability inspires.
If you missed it the first time-and I've missed it a few times myself over
the years when I previously read the ad — check out these phrases:
• "something so priceless it can not be measured in terms of
money."
• "a trademark of breeding that cannot be erased as long as they
live."
• "a facility in speech that marks them as educated people."
What Sackheim is so expertly doing is painting a picture of several slices
of life and how each one will be different for the successful student of


--- PAGE 62 ---

Sherwin Cody's course. He's not making broad, sweeping claims.
Instead, he's weaving together threads of a new life for the prospect and
presenting a vision that is practically irresistible.
When you think about it, there's hardly a better way than the one he
used, to spell out the markers of higher status-without actually using the
words "higher status." And surely that story within the ad is one of the
main reasons it has been so successful.
Notice how the story doesn't proclaim, "If you pronounce words like an
uneducated lower-class person does, you'll never get a good-paying job
—but now, with this course, you will." It doesn't say, "Immigrants with
poor language skills will never get their children into good colleges-but
that's all about to change, once you take this course."
Those indeed may have been the hopes of those reading the ad.
Sackheim was clever in the way he finessed these awkward issues in his
story. Issues about social status can be sore spots for a lot of people, and
this story threads the needle admirably to make its point without
creating hostility and distrust in the reader.
Tips For Improved Status Stories
Hinting works here. In Sackheim's case, people already know that being
marked "as an educated person" opens doors for them that previously
would have been bolted closed. They don't need to have it spelled out for
them. So the story promises improved status in a way that does not
create resistance, and that the reader can accept.
If you have a product or service that can improve your prospect's status,
it's often best to promise this indirectly rather than too blatantly and
bluntly. Again, notice Sackheim didn't say, "You'll be seen as a member
of the upper classes of society" or anything quite that direct. It's more
effective and believable when you allude to the improved status, rather
than hit the prospect over the head with it!

CHAPTER 6
REASSURANCE STORIES
People are funny. We rarely have just one emotion at a time. When we
are thrilled about something, there's also a part of us waiting to find out
if there's a catch. When we're excited about something happening now,
we're also wondering what will happen next. When we are at our most
decisive, our most timid and wishy-washy worries and doubts dance at
the edges of our minds.
We may not be completely aware of these multiple emotions as they
occur. But anyone in the business of persuasion is keenly aware that
prospects have more than one emotion at a time.
Take our whole legal system. As a juror, you have to be convinced beyond
a reasonable doubt that someone accused of a crime is guilty.
What does that mean? The way I see it, that means you can have a belief
that someone is guilty (first emotion), but if your doubt (second emotion)
is strong enough, then you cannot convict that person of the crime
they're charged with.
Well, if you can have belief and doubt at the same time, then you are
certainly experiencing two emotions simultaneously.
Most persuaders are not government prosecutors. But another version of
the same problem occurs for everyone else. No matter how much
rospects like what we are saying, they also have doubt... skepticism..
ear of the unknown... and similar feelings that stall or preven
persuasion. All these feelings at the same time.

persuasion can be successful.

STORIES ABOUT SOMETHING NEW OR UNFAMILIAR
There's an old joke in Hollywood:
A nervous screenwriter sits before an imposing studio chief. The chief

"I want something different—but familiar."
Stan Laurel (of Laurel and Hardy fame) put it bluntly: "Humor is the

And the joke does convey truth. People often say they'd like something
different, but what they really want is a slightly different version of what
they're already familiar with.
Most people, but not all people. Some people shy away from anything the
slightest bit familiar. Like composer John Cage. He says, "I can't
understand why people are frightened of new ideas. I'm frightened of the

A quick solution is often a metaphor.
What's that? Maybe you're remembering some complicated or
convoluted definition from high school English class. Put that aside, and
understand that a metaphor is when you describe one thing to make it
easier to understand another thing. Anne Miller, the expert on metaphors
I always turn to (and author of a great book about using metaphors in
business, The Tall Lady with the Iceberg), says a metaphor is simply a
comparison.
Let's look at a metaphor in action, to create a story that eases the
acceptance of something new.
Suppose you are selling a business insurance policy where most people,
for decades, have been used to getting three types of protection-and
your insurance policy, innovatively, has five.
Using a metaphor, you could say,
You know how when you put on a glove to protect yourself from the
cold, it snuggly covers all four fingers and your thumb on each hand?

Well, imagine if you had a pair of gloves that only covered three fingers
on each hand, but didn't cover your thumbs or your index fingers. They
would be exposed to the bitter cold. You could get frostbite on your
thumb and finger, even with your gloves on.
That's what we thought about this important form of protection, so we
added two coverages to the policy. Now, just like with a full glove, you
are protected in five ways."
Because it is a metaphor, it doesn't explain all the details or even the
reason for the change. But somehow, it makes sense. If you use a
metaphor, make sure it's simple and easy to understand, like this one
was.
A good metaphor uses images and is simple to visualize, and thus
understand. Like when you talk about a glove that leaves your thumb
and first finger exposed to the cold. Anyone who's ever worn a glove in
the cold can quickly visualize and understand that.
And when you get it right, your metaphor gets you over the hump of
untamiliarity with your prospect. And sometimes-like when you are
making just a few changes to what people are used to, but your product
or service is still largely the same—a metaphor is all you need.
Other times, though, when the differences are greater, you need more.

To be sure, not every product or service is a good fit for a money-back
guarantee. If, say, you are selling a consultation, a guarantee probably
won't be a fit. But whenever it makes sense, you should use a guarantee,
because you'll get more sales overall. Most people won't ask for a refund
if you've delivered a quality product.
What's important here when you're looking at this story is not to get
caught up in the style, as impressive as it is. Pay attention instead to the
layers of reassurance it provides. You need to get this result or you get all
your money back (Guarantee #1). If you don't play the best round of
your life, you not only get all your money back, I'll spot you $10 extra
just for your trouble (Guarantee #2).
In a market like golf, where customers have tried so many things and
been disappointed, this kind of reassurance story helps nudge wary
prospects off the fence. In most markets, you don't need to go so far. But
it is always a good idea to bring a story about a guarantee to life with
details, images and specifics the best you can.

STORIES THAT EXPLAIN
When I took my first sales training more than 30 years ago, I got a strict
warning: "Sell now, educate later."
What I got from that is: Let your client do most of the talking. Don't say
too much. Hear what your prospect has on their mind.
But when I got deep into the study of direct-response copywriting a few
years later, I frequently heard this advice: "The more you tell, the more
you sell."
That is, provide details. Make your complete sales pitch. Don't hold
important information back.
These two rules seem to contradict each other. But do they?
I came to a nuanced understanding that includes both of them. Iť's true
that if you spend a lot of time educating without using persuasion stories,
vou'll have a hard time winning someone else over. And it s also true
that you've got to tell your prospect something, because nearly all of the
time they're not going to persuade themselves with no input from you.
So what's the sweet spot?
Carefully-crafted stories that explain while persuading at the same time.
It's not that they don't educate. It's that they educate your prospect about
reasons to buy (benefits) and desired outcomes (results). And, they give
your prospect an advance look at the experience of using your product
and service.

STORIES THAT SHOW HOW TO USE A PRODUCT
So one of the questions looming in the mind of anyone thinking about
buying something is so simple that many persuaders overlook it entirely:
"How does this work, and how can I use it?"
You may know the answer to this question so well that it may never
occur to you that your prospect doesn't know the answer at all.
But, think about it. If your prospect doesn't know, then all the big claims
you may be making could ring hollow and have no meaning. You believe
the claims are true. But your prospect is in the dark. And if you think
your prospect will figure this out on their own, you'll be wrong more
often than you'll be right.
That's where a Story That Shows How To Use A Product comes in.
This kind of story is not a mini-instruction manual. (That would be the
kind of "educating" I was warned against in my sales training.)
Instead of being a set of instructions, it's a narrative about how someone
else used the product to get results. It touches on some of the key points,
but does not need to spell out in hands-on detail how the results
occurred.
Your main goal with one of these stories is to create a realistic guided
visualization of your product in use (experience), ending in success
(results). This kind of story tells about a user of your product who your
prospect can identify with. As they hear or read your story, they start to
imagine themself going through the same experience.
Here is such a story from a 1921 ad written by copywriter (and later,
author) Victor Schwab. The story is about a secretary who took a course
and ended up becoming a professional writer of fiction. The headline
was very intriguing at the time:
How I Made $350.00 On One Short Story
And How I Learned to Write Stories That
Actually Sell Themselves.

When And How To Use This Kind Of Story
It's tempting to use a story that shows how to use a product right at the
start of your presentation or advertising, but that's usually a mistake.
You first need to engage your prospect's interest, establish rapport, and
introduce the idea that you have a product that can solve the prospect's
problem—all before you tell your prospect how to use what you're
offering.
Remember, you already know that your prospect needs what you have
and that their life will be better once they have it. But often, they don't.
So you have to warm them up first, and lead them to a place where they
accept that they might want something like what you have.
A few questions to ask to help you get started putting a story like this
together:
1. What problems, doubts, fears or desires might be on the mind of your
prospect—in regard to the results your product or service provides?

2. How can you acknowledge those feelings so as to open the mind of
the prospect to being walked through how to use your product?
3. What are some of the most exciting actions the prospect will take
when using the product, and how can you include them in a description?
STORIES THAT DESCRIBE A PRODUCT'S MECHANISM
In his landmark 1966 book Breakthrough Advertising, copywriter Gene
Schwartz introduced the idea of mechanism as a persuasion tool. In short,
in this context, a mechanism is a compelling description of how a
product works. Usually this means a unique process or combination of
features, which sets the product apart from its competition.
When a story describes a mechanism effectively, it's not all mechanical
and matter-of-fact. A good mechanism description includes exciting
benefits that show and explain why the product is unique, and better
than its competitors.
Stories That Describe A Product's Mechanism almost never go deep into
technical details. They provide just enough specific, tangible information
to persuade a prospect that there really is something better-and more
appealing-about a product.
An easy way to understand this is to think about "normal" (one-gear)
bikes and compare them to 10-speed bikes. The set of 10 gears, and the
system for shifting from one gear to another, is the mechanism. The
benefit of this mechanism is that you get the ability to change the
pedaling resistance of the bike, depending on the gear selected-and in
that way, make it easier to get where you're going.
If you're going to tell a story about the 10-speed bike, you won't go into
gear ratios and pedal-to-bike-chain mechanics. You'll simply explain
how you use a certain gear while climbing a hill... another gear on a flat
road... and what's the advantage of having that flexibility.
Now, not every product or service has a mechanism worth highlighting
in a persuasive story. But it's worth analyzing and thinking about what
you're selling to see if yours does.
Because if you do have a unique mechanism and you can tell a clear,

Hans Zimmer is a legendary composer for the movies. He has won two

CHAPTER 8
STORIES THAT BUILD TRUST
It's perplexing, but it's real: The more someone wants something, the
more they are going to resist your efforts to persuade them.
Think about your own life. Maybe you were planning a special vacation
for a long time. The closer it came, the more excited you got. But at the
same time, so dia this persistent sense of dread: What it something goes
wrong? What it the airline loses my luggage? What if it rains all week
and we can'tgo anywhere?
Most people experience this twin sense of excitement and dread,
especially when they need to make a decision about whether or not to
buy something.
It doesn't make much sense. Until you realize that most people have had
a version of this happen to them: They got excited about a new idea or
about something they were going to buy... they tried it out... and for one
reason or another, they were bitterly disappointed.
Of course, this doesn't always happen. Sometimes things work out just
the way they expected. Occasionally, things exceed expectations.
But it does happen sometimes. So, to protect themselves against the same
kind of disappointments they have suffered in the past, people get wary.
Suspicious. Skeptical.
This leads to their having a natural reluctance to believe a persuader. It's
realistic, therefore, to assume people don't fully trust you, or what you're
saying.

All experienced persuaders know this. And that is why they do their best
to dissolve resistance in advance with stories that build trust. We have a
wide variety of story types you can use, so let's take a look at each of

Tips For Case Studies
• Decide which result or superior quality you want to highlight.
• Now, list all the events that led up to that result.
• Decide which aspect of yourself, or your product, you want to
highlight in the case study. For example, you might want to highlight
that you re a consultant who can really solve the toughest marketing

We need to talk about this. Because to get the trust of the people you are
persuading, they must believe that what you are telling them is true.
And I can't think of a better way to start this chapter than to take a quick
trip to the town called "Truth or Consequences."

In 1950, NBC announced a contest: They would broadcast one of their
shows from the first municipality that named itself after that show.

contest. And that is how the town's new name came about.

itself was goofy. Contestants had only a couple seconds to answer a fairly
impossible trivia question (giving the right answer would be considered

consequences for getting caught in a lie.
But, the show was fun—if you like that kind of thing.
Now I bring "Truth or Consequences" up in these two versions-the
how and the town-because the question of truth in persuasive storie

untruth, than with the truth itself.

selling with the truth does require more effort going in, it's a sale that
won't come undone in the way a sale based on a lie can and often does.

it's entertaining. And, as long as it's clearly labeled as fiction.

would kind of kill the mood, don't you think?

fictional and does not depict any actual person, entity or event." You

happened."

"Fiction has to make sense,"

-knowing

The celebrated British writer Samuel Johnson said it best: "Promise, large
promise, is the soul of an advertisement." Though he said it in the 1700s,
it is still true today. But it's true in more than advertising—it's true in any
persuasive effort.
If you are making a business proposal... if you are pitching a business
idea... if you are trying to sell anyone on anything... then a large
promise (especially one that is believable, and that you can fully deliver
on) is the fuel that sends your prospect towards a willingness to agree.
Persuasion stories that predict the future are the kind of promises that
gives extra momentum to your ads and presentations. One of them, the
Unexpected Benefit Story, can work in almost any persuasion effort. The
other three types are more specialized and will work best in particular
kinds of situations, which we cover in detail in Chapter 5.
Here are the four kinds:
• Unexpected Benefit Story
• Predicted Transformation Story
• Improved Status Story
• Future Health Improvement Story
The key thing to remember about this kind of persuasion story is that
you use it to make the most legitimate, attractive appeal regarding a
future that your prospect will want. You can use it right at the beginning
of an ad or a pitch, or right before the point where you spell out your
offer.
Reassurance Stories (Chapter 6)


--- PAGE 132 ---

These stories are the cure to the widespread human oddity that we often
have more than one emotion at a time-and one of those emotions is
doubt. That is, someone can have a very strong desire to agree with you
or to buy something, but at the same time they can also have a nagging
doubt that maybe they shouldn't.
Most of the time, doubt wins. It's always easier to do nothing than to do
something you're not quite sure about.
Until, that is, there's no doubt remaining. Then, there's really no choice
but to move forward on something you clearly want.
Ihus, reassurance stories. They help ease, diminish, and remove a
prospect's doubt without you having to resort to being too pushy or
obvious about it.
We cover four types:
• Stories About Something New Or Unfamiliar
• Stories About How Others Have Made It Work
• Stories About A Hard-To-Believe Claim Of Endurance
• Stories About If You Want A Refund
The best place to use a reassurance story is where doubts come up. In a
live presentation, you can often tell by body language or questions. In
sales copy, this is something you learn from experience.
Stories That Explain (Chapter 7)
Many persuaders believe that if you find yourself forced to explain
yourself, you've already lost the persuasion battle. What I've found is
that's only true when your explanation itself is not persuasive. A long,
bland recitation of facts comes across like a boring lesson, which is the
opposite of an intriguing piece of persuasion.
Nevertheless, you can't avoid explaining entirely as part of the
persuasion process. Because sometimes you do need to explain to get a
prospect comfortable with what you're selling. Why? Because if they
don't understand it, they can't get on board with you.
The way to explain successfully in this situation is to use a persuasive


--- PAGE 133 ---

story that explains. These stories are both elucidating and convincing. We
cover four types in this chapter:
• Stories That Show How To Use A Product
• Stories That Describe A Product's Mechanism
• Stories That Eliminate All Other Alternatives
• Problem/Solution Stories
The key to making these stories work is to provide enough information,
as simply as possible, to keep your explanation as exciting and
convincing at the same time. But don't get stuck in the weeds of intricate
detail, or drone on and on.
The best place to use this kind of story is after you have fully introduced
your idea, process, service or product. Imagine the question coming up
in your prospect's mind: "Well that sounds interesting, but how in the
world does it work?"
Your story that explains will answer that question-again, in the most
persuasive way possible.
Stories That Build Trust (Chapter 8)
When most people want something, they experience two things at once: a
sense of excitement (about getting what they want) and a sense of dread
(that something will go wrong).
This is a normal human reaction. You might even say that the seemingly
instantaneous sense of dread is hard-wired into us-emotional
protection against making a mistake that would lead to disappointment,
or, worse yet, disaster.
Persuasion stories can really help you here. This chapter covers five types
of stories that build trust (in other words, extinguish that sense of dread
in your prospect):
• Case Studies
• Certification Stories
• Expert Testimonials


--- PAGE 134 ---

• Results Testimonials
• Experience Testimonials
All of these stories do a different version of the same thing: They convey
the experience other people have had with what you are proposing, and
showing the prospect why they can feel safe accepting what you suggest.
Stories that build trust will fit just about anywhere in a presentation or
written sales promotion. That's because prospects are always wondering
about what other people say and what other people will think. These
stories tell them.
What Makes A Story Both Compelling And Believable
Chapter 9 talks about the importance of using the truth as the basis for
persuasion stories, as well as the challenges of staying truthful. No doubt
false stories can be popular and successful, and become known very
quickly. As Winston Churchill said, "A lie gets halfway around the
world before the truth has a chance to get its pants on."
A persuasion story lie seems very appealing, until you have to confront
two serious consequences:
1) Anger from customers, and
2) Accountability from regulators.
Plus, depending on how you look at things, your own ability to sleep
well at night. Therefore, though sometimes it's a little extra work to
create them, the stories based on truth are always your best bet.

One More Thing About The Hero's Journey
By now I think you understand my position that the hero's journey is just
not the right format for persuasion, in nearly all situations. True, there is
that rare instance where it can work. But for everyday persuasion, and
even for some of the biggest deals being made, the type of persuasion
stories we've talked about are simply much better.
Especially when you're under time pressure-as most of us are, nearly
all of the time.
I deliberately did not include much on hero's journey stories up until
now. Why? Because I believe it's more useful to focus on what works
than on what doesn't.
But so many people talk about hero's journey stories-and more than a
few "experts" will tell you with a straight face that these are the only kind
of stories you should be using for persuasion-that I want to take a
moment to show why hero's journey stories are not a good fit for
persuasion.
I have read dozens of books on writing dramatic stories, and I picked a
few key insights from a couple of really good ones:
First: The Protagonist's Journey, by Scott Myers. He acknowledges there
are many story models that work for movies.
He would be the last person to tell you there's one size that fits all.
Nevertheless, he offers the following set of plot points (milestones) you
would typically find in a hero's journey story, scripted to be a film:
1. Opening
2. Hook
3. Lock
4. Deconstruction Test
5. Transition
6. Reconstruction Test


--- PAGE 136 ---

7. All Is Lost
8. On The Offensive
9. Final Struggle
10. Denouement
This is one of the best shorthand descriptions of the hero's journey I've
ever seen. But think about all the time it takes to tell a story with 10
sections like that
Setting aside the complexity and the enormous amount of effort it takes
to write it, how long does it take for the story to play out in a movie? Ten
to 15 minutes of edited dialogue, action, camera work and music for the
filmed entertainment occurring after each of the 10 plot points. One-and-
one-half to two hours in total.
Now, in the world of filmed entertainment, the person who can write a
successful story like this for the screen is called "a genius" and just might
win an Oscar.
But in business, someone who takes from 90 minutes to two hours to tell
a single story while making a presentation is called "a bore." Few sales
professionals can do this in a conversation with a prospect and close a
sale, much less keep a job.
So that's a big reason hero's journey stories don't work in persuasion. Put
plainly, they're much too complicated and they take too long.
The second thing I want to mention is the grand purpose of a good hero's
journey. I mean this with all sincerity. Many people (including me)
believe that the intense interest we all have in a good story of this type, is
due to one simple fact: For thousands of years, hero's journey stories
were how we passed wisdom from one generation to another.
We are in fact wired to lock our attention in on a good story of this type
for this very reason. We know we might learn hard-won wisdom in the
story that could protect us. Maybe even something that could save our
lives.
In The Moral Premise: Harnessing Virtue and Vice for Box Office Success,
Stanley D. Williams sums it up this way:


--- PAGE 137 ---

The goal of the storyteller is to take the audience through an
emotional and psychological journey that reveals a poignant truth
about the human experience.
Yes. That's what makes great movies. And novels. And plays.
But that same goal-to reveal a poignant truth about the human
experience—ls way too ambitious (and weighed down with far too muci
aggage) to work for a persuasion story in a business setting
As persuaders, we often just want to get from a no (or a maybe) to a
willingness to agree. We normally don't set out to take audiences on an
emotional roller-coaster ride to remind them of life's eternal truths.
So, once again, my advice is learn from, and enjoy hero's journey stories,
whenever you have the time and inclination. But please, leave them
where they belong-on the screens, on the stage, and on a novel's
printed page.
Another Way To Think About When To Use Persuasion Stories
I've given careful instructions and suggestions about exactly when and
where to use the six kinds of persuasion stories. But now I'd like to offer
another point of view. And that is, use them responsively-in accordance
with how your prospect is reacting to what you're saying at any given point
in your persuasion presentation.
This is something ad copywriters learn through experience, and they
develop it into an instinct. So do some top sales professionals. It's much
easier to figure out how your prospect is reacting, in an in-person
persuasion situation. You can tell a lot from facial expressions, body
language, questions and objections.
Persuasion stories are especially effective when you need to address
problems that stump many persuaders: confusion, uncertainty, and
outright objections.
Confusion-If you are introducing a new idea, or you have a product or
process that works in a superior way, you can use a story to clear up
your prospect's confusion. An I-couldn't-find-what-I-was-looking-for-so-
I-created-it-myself story, well told, can turn confusion into
understanding-and fascination. A story about something new or

mechanism in simple, striking terms can help a prospect see why what
you have to offer is different and better.
Uncertainty-Some of the most common worries people have come in

• Does it really work?
• OK, even if it does work, will it work for me?
• What happens if something goes wrong?

work wonders soothing these worries.

Outright Objections—Persuasion stories are a gold mine for handling
objections without coming across as pushy or defensive.
If you get a question about product construction or how long it's going to
last, you can tell a convincing story about a hard-to-believe claim of
endurance.
If you're challenged about the price being too high, you can tell an
unexpected benefit story and a results testimonial.
If your prospect says they want to shop around, there may be nothing
you can do, but you can at least try a story that eliminates all other
alternatives. Sometimes just one fact, feature or benefit can turn things
around. And a persuasion story is the best way to deliver it.
