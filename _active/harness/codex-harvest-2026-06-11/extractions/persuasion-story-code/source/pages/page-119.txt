And it's the specific images that create more emotion, for that which we
see—whether in reality, or in our mind's eye-turns out to be one of the
most powerful and reliable ways to create emotion.
Being specific with images has another advantage: It keeps readers' (and
listeners') attention. We want our stories to be interesting, of course. An
image is always specific, but a generalization never is.
Story guru Lisa Cron, in her book Wired for Story, says:
Abstract concepts, generalities, and conceptual notions have a
hard time engaging us. Because we can't see them, feel them, or
otherwise experience them, we have to focus on them really, really
hard, consciously-and even then our brain is not happy about it.
We tend to find abstract concepts thumpingly boring.
One great way to find out whether you are using word-pictures or
abstractions is to read a sentence and ask yourself these questions: Could
I take a picture of it? Or, could I make a movie scene of it? If the answer
is an instant, definite yes—if, in your mind's eye, you can see the images
or succession of images-then you're in good shape. If you're not sure or
the answer is no, then it's time to rewrite and add more word-pictures.
