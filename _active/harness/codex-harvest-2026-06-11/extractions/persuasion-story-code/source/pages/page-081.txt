The story continues until its inevitable "Hollywood ending"-literally.
That's because the writer ends up in California, writing stories for the
movies. Happily ever after? We don't know for sure, but for her sake,
let's assume that it was.
There's a lot to learn from in this story. Professor Walter B. Pitkin—a real
person—is presented factually in a way that establishes great authority
and believable proof that he can deliver the goods. That is to say, he is
the teacher that even the best writers go to and count on.
What makes this a Story That Shows How To Use A Product, though, are
the touchpoints in the secretary's journey to become a writer, and these
sentences in particular:
Prof. Pitkin's course revealed to me the secret of creating interest.
It taught me how to give my story that subtle touch that appeals
to the editor.
Best of all, it taught me how to find ideas for stories in the most
trivial happenings.
And so I studied Prof. Pitkin's course in my spare time, and while
I studied it I wrote a story based on one of its plot suggestions.
I sent it to one of the biggest magazines in the country, confident
that the technique was faultless, and that I had woven setting, plot
and characters into an absorbing narrative.
Creating interest... subtle touch... finding ideas for stories... writing the
story in her spare time... sending the story to one of the biggest
magazines in the country. (In case you didn't know, way back in the day,
a good number of large national magazines bought short stories from
freelancers—far more magazines than buy short stories today.)
Each of the five sentences above, quoted from the story, show specific
actions the writer took (or, if you like, with sentences two and three, the
actions the product took in teaching her what to do). And, in that way, the
story shows how she used the product-and how the prospect can, too.
