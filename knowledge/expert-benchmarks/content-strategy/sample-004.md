# Multi-Channel Distribution Strategy: Context-Engineered Content Deployment

## Built on Nate B. Jones's Five Vectors of Memory Attack

### The Problem with "Post and Pray" Distribution

Most content distribution is linear: write piece → share on platforms → hope for engagement. This ignores the fundamental architecture of how content persists in audience memory.

Jones's Five Vectors framework treats distribution as a **memory engineering problem**: how do you ensure your content occupies persistent space in the audience's working context?

---

### The Five-Vector Distribution Architecture

**Vector 1: Recency (The Fresh Signal)**
Content deployed in the last 24-48 hours occupies the audience's immediate working memory.

*Distribution tactic*: Stagger publication across platforms by 6-12 hours. Don't post everywhere simultaneously — create a rolling wave where each platform encounter feels like a fresh touchpoint, not a duplicate.

*Schedule*:
- Hour 0: Primary platform (where your core audience lives)
- Hour 6: Secondary platform (format-adapted, not copy-pasted)
- Hour 12: Community seeding (Slack, Discord, Reddit — genuine question format)
- Hour 24: Email to subscriber list (with unique framing not available publicly)
- Hour 48: Outreach to 3-5 creators for reshare/commentary

**Vector 2: Frequency (The Repetition Signal)**
Repeated exposure builds familiarity. But raw repetition causes annoyance.

*Distribution tactic*: The same INSIGHT, in 5 different FORMATS. Not the same post reshared — the same truth repackaged so each encounter adds a new dimension.

*Format ladder*:
1. Text post (the insight, compressed)
2. Carousel (the insight, visualized step-by-step)
3. Video (the insight, demonstrated with screen-capture or talking head)
4. Thread/article (the insight, contextualized with evidence and case study)
5. Comment/reply (the insight, deployed as response to someone else's adjacent post)

**Vector 3: Relevance (The Personal Signal)**
Content that maps to the audience's active problems bypasses the attention filter entirely.

*Distribution tactic*: Segment your distribution into 3 audience layers:
- **Hot layer** (actively searching for this topic): SEO-optimized long-form + targeted DMs to people who've posted about this problem
- **Warm layer** (aware of the problem, not actively seeking): Social posts with problem-recognition hooks
- **Cold layer** (unaware): Pattern-interrupt formats that name an invisible problem

**Vector 4: Salience (The Emotional Signal)**
Emotionally charged content persists in memory longer than neutral content.

*Distribution tactic*: Every distribution pass should lead with ONE of these emotional triggers:
- Surprise (counterintuitive data)
- Validation (confirmation of what they suspected but couldn't prove)
- Fear (consequence of inaction)
- Hope (transformation story)

Rotate the trigger across platforms so the same audience encountering the content on multiple channels gets a different emotional angle each time.

**Vector 5: Association (The Network Signal)**
Content connected to existing mental frameworks persists because it attaches to pre-built neural pathways.

*Distribution tactic*: In every distribution channel, explicitly connect your content to a known framework, person, or cultural reference:
- "This is the PLG version of what Netflix did with..."
- "Hormozi talks about offer stacking — here's the content equivalent..."
- "Remember when Basecamp..."

The association gives the audience a filing cabinet for your content, preventing the "interesting but I forgot where I read it" decay.

### The Context Compression Principle

Every distribution touchpoint should be SHORTER than the previous one:
- Blog post: 1500 words → LinkedIn post: 250 words → Tweet: 50 words → Quote card: 15 words

This isn't lazy repurposing — it's **compression engineering**. Each format strips away context until only the most essential signal remains. The compressed version should be able to stand alone AND drive curiosity back to the full piece.

**Test**: If your tweet doesn't make sense without reading the blog post, you haven't compressed — you've excerpted. Compression preserves the complete insight. Excerpting preserves a fragment.
