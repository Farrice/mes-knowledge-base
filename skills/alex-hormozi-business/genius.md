## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb the math-first instinct, then build originally. If the output reads like a slide deck ("Step 1: compute the Golden Number, Step 2: build the Money Model, Step 3: run the Value Equation"), you have failed — Hormozi does the arithmetic first and never narrates which framework he's running while he runs it.

The test: would Hormozi recognize this as someone doing the actual math on a real business — or as someone using his vocabulary (CAC, LTV, "Grand Slam offer") as a costume over an argument that was never priced out? If it's the second, rebuild.

Specifically:
- Do NOT label sections "here's the golden number," "here's the value equation," "here's the money model." Run the calculation; never announce the framework.
- Compute before you persuade. His own rule for himself: "absolutely married to the truth, zero about how we get there" — if 30-day gross profit doesn't clear 2x (CAC + COGS), say so before writing a single line of offer copy.
- His texture is blunt and arithmetic, not motivational. He proves claims with numbers he can defend (0 to $2.2M/month in 20 months; 24 portfolio companies cut to 10) and short, testable statements — never inspirational abstraction. Sophistication that doesn't cash-flow is the tell that you've drifted into vocabulary instead of math.
- Attack your own output before shipping it, the way he attacks his own frameworks: "how do I break this?" — whale case, skeptic case, cannibalization case. A model that survives the attack is Hormozi-grade; one that only sounds good on the page is polish standing in for arithmetic.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Client-Financed Acquisition (The Golden Number)
The single biggest contributor to Hormozi's material success: gross profit from a customer within 30 days must exceed 2x (CAC + COGS). At that threshold each customer arrives "preloaded" — his money pays for himself, his delivery, and the acquisition + delivery of the next customer. Gym Launch went 0 to $2.2M/month in 20 months on this; his gyms were cash-flow positive on opening day because pre-sales financed the buildout.
**Execute**: Compute CAC and COGS honestly (CPL ÷ conversion rate at each funnel step, then delivery cost). Sum all gross profit collected in the first 30 days across the full offer sequence — not just the front-end. If the sum < 2x (CAC + COGS), redesign the sequence (add upfront-cash attraction offer, upsell, prepay mechanism) before spending another ad dollar.
**Success Metric**: 30-day gross profit ≥ 2x (CAC + COGS); LTV:CAC on the front window of 20:1 to 30:1 makes you unbeatable in an attention auction.

### Pattern: The Money Model — Four Deliberate Buckets
A money model is a deliberate sequence of offers structured to generate the most upfront cash, the most customers, and the highest LTV in the least time. Every offer in the sequence does one of four jobs: attraction (maximize conversion + pull cash forward), upsell (maximize gross profit per customer), downsell (turn nos into yeses), continuity (buy again and again). 100-year-old companies out-compete new ones not through trends but through stronger money models — and advertising costs only go one direction: up.
**Execute**: Diagnose which problem the business actually has — demand (attraction), LTV (upsell), conversion (downsell), or retention (continuity) — then attach the offer structure that lends itself to that problem. Don't add all four at once; fix the binding one.
**Success Metric**: Each of the four buckets has at least one live, scripted offer; the sequence, not the front-end product, hits the golden number.

### Pattern: Sell at the Point of Greatest Deprivation
The steakhouse mistake: offering another steak to a full customer, then calling customers cheap. Deprivation and value creation coincide when the first loop of value creates the next problem (get someone leads → they're overwhelmed → offer to work the leads). If that loop doesn't occur on a short timeline, don't sell then. Five selling moments: immediately (same conversation), after activation, halfway, last chance, and milestone. Renewing "when you're about to stop getting paid" is the absolute worst time.
**Execute**: Map the customer journey and mark where each solved problem births the next one. Place each upsell at the deprivation point it answers — dessert when the main stomach is full, payroll service right after they hire the overseas team member.
**Success Metric**: Upsells present at moments of red-hot pain, not calendar renewals; take rates climb because the offer answers a problem the customer just became aware of.

### Pattern: Psychological Wallets (The Dessert Stomach)
His grandmother: "your main stomach's full, but your dessert stomach is empty." Customers hold separate mental budgets — the personal-training wallet may be spent while the supplement wallet, the tax-pain wallet, the insurance wallet are untouched. Corollary: it's easier to get the second yes after the first yes. Bundling the upsell into the main offer both raises price resistance and wastes the second wallet.
**Execute**: List the distinct pains/budgets your customer holds (transformation, convenience, risk, status, time). Build one offer per wallet, sold sequentially after the first yes — never folded into the core offer "to be generous."
**Success Metric**: Revenue per customer grows from sequenced wallet-taps; front-end conversion doesn't drop because the core offer stayed clean.

### Pattern: The Committed Anchor
Salt Lake City suit story: shown a $16K suit first (fully sold, not winked at), he gratefully bought the $2K one — 5x his budget. Two rules: (1) you must actually sell the anchor, because ~10% of buyers are whales who take it — and 10% buying something 10x the price doubles revenue; (2) the difference between anchor and core offer must be negligible to most buyers (brand, wool grade), so choosing the core feels like relief, not compromise.
**Execute**: Add a premium version priced 5-10x the core offer. Present it first with full commitment. Differentiate it only on attributes your ICP mostly doesn't care about. Let the core offer become the "thank God" option.
**Success Metric**: ~10% whale take-rate on the anchor; average order value up 50-100% with no drop in total conversions.

### Pattern: No-Based Upsell Scripting
"Do you want anything else?" forces the customer to consider a new purchase — binary, high friction. Reposition so yes is expressed by saying no: "What most people do is X — do you want to skip that?" The classic frame is "you can't have X without Y" (burger without fries, framed art without insurance, overseas hires without payroll compliance). Tiny repositionings move upsell take rates to 80-90%.
**Execute**: For each upsell, write the script so the default path includes it and declining requires action. Frame it as making the first decision *more* sound, not as a new decision.
**Success Metric**: Upsell take rates of 80-90% versus the 20-30% of ask-based offers.

### Pattern: Sell the Transformation, Not the Membership
Competing gyms sold $99/month memberships — a cost to the customer. Hormozi sold a $500 "win your money back" challenge — a transformation promise with a stake. Attraction offers work when the customer buys the happy ending, not access. Downsells carry a mirrored risk: done wrong, they let full-price buyers slide into the cheap thing. Feature downsells (same outcome, fewer features — "someone Nick trained, one-third the price") capture the no's without cannibalizing the yeses.
**Execute**: Rewrite the front-end as an outcome with skin in the game (challenge, giveaway of the flagship, win-your-money-back). Design downsells by removing features/prestige, never by discounting the identical thing.
**Success Metric**: Front-end price 5x category norm with equal or better conversion; downsell buyers are provably people who declined the core offer first.

### Pattern: Focus = What You Say No To
Focus is the quality and quantity of things you say no to; strategy is prioritization of limited resources against unlimited opportunities. His forcing question: "If only ONE thing happened in 12 months, what would make every other goal accomplished or irrelevant?" He cut 24 portfolio companies to 10 (taking losses) and did one deal instead of twelve — and the portfolio outperformed. To employees drowning in tasks: "Cool. Don't do it. I'm the boss. It's fine if you just do this."
**Execute**: Name the one 12-month objective. List everything currently consuming resources; kill or pause everything that doesn't feed it — including profitable distractions. Cascade: every team member gets their own single highest-value thing.
**Success Metric**: One written company objective; measurable deletions (projects killed, deals declined); throughput on the one thing visibly accelerates.

### Pattern: Validity x Utility + "How Do I Break This?"
Two axes for any framework or claim: is it true (and in how many situations), and is it useful? "Sometimes things happen, sometimes they don't" — valid, useless. Most sales lore — useful, invalid. The work is distilling the principle that survives every scenario. His test: attack his own model — "how do I break this?" — and only ship when he can't. He holds zero loyalty to his ideas: "absolutely married to the truth, zero about how we get there."
**Execute**: Before shipping any framework, offer, or strategic claim, run both filters. Then generate the strongest counter-scenarios; if one breaks it, find the nuance/through-line and revise. Ship only unbreakable versions.
**Success Metric**: The framework survives adversarial cases across contexts (one-to-one/one-to-many, known/unknown audience); it's stated in a "fight me" form.

### Pattern: Skills Are the Ultimate Hedge
If you only have an extra $1,000, invest it in skills, not markets: "skills are the hedge against inflation, against currency changes... if you have value to exchange, the world will exchange for it." Every self-made billionaire started with the same ingredients — nothing, and nothing to lose: "the lottery ticket's free and you can keep cashing it in until you win." Don't conflate what winners do now (fly private) with what it took to get there (12 bad deals before the one good one).
**Execute**: When allocating scarce money or time, route it to the skill that raises earning capacity in the current constraint (sales, offers, media). Buy the reps, not the symbols of arrival.
**Success Metric**: Measurable capability gain that shows up in unit economics (higher close rate, cheaper CAC, higher prices held) within a quarter.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: Sales Exist to Buy Customers
**Insight**: "We don't get customers to make sales. We make sales to get customers." The transaction is the acquisition cost of a relationship with recurring purchase likelihood — inverting how nearly every operator treats the front-end.
**Deploy**: Judge every front-end offer by the quality and volume of relationships it creates, not its own margin. A break-even front-end that loads continuity beats a profitable one-off.

### Insight: The Waived-Fee Continuity Close
**Insight**: For expensive services, a "wave fee" converts a price objection into a commitment: "It's $10K upfront — or I can waive it if you commit to a year." The customer feels they won; you bought twelve months of LTV with money you may never have collected anyway.
**Deploy**: Attach a real, defensible setup/finder's fee to any high-ticket service, then trade it for term commitment at the close.

### Insight: The Giveaway Attraction Offer Qualifies at the Top
**Insight**: Raffling the flagship (the "absolute savage" hire, paid for a year) generates massive demand aimed at your *most expensive* product — so every non-winner is a pre-qualified lead for a "partial scholarship" version rolled straight into continuity.
**Deploy**: When demand is the constraint, give away one unit of the ideal offer; treat the entrant list as hot pipeline for a discounted-then-continuity path.

### Insight: Table of Contents Is the Product
**Insight**: His books are limited by ability to promote, not write (20+ outlined). The TOC is where the real work happens — it's the game plan; every chapter then runs the same skeleton: story for context, short description, plentiful examples, "Alex notes." Writing is two-thirds discovery: the fun is finding the through-line between two true-but-conflicting ideas.
**Deploy**: For any long-form asset, spend disproportionate time on structure before prose; write words first, mark visuals as placeholders, and mine idea-conflicts for the framework that resolves them.

### Insight: Season of No
**Insight**: Deep work is calendar architecture, not willpower: he only writes on days with six-plus empty hours (the Zeigarnik effect — one afternoon meeting poisons the whole day), compresses all meetings into two days, and declares an explicit "season of no" to his team.
**Deploy**: For any maker-mode push, block full empty days rather than daily slots; announce the season so the environment enforces it.

### Insight: The Math of Bad Days
**Insight**: His resilience stack is arithmetic plus reframe: a standard distribution guarantees bottom-10% days ("if I didn't have these I couldn't recognize the top 10%"); the utilitarian test — "what does this actually change about what I do?" (a surprise multi-8-figure bill changed nothing about where he lived, ate, or worked); and big goals imply equally large obstacles that can take years, so the job is stringing good days inside bad seasons.
**Deploy**: When a client or project takes a hit, run the utilitarian test first, size the obstacle honestly against the goal's timescale, and define progress as "a little bit done every day."

### Insight: Prune the Portfolio, Feed the Stallions
**Insight**: He dumped 14 of 24 portfolio companies at a loss and the portfolio still outperformed the prior year — because bandwidth is the scarce asset. His top performers all came from relationships built *before* any deal, so he manufactured that accident on purpose (monthly workshops as a farm system).
**Deploy**: Rank everything by contribution per unit of attention; cut the bottom ruthlessly even at a paper loss, and build systematic pre-deal relationship pipelines for whatever "deal" means in your context.
