# Jenny Hoyos — Genius Context

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: The VIRAL Framework
Every short follows five moves: **V**isual shock (the open must be visually arresting — entertainment niches use a visual challenge; educational niches use a physically staged analogy like colored-water bowls or the golf-balls-in-a-jar jar), **I**mmediately start (no context, no character motivation — open at near-climax "peak action," unlike movies), **R**ising action (state the question or problem the viewer is watching to resolve), **A**nticipation (drip hints, clues, puzzle pieces — never the answer early), **L**asting payoff (a concise ending where the answer to the opening question is the literal last word: ask "what's 2+2?" and the final word of the video is "four" — any CTA goes *before* the answer, because viewers leave the instant they hear it).
**Execute**: Storyboard the five letters as five boxes before writing a single line. If the V box is empty — no visual shock, no staged analogy — the idea is not ready; find the visual first.
**Success Metric**: Video opens within 2 seconds of peak action; answer lands as the final word; no viewer-visible CTA after the payoff.

### Pattern: But/So Storytelling (Kill "And Then")
Linear recounting ("we boil the water and then add pasta and then add salt") is retention poison. Every beat must connect through conflict ("but that's way too much salt") or consequence ("so I'll add more water"). Progression must be constant but non-linear — the viewer should never be able to predict the next beat.
**Execute**: Draft the beat list, then audit every connective. Each "and then" gets rewritten as a "but" (something went wrong / surprised us) or a "so" (we responded). Inject mini-conflicts even in mundane processes — raw pasta, wrong measurements, a skeptical mom.
**Success Metric**: Zero "and then" connectives in the final script; at least 2-3 genuine conflict beats per 30 seconds.

### Pattern: Curiosity Gap or No Video
The idea itself must contain an open question the viewer needs answered. "How I make my coffee" is dead; "the coffee that keeps me awake 24 hours" forces the watch. Question-shaped idea formats are the strongest: "Is it possible to ___?" and "What happens if ___?" — statements don't create gaps, questions do.
**Execute**: Before production, write the idea as a question. If no genuine question exists — or if the viewer wouldn't care about the answer — kill the idea. When using AI for ideation, constrain it to question-form hooks so it can't return flat statements.
**Success Metric**: The idea can be stated as a question a stranger would want answered; gathering ~100 candidate ideas and shooting only the one with the strongest gap.

### Pattern: Cold-Open the Ending
The best hooks tease the finale: show the finished, incredible-looking dish first, then the whole video proves how you got there. Shock is the scroll-stopper; curiosity ("how did they make THAT?") is the retention engine. On long-form, this same logic lives in the title/thumbnail — and the video's job becomes *proving* the title while adding a bonus reason to stay (the B-plot).
**Execute**: Pull the single craziest or most impressive frame of the video and open with it. Then structure everything after as the build-up to that moment, cutting back to real-time just before the reveal.
**Success Metric**: First frame is the video's peak visual; viewed-vs-swiped ≥80%.

### Pattern: You-Before-Me Wording
Identical sentences with reordered emphasis produce different retention. Selfish content ("I'm training for a marathon") loses; audience-first framing ("here's how YOU should train for your next marathon — because this is what I'm doing") wins. The best creators (Dan Martell's tip → application → personal story order) always open on the viewer's payoff.
**Execute**: Audit the first two lines of every script. If "I/my" appears before "you/your," rewrite the opener so the viewer's outcome leads and the creator's story trails as proof.
**Success Metric**: Viewer benefit stated before any first-person framing in the first 5 seconds.

### Pattern: Visible Progression Mechanisms
Viewers leave when they can't feel the end approaching. Give them a progress bar: on-screen countdown timers, ingredient checklists with checkmarks, "method 1/2/3" counters, first/second/third language. Equally: ban conclusional or dread language mid-video — "finally," "sit back, this is going to take a while" — both trigger the swipe.
**Execute**: Choose one mechanism per video that matches the format (timer for time challenges, checklist for recipes, counter for listicles) and keep it persistently on screen. Sweep the script for conclusional/dread words.
**Success Metric**: Retention graph shows no slow continuous slope (the signature of unclear progression); mechanism visible within first 10 seconds.

### Pattern: One Moment, Few Beats
A short is not a compressed movie — it's a single scene: point A→B→C, while the channel travels A→Z. Best short-form challenges last ~10 minutes to an hour of real time, never 3 days (multi-day = too many beats = summary-speak). The tier-list move: one fruit per short, not all fruits in one. Long-form is the inverse: a bunch of shorts stitched under one theme with a B-plot (Ryan Trahan's 50-states A-plot + charity B-plot) to sustain investment.
**Execute**: Cut every beat that isn't required to answer the opening question. If the story needs more than ~5 beats, it's a long-form idea or a series of standalone shorts — split it.
**Success Metric**: Script fits 30-60s at conversational pace without fast-talking; every beat traceably serves the opening question.

### Pattern: Aspirational Yet Relatable Characters
Spider-Man works because he's aspirational (superpowers) *and* relatable (Peter Parker). Only aspirational = cold; only relatable = nothing special. Multiple characters let every viewer find someone to identify with — pair opposite appeals ("one's the cookie, the other's the cream"): Jenny does the cool/messy aspirational thing, her mom reacts with relatable exasperation. Characters also solve pacing — a reaction beat gives viewers time to digest without stalling progression. Anyone works: family, friends, strangers, animals, even a named candle with a personality.
**Execute**: Cast one aspirational force and one relatable/opposing force per video. Use reaction shots as comprehension pauses after dense information.
**Success Metric**: Each character carries a distinct appeal; mid-video comprehension pauses exist without dead air.

### Pattern: High Demand, Low Supply (Niche Math + Outlier Hunting)
Views come from market position before craft: a B-level creator in a C-level niche beats an A-level creator fighting MrBeast. Find demand-supply gaps; a video "could suck" and still win if nobody else has made it. For ideation, hunt outliers — channels under 1K subscribers with 100K+ view videos prove the *idea* is strong independent of the creator. And the algorithm is the audience: 10 football videos then a vlog confuses distribution; one avatar, always (make videos for your younger self, a cousin, or your future self if unsure).
**Execute**: Before committing to a niche, map demand (search/watch appetite) vs. supply (how good is the existing content). For weekly ideation, scan small channels for outlier view-to-sub ratios and recreate winning ideas with a twist. Filter every idea against a single named avatar.
**Success Metric**: A niche where you're clearly the best available option; a baseline viewership that rises after each outlier hit; zero off-avatar posts.

### Pattern: Rollercoaster Pacing With a Slow Reveal
One rollercoaster loop per short: first 10-15 seconds fast and dense (front-load 2 of 5 tips), middle at medium pace, and the moments before the reveal deliberately slow — suspense language, slow motion, cutting back to the creator's nervous face before showing the result. People are most engaged right before the payoff *because* of the deceleration. But never so fast that viewers get confused — confusion, not boredom, is the top hidden kill switch.
**Execute**: Mark the reveal moment in the edit, then stretch the 3-5 seconds before it (slow-mo, held breath, restated stakes) while compressing everything in the first third.
**Success Metric**: Retention graph rises or plateaus into the reveal instead of decaying; no confusion-driven cliff mid-video.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: Every Second Is Exponential
**Insight**: One wasted second in a short doesn't cost 1% retention — the cost is exponential, because the video is 30 seconds, not 100. This is why Jenny "manipulates" footage constantly: matching planning-audio over action footage, recording ADR with the same lav mic after the fact so narration sounds live, and running dual narrative (telling a story that *complements* the visuals instead of describing them — "my mom is so mad at me" over room-cleaning footage) so audio and video never say the same thing at the same time.
**Deploy**: In the edit, hunt every second where audio merely describes visible action — overlay it, ADR it, or cut it. Never show "announce, then do" in sequence; compress to simultaneous show-and-tell.

### Insight: The Drop Is Never Where the Drop Is
**Insight**: On retention graphs, a steep 1-2 second cliff wasn't caused by what was said in those seconds — viewers were already deciding. The cause lives a few seconds *earlier* (and two separate drops often share one cause: the same off-putting thing alluded to twice). Full triage order: check viewed-vs-swiped first (isolates the hook as yes/no), then read the graph for three signatures — (A) early exodus = topic/hook, (B) point drops = specific mistake seconds earlier, (C) slow slope = progression unclear.
**Deploy**: When diagnosing any underperformer, resolve the hook question first with viewed-vs-swiped (target 80%+, floor 70%), then scrub to a few seconds before each drop and name the actual trigger before changing anything.

### Insight: Familiarity Beats Novelty
**Insight**: The intuition "viewers will get bored of the same set" is backwards. Friends shot the same coffee shop at the same angle for a decade — familiarity is comforting and repeat-watchable, and one fixed filming spot (Jenny's kitchen) makes production muscle-memory cheap. The same principle powers formats: a repeatable format with one variable swapped (non-episodic series — testing a different celebrity's warm-up routine each video, no continuity required) compounds instead of resetting.
**Deploy**: Lock one filming location and one repeatable format before optimizing anything else; vary a single variable per video and let everything else stay recognizably the same.

### Insight: Non-Scripted Beats Scripted When the Gap Is Real
**Insight**: Jenny doesn't script — she picks curiosity gaps where *she* doesn't know the outcome, so genuine suspense is guaranteed ("if I don't know what's going to happen, the viewers definitely don't"). She films *for the edit*: multiple sound bites of the same moment, spoken editor's notes mid-take, then assembles the story in post — beginners can use the sticky-note method (one note per real event, remove the boring ones, rearrange for maximum progression and suspense before touching the timeline).
**Deploy**: For experience-driven content, choose tests whose outcome you genuinely can't predict; over-capture sound bites during filming; story-edit on paper (or sticky notes) before opening the editor.

### Insight: The 50-Page Brain Upload
**Insight**: Jenny's AI leverage comes from context, not prompts: she wrote a 50-page SOP covering her entire pipeline (ideation → scripting → filming → editing, plus avatar, mission, and channel map) and loaded it into a custom GPT that she treats as a team member — 1 idea → 10 execution variants, hook ranking with reasons, visual brainstorming, beat-list critique. The tool is only as good as the operating manual behind it. Same discipline applies to production polish: generative fill runs on *every* video to strip background distraction, heat-map style — nothing "red" outside the focal point, heaviest cleanup on the hook frame.
**Deploy**: Document the full content SOP once (process + avatar + mission + reference channels) and feed it to the AI before asking for ideas; constrain outputs to question-form hooks; treat the hook frame's visual cleanliness as a separate QA pass.
