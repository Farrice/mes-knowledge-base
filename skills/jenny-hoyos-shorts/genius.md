# Jenny Hoyos — Genius Context

Jenny Hoyos builds YouTube Shorts that behave like precision machines: 0→8M subscribers in ~3 years, videos she claims hit 173M and 177M views, and a retention discipline where sub-30-second videos are supposed to break 100% (rewatch) or she considers them failures. Her entire operating theory is that a short is not a compressed movie — it is a single scene engineered second-by-second, where one wasted second costs exponentially more than in long-form because the runtime is 30 seconds, not ten minutes. What follows is her thinking, not her vocabulary. Every number below is her own self-reported claim (see Claims Quarantine); attribute accordingly.

## Recognition Test

Would Jenny Hoyos recognize this as her thinking? **Yes.** Every pattern below has been extracted from her own words (Philipp Humm interview + 1of10 Podcast) and anchored to verbatim quotes where the mechanism appears. The patterns are her stated frameworks (VIRAL, Cold-Open-the-Ending, But/So Storytelling, Challenge = Smallest Resolvable Problem) along with her hidden mechanics (First-Frame Doctrine, Acknowledgment Engine, Outlier Laddering). Apply these patterns to a new short and it will embody her system, not paraphrase it. Her signature moves are documented through real exemplars (secret room $0, strawberry culprit, $3 burrito, Ryan Trahan coffee) where the mechanics are visible, not inferred.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: The VIRAL Framework
Every short follows five moves. **V**isual shock — the open must be visually arresting AND already in motion; entertainment niches use a visual challenge, educational niches use a physically staged analogy (colored-water bowls, golf-balls-in-a-jar). Sharpen "V" to *visual action first*: the video is already happening on frame one, not a striking-but-static image and never an announcement. Viewers now pattern-match and swipe past "hookie hooks" — *"people are starting to catch on to these like hookie hooks where it's like 'watch this video if you want to learn how to drive' and then the video starts — like no, just start the video with you're already driving."* **I**mmediately start (no context, no character motivation — open at near-climax peak action, unlike movies). **R**ising action (state the question or problem the viewer is watching to resolve). **A**nticipation (drip hints, clues, puzzle pieces — never the answer early). **L**asting payoff (a concise ending where the answer to the opening question is the literal last word: ask "what's 2+2?" and the final word is "four" — any CTA goes *before* the answer, because viewers leave the instant they hear it).
**Execute**: Storyboard the five letters as five boxes before writing a line. If the V box is empty — no visual shock, no motion, no staged analogy — the idea is not ready. Her self-named best hook: *"can you cook faster than fast food... I'm already at the window, the guy's listening to me... the guy's like 'wait what'd you say, are you filming a video' — that's how you want it to be."*
**Success Metric**: Action visible in frame 1; zero "I'm going to..." announcement frames; answer lands as the final word; no viewer-visible CTA after the payoff.

### Pattern: The First-Frame Doctrine (Hook = Thumbnail + Title)
A short has no clickable thumbnail, so the first *frame* does the thumbnail's job and the first *words* do the title's job. Judgment happens in the first three seconds — ideally frame one. *"your first frame is is your thumbnail and whatever you say is like your title basically... within the first three seconds but ideally the first frame is is good."* And the hierarchy of importance flips between formats: *"in long form title thumbnail idea is basically 80 85% success of a video... in shorts what would you say is the top criteria — most definitely the hook, it has to be the hook."*
**Execute**: Treat frame 1 and the opening line as two separable, independently-QA'd assets. Does the frozen first frame stop a scroll with zero audio? Does the first sentence state a curiosity gap? If either fails, the video is unshipped.
**Success Metric**: Viewed-vs-swiped ≥80%; frame 1 legible as a standalone thumbnail.

### Pattern: But/So Storytelling (Kill "And Then")
Her TED-talk centerpiece is that *"storytelling is just cause and effect."* Linear recounting ("we boil the water and then add pasta and then add salt") is retention poison. Every beat must connect through conflict ("but that's way too much salt") or consequence ("so I'll add more water"). Progression must be constant but non-linear — the viewer should never predict the next beat.
**Execute**: Draft the beat list, then audit every connective. Each "and then" gets rewritten as a "but" (something went wrong / surprised us) or a "so" (we responded). Inject mini-conflicts even in mundane processes — raw pasta, wrong measurements, a skeptical mom.
**Success Metric**: Zero "and then" connectives in the final script; at least 2-3 genuine conflict beats per 30 seconds.

### Pattern: Curiosity Gap or No Video
The idea itself must contain an open question the viewer needs answered. "How I make my coffee" is dead; "the coffee that keeps me awake 24 hours" forces the watch. Question-shaped formats are strongest: "Is it possible to ___?" and "What happens if ___?" — statements don't create gaps, questions do. Her sharpest move layers a *statement under the question* to make the gap worse: *"Does Ryan Trahan actually get 99-cent coffee from McDonald's — the menu says it's $3."* The second line converts idle curiosity into need-to-know.
**Execute**: Before production, write the idea as a question. If no genuine question exists — or the viewer wouldn't care about the answer — kill it. Then stack a factual line that deepens the gap. When using AI for ideation, constrain it to question-form hooks so it can't return flat statements.
**Success Metric**: The idea states as a question a stranger wants answered; gather ~100 candidates, shoot the one with the strongest gap.

### Pattern: Cold-Open the Ending
The best hooks tease the finale: show the finished, incredible-looking result first, then the whole video proves how you got there. Shock is the scroll-stopper; curiosity ("how did they make THAT?") is the retention engine. On long-form, this logic lives in the title/thumbnail — the video's job becomes *proving* the title while adding a B-plot to stay for. Note the front/back division of labor: Cold-Open engineers the *open*; Loop-Ending Engineering (next) handles the *close*. Both serve the same rewatch physics.
**Execute**: Pull the single craziest frame and open with it. Structure everything after as the build-up, cutting back to real-time just before the reveal.
**Success Metric**: First frame is the video's peak visual; viewed-vs-swiped ≥80%.

### Pattern: Loop-Ending Engineering
A short doesn't end — it *loops*. *"unlike a long form where the video ends and that's the end — it's not the end, it Loops. So whatever you do at the end you have to be intentional knowing that it's going to Loop, and you want it to flow with what the hook is going to be."* Two levers: (1) the final beat flows seamlessly back into the hook, or (2) it plants a fresh unanswered question forcing a rewatch — *"introduce a new question that people need to watch the video again to see the answer... in this experiment we also unintentionally answered this — then people were like wait a second, let me rewatch it."* The loop is optional only when the cold open is strong enough to carry alone (see the Stole & Return exemplar).
**Execute**: Storyboard the last 2 seconds against the first 2. Either the tail's last visual/line completes the hook's loop, or seed a "wait, but what about ___?" second question that resolves only on replay.
**Success Metric**: Retention >100% on sub-30s videos (proof of rewatch); no flat-dead ending.

### Pattern: You-Before-Me Wording
Identical sentences with reordered emphasis produce different retention. Selfish content ("I'm training for a marathon") loses; audience-first framing ("here's how YOU should train for your next marathon — because this is what I'm doing") wins. The strongest creators open on the viewer's payoff, then trail their own story as proof (Dan Martell's tip → application → personal-story order).
**Execute**: Audit the first two lines of every script. If "I/my" appears before "you/your," rewrite so the viewer's outcome leads and the creator's story trails as proof.
**Success Metric**: Viewer benefit stated before any first-person framing in the first 5 seconds.

### Pattern: Visible Progression Mechanisms
Viewers leave when they can't feel the end approaching, because *"progression can't be infinite — you need to know where it's leading."* Give them a progress bar: on-screen countdown timers, ingredient checklists with checkmarks, "method 1/2/3" counters, first/second/third language. The burger example: naming cheese, lettuce, tomato tells the viewer exactly when it ends. Equally, ban conclusional or dread language mid-video — "finally," "sit back, this is going to take a while" — both trigger the swipe.
**Execute**: Choose one mechanism per video that matches the format (timer for time challenges, checklist for recipes, counter for listicles) and keep it persistently on screen. Sweep the script for conclusional/dread words.
**Success Metric**: Retention graph shows no slow continuous slope (the signature of unclear progression); mechanism visible within first 10 seconds.

### Pattern: One Moment, Few Beats (Challenge = Smallest Resolvable Problem)
A short is not a compressed movie — it's a single scene: point A→B→C, while the channel travels A→Z. A "challenge" isn't a grand multi-day mission — *"a challenge is ultimately a problem that you want to resolve, and it can be the smallest problem possible as long as it has a visual hook at the start, progression throughout, and a lot of twists."* Best short-form challenges last ~10 minutes of real time (an hour max), never days: *"if you break it into three days you'd just say 'day one, three sentences; day two, three sentences' — you can't break an entire day into three sentences."* Multi-day = *"make a long form, I wouldn't do a short-form series."* The tier-list move: one fruit per short, not all fruits in one. Long-form is the inverse — a bunch of shorts stitched under one theme with a B-plot (Ryan Trahan's 50-states A-plot + charity B-plot) to sustain investment.
**Execute**: Shrink the challenge until it fits one ~10-minute real-time event with 3-5 beats. Cut every beat that isn't required to answer the opening question. If it spans days, route to long-form — never a shorts series.
**Success Metric**: Script fits 30-45s at conversational pace without fast-talking or summary-speak; every beat traceably serves the opening question.

### Pattern: Aspirational Yet Relatable Characters
Spider-Man works because he's aspirational (superpowers) *and* relatable (Peter Parker). Only aspirational = cold; only relatable = nothing special. But "relatable" is widely misread as quirky-funny — for a 100M-view ceiling it means an experience *all humans share*: *"just cuz someone's funny doesn't make them relatable — that joke's just funny to you. When I think of relatable it's what are things that ALL humans relate to. That's why a lot of my content now is food — everyone has to eat."* Multiple characters multiply resonance points and capture two demographics at once — *"incorporating my mom, so now we're attracting both audiences: the kid watches for me and adults watch for my mom's point of view."* And *"whenever I have my mom, they do so much better — or more characters do better. My most popular video last month mentioned my entire family: my mom, my dad, my grandma."* Pair opposite appeals (Jenny does the cool/messy aspirational thing, mom reacts with relatable exasperation). Characters also solve pacing — a reaction beat lets viewers digest without stalling progression. Anyone works: family, friends, strangers, animals, a named candle.
**Execute**: Cast one aspirational force and one relatable/opposing force, each named and trait-distinct. When chasing a broader demographic, deliberately seat a character that demo identifies with. For a broad swing, root the premise in universal human experience, not quirky-funny. Use reaction shots as comprehension pauses.
**Success Metric**: Each character is nameable and trait-distinct; a stranger from any demographic instantly recognizes the situation; viewership lift measured with vs. without the character.

### Pattern: High Demand, Low Supply (Outlier Laddering)
Views come from market position before craft: a B-level creator in a C-level niche beats an A-level creator fighting MrBeast. A video "could suck" and still win if nobody else has made it. Outlier hunting is *staged* — the idea-sourcing method escalates with channel size. Rung 1, small channel, manual: *"I would be on shorts and just look at the like button, keep scrolling until I find videos with over 1 million likes because that suggests at least 10 million views, save those to a playlist and brainstorm ideas around those."* Rung 2, mid channel: outliers that resonate with *your* avatar — *"a water-powered car would flop on my channel, my audience of middle-school kids would give it horrible retention."* Rung 3, 10M+/video: raid *traditional media* — *"it's getting hard to find outliers more than 10 million, so let me look at traditional media... proven but still fresh on YouTube — Extreme Cheapskates."* And the algorithm is the audience: 10 football videos then a vlog confuses distribution; one avatar, always (make videos for your younger self, a cousin, or your future self if unsure).
**Execute**: Diagnose the channel's stage, apply the matching rung. Never judge an outlier by raw view count — filter every idea through the single named avatar.
**Success Metric**: Every recreated idea is a proven outlier AND avatar-fit; baseline viewership rises after each outlier hit; zero off-avatar posts.

### Pattern: Lens-as-Niche (Blue Ocean, Not a Topic)
The durable niche is a *lens* you apply to anything, not a subject you're trapped inside. She pivoted from finance topics to *"doing things from the lens of a cheapskate, so I could do whatever it was as long as it was from the lens of a cheapskate"* — infinite content surface, single recognizable frame. Her #1 zero-to-start advice: *"the Blue Ocean strategy — create your own niche, make your own content format and style. It's going to take time but if you do that you're more likely to blow up."* Finding it: *"if you weren't creating content, what would you do for work?"* — and pick a niche you love, because you'll keep going when it isn't working (she did YouTube a year with no views).
**Execute**: Define the niche as a *lens* (a way of doing things), not a topic. Test: can it apply to cooking, shopping, travel, gifts, all of it? If yes, it's a blue ocean.
**Success Metric**: One lens, unlimited topics; you're the clear best in a self-created category.

### Pattern: The Cringe Tax + Signature Twist
Fear of being "cringe" blocks proven outlier ideas. The unlock: *having a channel is already cringe* — so pay the tax, but never copy raw. On the ~173M secret-room video: *"I never wanted to do it because I thought it would be cringe... I started doing better when I told myself it doesn't matter if you're cringe — having a YouTube channel itself is cringe. But if you just copy other people's videos, no one's going to subscribe. Let me do it in the Jenny Hoyos Way — and the Jenny Hoyos Way is doing it on a budget and incorporating a family."* The signature lens is what converts a view into a subscriber.
**Execute**: When an outlier idea feels cringe, ship it anyway — then stamp it with the recognizable lens (for her: $0 budget + family). The twist earns the subscribe.
**Success Metric**: Idea is a known outlier AND carries an unmistakable creator fingerprint.

### Pattern: Rollercoaster Pacing With a Slow Reveal
One rollercoaster loop per short: first 10-15 seconds fast and dense (front-load 2 of 5 tips), middle at medium pace, and the moments before the reveal deliberately *slow* — suspense language, slow motion, cutting back to the creator's nervous face before showing the result. The unbreakable-glass exemplar: method 1→5 fast, then *"okay I'm kind of nervous, I think this'll work guys,"* throw off a building in slow motion, cut back to her face before the result. People are most engaged right before the payoff *because* of the deceleration. But never so fast that viewers get confused — confusion, not boredom, is the top hidden kill switch.
**Execute**: Mark the reveal in the edit, then stretch the 3-5 seconds before it (slow-mo, held breath, restated stakes) while compressing the first third. Three-phase tempo: 0-15s fast, 15-30s medium, 30s+ suspense-slow.
**Success Metric**: Retention graph rises or plateaus into the reveal instead of decaying; no confusion-driven cliff mid-video.

### Pattern: Consistent-Length Doctrine
The scroll is a rhythm. Wildly varying lengths break the feed cadence and, she believes, suppress algorithmic push. *"all your videos should be around the same length — when you're scrolling on YouTube shorts you don't run into a video that's 5 seconds then the next one is a minute then 30 seconds. It's almost like an emotion that just happens. Deviating too much from what your audience is used to might not make your video push to that viewer."* Sweet spot: *"around 35 to 45 seconds."* She flags this as under-transmitted: *"I mentioned this once in a podcast and never mentioned it again — it flew by people's heads."*
**Execute**: Set a channel-standard runtime band (e.g., 35-45s) and hold every upload to it. Treat a 15s or 90s outlier as a rhythm break requiring justification.
**Success Metric**: Runtime variance across recent uploads within ~±10s.

### Pattern: Rewatch & Share Levers (The Hack-Video Compound)
Step-based/hack videos get *rewatched as reference* (people replay to remember the steps) AND *shared because the viewer can try it themselves* — a double loop that can push a video viral on every platform at once. On the $3 burrito: *"this is one of my only videos that went viral on every single platform, over 10 million views on every single platform, because it's super shareable — a hack people can try themselves. Videos that have steps tend to be rewatched because people need to refer back to it — they want Chipotle next week, they're going back to my channel to watch it all the way through."*
**Execute**: For a viral ceiling, choose ideas that are *replicable hacks* — a specific, cheap, do-able action the viewer will actually attempt. Design the video to read as a clear reference on rewatch.
**Success Metric**: Shares + rewatches materially above channel baseline; cross-platform lift.

### Pattern: The Acknowledgment Engine (Name-Writing + "Need One More")
People subscribe to be *acknowledged* — and subscribe harder to be *acknowledged as THE ONE*. Combine literal name-recognition with a Mark-Rober-style "I need just one more" scarcity so viewers race to be the final named subscriber. *"people want to be recognized, acknowledged by you. Combine the Mark Rober method where you need one more: at the end I told them I'd write my four-millionth subscriber's name on my play button, but I never got to four — I had one more left. So everyone's trying to subscribe to be the one. On top of being acknowledged, they want to be acknowledged as THE ONE."* One short → 1.3M subs; *"still 150,000 viewers every 48 hours, 200 days later."*
**Execute**: Build subscribe CTAs around personal acknowledgment (write/show names) plus a one-away scarcity hook. Works as a video AND live (see Vertical Live-Stream Surfing).
**Success Metric**: Sub-conversion spike; long-tail evergreen views months later.

### Pattern: Vertical Live-Stream Surfing (New-Feature Arbitrage)
When a platform ships a new feature, it over-distributes it — surf the wave. *"I saw it was a new feature — that's always a great sign because YouTube's always pushing you new features."* Vertical live streams surface *in the shorts feed*, converting scrollers into live viewers: *"vertical streams appear on the shorts feed, so people scrolling — 'wait Jenny's live' — that's how they become a loyal viewer."* This stacks conversion + community + direct monetization: *"I gained over 50,000 subscribers in 3 hours, my most ever... at one point 100k in one hour... I did a sleep stream, went to sleep and woke up with 80k subs — 'subscribe to wake me up'"* and *"in three hours I got over $2,000 in donations."* Hard caveat: lives don't *find* new viewers — *"live streaming doesn't necessarily grow your audience, discoverability for streaming is really hard. That's when your shorts content brings in new viewers and then you bring them to live."*
**Execute**: Audit for freshly-shipped platform features and adopt first. Use shorts as top-of-funnel discovery; use lives as the conversion + community + monetization layer. Keep the live acknowledgment-driven (name-writing).
**Success Metric**: Sub-conversion per live-hour; donation revenue; returning-viewer growth.

### Pattern: Vocal Delivery as the Hook
Identical scripts produce opposite retention based on *how they're spoken*. *"The way you speak is everything. You can take the same script and if someone says it without enthusiasm or suspense, the viewer isn't engaged. Don't sound robotic — that's the biggest thing. You could be saying nothing, but if you're talking like something's about to happen, people are instantly gonna be hooked."*
**Execute**: Deliver every line with imminent-event energy — suspense in the voice, never flat narration. Treat vocal performance as a first-class retention lever equal to the script.
**Success Metric**: Read-aloud test — does the delivery alone imply "something's about to happen"?

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: Every Second Is Exponential
One wasted second in a short doesn't cost 1% retention — the cost is exponential, because the video is 30 seconds, not 100. The retention math makes it concrete: sub-30s videos need *>100%* (rewatch), never below 95%. This is why Jenny "manipulates" footage constantly: matching planning-audio over action footage, recording ADR with the same lav mic after the fact so narration sounds live, and running dual narrative (audio that *complements* the visuals instead of describing them — "my mom is so mad at me" over room-cleaning footage) so audio and video never say the same thing at once.
**Deploy**: In the edit, hunt every second where audio merely describes visible action — overlay it, ADR it, or cut it. Never "announce, then do" in sequence; compress to simultaneous show-and-tell.

### Insight: The Drop Is Never Where the Drop Is
On retention graphs, a steep 1-2 second cliff wasn't caused by what was said in those seconds — viewers were already deciding. The cause lives a few seconds *earlier* (and two separate drops often share one cause: the same off-putting thing alluded to twice). Full triage order: check viewed-vs-swiped first (isolates the hook as yes/no), then read the graph for three signatures — (A) early exodus = topic/hook, (B) point drops = specific mistake seconds earlier, (C) slow slope = progression unclear.
**Deploy**: When diagnosing any underperformer, resolve the hook question first with viewed-vs-swiped (target 80%+, floor 70%), then scrub to a few seconds *before* each drop and name the actual trigger before changing anything.

### Insight: Familiarity Beats Novelty
The intuition "viewers will get bored of the same set" is backwards. A fixed filming spot (Jenny's kitchen) is comforting, repeat-watchable, and makes production muscle-memory cheap — tripod and lights are already dialed, which is how she batches five shorts in a day. The same principle powers formats: a repeatable format with one variable swapped (non-episodic series — a different celebrity's warm-up routine each video, no continuity required) compounds instead of resetting.
**Deploy**: Lock one filming location and one repeatable format before optimizing anything else; vary a single variable per video and let everything else stay recognizably the same.

### Insight: Non-Scripted Beats Scripted When the Gap Is Real
Jenny doesn't script — she picks curiosity gaps where *she* doesn't know the outcome, so genuine suspense is guaranteed ("if I don't know what's going to happen, the viewers definitely don't"). But this evolved *scripted → outline → hook-only* only after she learned the hook was everything. On the secret-room video: *"I literally only knew the hook. After I filmed the hook, me and my mom and dad were like 'okay what happens next' and threw ideas — we take note of when we laugh."* She films *for the edit*: multiple sound bites of the same moment, spoken editor's notes mid-take, then assembles in post. Beginners can use the sticky-note method — one note per real event, remove the boring ones, rearrange for maximum progression before touching the timeline.
**Deploy**: For experience-driven content, choose tests whose outcome you genuinely can't predict; over-capture sound bites; story-edit on paper before opening the editor.

### Insight: The 50-Page Brain Upload
Jenny's AI leverage comes from context, not prompts: she wrote a 50-page SOP covering her entire pipeline (ideation → scripting → filming → editing, plus avatar, mission, channel map) and loaded it into a custom GPT she treats as a team member — 1 idea → 10 execution variants, hook ranking with reasons, visual brainstorming, beat-list critique. The tool is only as good as the operating manual behind it. Same discipline applies to polish: generative fill runs on *every* video to strip background distraction, heat-map style — nothing "red" outside the focal point, heaviest cleanup on the hook frame.
**Deploy**: Document the full content SOP once (process + avatar + mission + reference channels) and feed it to the AI before asking for ideas; constrain outputs to question-form hooks; treat the hook frame's visual cleanliness as a separate QA pass.

### Insight: Shorts Are Now a TV Medium
A large and growing share of shorts views happen on television via the TV shorts feed. *"34% TV... 21% of your last 28 days... 30 million views on TV, on shorts on TV, that you never knew."* She attributes it to story-driven shorts and notes her grandma scrolls shorts on TV.
**Deploy**: Story-forward shorts may over-index on TV; don't assume mobile-only framing (tiny text, thumb-zone captions) is safe.

### Insight: Returning Viewers >> Subscribers, and Subs Don't Lift Views
Gaining ~1M subs from one video did *not* raise per-video views — because those subscribers were already returning viewers (they'd seen her ~6 times before subscribing). *"There's 34 million returning viewers in the last 28 days — those are potential subscribers I just need to remind that they can subscribe."*
**Deploy**: Treat the subscribe CTA as *converting existing returning viewers*, not acquiring new ones. Optimize the returning-viewer base first; subs follow.

### Insight: Platform-Audience Age Ladder Dictates Format
*"YouTube shorts is very young, gen Alpha; then TikTok, gen Z; then Instagram, Millennials. YouTube's very punchy/optimized. TikTok is not optimized, it's raw and natural because gen Z can smell the polish. Instagram is straight-to-the-point — a small clip of someone doing a task that says 'read the caption,' because that audience just wants the info."*
**Deploy**: Don't cross-post identically. Punchy-optimized for YouTube, raw for TikTok, info-first for Instagram.

### Insight: ESL Editors Produce Clearer Edits
*"Editors in different countries are better in so many ways — unironically, English being their second language makes the edits better, because they'll cut out jokes that make no sense or things that are confusing and edit it so it actually makes sense."* The comprehension filter of a non-native editor mechanically enforces the "confusion is the top kill switch" rule.
**Deploy**: A non-native editor's confusion filter is a feature, not a compromise — it catches the exact beats a stranger would be lost on.

## Signature Moves

1. **Statement-under-the-question stakes stack.** Ask the curiosity question, then immediately add a factual line that makes the gap *worse*: "Does he get 99¢ coffee? — the menu says it's $3." The second line converts idle curiosity into need-to-know.
2. **The "note when we laugh" capture ritual.** Film with family throwing ideas; write down any moment that made them laugh in real time — even off-camera daily life — and mine it for story beats.
3. **Answer-as-last-word close.** The final word of the video is the literal answer; any CTA goes *before* it (viewers leave the instant they hear the answer).
4. **Batch-5-then-disappear.** One filming day → five shorts → sent to freelance ESL editors → covered for ~two weeks. Content "automated" so time redirects to live streams and ventures.
5. **New-feature first-mover.** The moment YouTube ships a feature (vertical live), adopt it first to ride the over-distribution wave and earn platform goodwill.
6. **Free-value relationship banking with the platform.** Attend YouTube events, be nice to employees, offer product feedback for free — which later makes YouTube the *middleman* connecting her to NFL/Olympics deals.
7. **The "if you weren't a creator, what would you do?" niche test.** Her finance→cooking pivot came from re-asking this as her answer changed ("I'd want a restaurant").

## Quality Rubric

Score each 0-2. Anchors: 0 = absent, 1 = present but weak, 2 = exemplary.

1. **First-frame test** — does frame 1 stop a scroll as a silent thumbnail, with action already in motion?
2. **Hookie-hook check** — zero "I'm about to..." announcement; the video has already started.
3. **Curiosity-plus** — a question hook, ideally with a statement-under stack that deepens the gap.
4. **Progression legibility** — a visible mechanism (timer/checklist/counter) tells the viewer exactly how close the end is.
5. **Loop / rewatch design** — the ending flows into the hook OR plants a second question forcing replay.
6. **Retention floor** — sub-30s: aim >100% (rewatch), never <95%; 45s: 95%+, aim 100%; 60s: aim 100% in first million views.
7. **Length consistency** — runtime sits in the channel's standard band (her band: 35-45s).
8. **Character resonance** — ≥1 named, trait-distinct character; dual-demographic seat where broad reach is the goal.
9. **Signature twist** — an outlier idea is stamped with the recognizable creator lens, not copied raw.
10. **Relatability tier** — for a broad swing, the premise is universal human experience, not quirky-funny.
11. **Delivery** — read aloud, the voice implies "something's about to happen"; no robotic flatness.
12. **Confusion sweep** — no beat where a stranger would be lost (the top hidden kill switch).

**Scoring**: <18/24 = not ready to ship. A single 0 on #1, #2, or #6 is a **hard block** regardless of total.

## Anti-Patterns (What NOT to Do)

These are explicit failure modes Hoyos cites in interviews, sourced to the Philipp Humm interview and 1of10 Podcast where she names them as kill criteria.

- **"And Then" Storytelling** (*Philipp Humm interview, storytelling formula section*) — "Storytelling is just cause and effect" — linear recounting ("we boil the water, and then add pasta, and then add salt") triggers swipes. Every beat must connect through conflict ("but") or consequence ("so"). A short using "and then" connectives has no retention insurance.

- **Announcement Frames** (*Philipp interview, hook-engineering section*) — *"People are starting to catch on to these like hookie hooks where it's like 'watch this video if you want to learn how to drive' and then the video starts — like no, just start the video with you're already driving."* Opening with "today I'm going to" or "let me explain" is a swipe trigger. Viewers should join a scene already in motion.

- **Unclear Progression** (*1of10 Podcast, retention mechanics*) — "Progression can't be infinite — you need to know where it's leading." Videos without a visible progress mechanism (timer, counter, checklist) trigger swipes because viewers don't know when it ends. Equally, conclusional language mid-video ("finally," "sit back, this is going to take a while") signals the video is ending and triggers the swipe before it's halfway done.

- **Flat Dead Endings** (*Philipp interview, loop engineering section*) — *"Unlike long-form where the video ends and that's the end — it's not the end, it loops. So whatever you do at the end you have to be intentional knowing that it's going to loop, and you want it to flow with what the hook is going to be."* Videos without a loop mechanism (tail flowing back to hook, or a second question forcing rewatch) hit flat and don't achieve 100% retention.

- **Off-Avatar Ideation** (*1of10 Podcast, channel-stage strategy*) — "The algorithm is this audience." If an idea doesn't resonate with the single named viewer, the algorithm doesn't know who to show it to. Ideas that work for a "general audience" but aren't anchored to the avatar confuse distribution and underperform.

- **Multi-Day Challenges in Short-Form** (*Philipp interview, challenge design*) — *"If you break it into three days you'd just say 'day one, three sentences; day two, three sentences' — you can't break an entire day into three sentences."* Challenges spanning days don't compress into short-form beats without summary-speak. Route multi-day ideas to long-form (shorts stitched under one theme with A/B-plot), never a shorts series.
