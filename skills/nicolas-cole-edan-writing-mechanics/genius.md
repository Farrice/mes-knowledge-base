# Nicolas Cole EDAN Writing Mechanics - Genius Context

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These four blocks are intuition primitives, not a labeling checklist. Absorb them, then diagnose or compose originally. If the output mechanically tags every sentence "[E]" "[D]" "[A]" "[N]" and stops there, you have failed the assignment — Cole's own line on this is that cycling one clean block per paragraph in fixed order is "an extremely beginner way of thinking about it" (transcript `gAVbSZHwzNU`, 00:07:42-00:07:55). The test: would Cole recognize this as someone who has internalized what each block *does to the reader* — or as someone reciting the four-word acronym? If it's the second, rebuild.

Specifically:
- Do NOT announce the classification ("this sentence is Description") in the delivered draft unless the workflow explicitly asks for a block map. Execute the diagnosis, then let the revision speak for itself.
- Do NOT treat EDAN as a drafting template (paragraph 1 = Explanation, paragraph 2 = Description...). Cole is explicit that mastery is stacking and reordering blocks like chess openers, not sequencing them like a form.
- His texture: reduce everything to "what is happening here in this sentence, in this word choice, in this paragraph... and more importantly, how is it being assembled?" (00:01:37-00:01:48). Keep the diagnostic voice concrete and assembly-minded — Lego blocks and chess pieces, not literary theory.
- Polish is the tell-class warning here too: don't fix word choice before block function and sequence are correct. Cole's practitioner rule is "revise by changing block function, sequence, and payoff. Polish comes last" — sentence-level prettifying before the blocks are right just hides a structural problem under smoother prose.

## Core Operating Question

What is this sentence or paragraph doing on the page?

Do not ask first whether it sounds good. Ask whether it is Explanation, Description, Action, or Narration, and whether that block is doing the job the piece needs.

## The Four EDAN Blocks

| Block | Function | Strong Version | Weak Version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Explanation | Gives context, backstory, logic, or meaning conditions | Gives the reader the exact context needed now or plants context that pays off later | Dumps context the reader does not yet need, explains what a scene could have implied |
| Description | Creates implied meaning through concrete sensory or situational detail | Lets the reader infer tension, emotion, status, conflict, or theme | Describes for decoration without replacing or strengthening meaning |
| Action | Creates movement, consequence, reveal, or change | Moves the piece forward, reveals intention/weakness/strength, or changes stakes | Something merely happens; motion without consequence |
| Narration | Reveals narrator/author point of view, belief, theme, or world model | Gives the piece a unique way of seeing; placed sparingly enough to retain force | Generic commentary, moralizing, or overused theme statements |

## Genius Patterns

### 1. Writing Is A Bundled Term
Cole starts by unbundling "writing" into smaller functions. He names the standard beginner advice directly — "write what you feel, write what you love, be honest on the page" — and calls it "bundled instructions" that "require a lot of subjective interpretation" (00:06:26-00:06:41). The writer cannot improve a bundled activity directly. They improve by isolating the function that is weak.

### 2. EDAN As Page Anatomy
A paragraph is not just words. It is an assembly of block functions. Cole's own practice: reading *Tomorrow, and Tomorrow, and Tomorrow* by Gabrielle Zevin sentence-by-sentence, marking each one E/D/A/N in a different colored pen or highlighter (00:02:56-00:03:49). Once blocks are labeled, the writer can see whether the page is all context, all motion, all implication, or all commentary.

### 3. Color Before Revision
The practical diagnostic move is to mark each sentence by block type before revising — with actual different-colored pens or highlighters, per Cole's own annotation habit (00:03:31-00:03:38). Classification reveals the hidden structure faster than rewriting does.

### 4. Explanation Creates Meaning Conditions
Action and description do not automatically matter. Cole's worked example: a kid yelling at his "abusive alcoholic father" reads as either collapse or "a moment of growth... a moment of redemption" depending entirely on the backstory the writer supplies (00:09:49-00:10:14). Explanation supplies the context that makes a later action or image meaningful.

### 5. Description Is Implied Meaning
Description is not ornament. Cole cites Nikolai Gogol and Anton Chekhov as the reason he loves Russian literature — "a big part of their style is they don't explain anything... they just give you a ton of very specific description in which case you have to extract the meaning" (00:11:54-00:12:22). Its highest use is replacing blunt explanation with details that let the reader infer meaning.

### 6. Action Needs Consequence
Action is movement, but movement alone is cheap. Cole's counter-example is a character who narrates four flat steps in a row ("I took a step forward and then I took another step...") — motion with zero stakes attached (00:16:12-00:16:23). Strong action changes direction, reveals character, escalates stakes, proves a claim, or activates earlier context.

### 7. Narration Is Protected Power
Narration carries point of view and theme. Cole's chess analogy: narration is "the king or queen on the chessboard" — pieces you "don't typically... use that much," reserved as "the last resort" precisely because they're "the two most valuable pieces in the game" (00:21:34-00:22:19). Overusing it makes the writer sound preachy; underusing it leaves the work without worldview.

### 8. Stacking Creates Feeling
Reader emotion comes from block order. Cole's own written example, built specifically to demonstrate the E-then-N transition: "We hadn't talked all summer, not since his mom died and my parents got divorced. And that's the thing about divorce is it's selfish because it hurts the people around you more than it hurts the people getting divorced" (00:19:57-00:20:12) — two sentences of Explanation/backstory followed by one sentence of Narration voicing an opinion. Explanation before action creates understood consequence. Action before explanation creates curiosity. Description before narration lets the reader feel before being told what the feeling means.

### 9. Deliberate Practice Beats Volume
Writing more helps beginners. Cole cites the book *Peak* directly: a golfer who plays "once a month or twice a month" for years "roughly" doesn't improve, because volume alone plateaus — the jump comes from "upgrading your mental model" and then isolating one skill to drill it (00:24:38-00:25:34, 00:26:32-00:26:48). Past the beginner phase, improvement requires isolating one block or combination, upgrading the mental model, and practicing it repeatedly.

### 10. Opener Libraries Build Mastery
Like chess openers ("a chess book that's the size of a dictionary that is nothing but openers," 00:07:22-00:07:34), EDAN sequences can be studied and stored. Cole keeps "a separate doc where I articulate that combination to myself" every time he finds a new one — opener number one, opener number two, opener number three (00:31:57-00:32:24). A writer can build a library of block combinations for tension, clarity, intimacy, momentum, authority, or implication.

## Anti-Patterns (Sourced)

Each item below is a verbatim-grounded failure mode Cole names directly in the source video (`gAVbSZHwzNU`, "The EDAN Writing Framework," published 2026-05-01, `extractions` package at `_active/harness/codex-harvest-2026-06-11/extractions/video-context/gAVbSZHwzNU/transcript.txt` — see `references/source-ledger.md`). Each closes with the recognition test: would Cole recognize the flawed output as *his* framework in use, or as vocabulary borrowed without the underlying move?

- **Mechanical block-rotation** (00:07:42-00:07:55, transcript): "it's not just, oh, I do a paragraph of explanation and then I do a paragraph of description and then I do a paragraph of action and then I do a paragraph of narration... that is an extremely beginner way of thinking about it." Cole would not recognize this as EDAN mastery — he'd recognize this as someone who learned the four labels but never got past sequencing them like a form.
- **Bundled, unactionable advice** (00:06:26-00:06:41, transcript): "write what you feel, write what you love, be honest on the page. The problem with all of those instructions is they are bundled instructions... it requires a lot of subjective interpretation." Cole would not recognize generic "write authentically" coaching as his method — he'd recognize this as the exact vague advice EDAN was built to replace.
- **Description for its own sake** (00:14:33-00:14:57, transcript): "we've all read books and stories like this — this is where the writer is describing things for the sake of describing them... there's two pages describing a doorknob... that's where description tends to go awry." Cole would not recognize ornamental, decorative description as advanced craft — he'd recognize this as description that forgot its job is to replace explanation with implied meaning.
- **Weak, non-consequential action** (00:16:09-00:16:23, transcript): "the character just goes, I took a step forward and then I took another step and then I took a third step and then I took a fourth step. Just because actions are happening don't mean that every action is consequential." Cole would not recognize motion-for-motion's-sake as his "action" block — he'd recognize this as filler that never earned its place on the page.
- **Action deployed for the sake of happening** (00:16:33-00:16:41, transcript): "you don't want to push yourself and not just go, okay, well, let me just have something happen for the sake of happening." Cole would not recognize a scene event chosen because it was easy to write as strong action — he'd recognize this as the writer taking the path of least resistance instead of asking what the action reveals or changes.
- **Narration spent like a pawn instead of a king** (00:21:43-00:22:19, transcript): narration is "the king or queen on the chessboard... the last resort," and "you're not going to move your king out in the first three moves of the game." Cole would not recognize frequent, casual theme-statements as protected narration — he'd recognize this as a writer draining the one block whose power depends on scarcity.

## Hidden Knowledge

1. A flat paragraph is often a block imbalance, not a talent problem.
2. The fastest diagnosis is "what block is missing?"
3. Explanation is not bad. Unpaid-off explanation is bad.
4. Description is only advanced when it carries meaning.
5. Small actions can hit harder than large actions when earlier context gives them weight.
6. Narration is where voice becomes worldview.
7. A writer who cannot classify a passage cannot deliberately reproduce its effect.
8. The point of reverse engineering is not admiration. It is reusable combinations.
9. A training loop should isolate one skill until the writer can produce it on demand.
10. EDAN works for fiction, nonfiction, scripts, essays, ghostwriting, and brand storytelling, but the balance changes by format.

## Hall Of Fame Exemplars (Verbatim)

### Context Makes Action Matter
Cole's own worked example, in his words: "let's say you have a situation where one character yells at another character. Well the yelling is the action, right? But yelling in and of itself isn't necessarily bad or good... And so, for example, if it's like the a kid who has an abusive alcoholic father and the kid finally musters up the courage to yell at the alcoholic father, right? That is a moment of growth. That is a moment of redemption." (00:09:27-00:10:05, transcript). One action, two possible readings — context is what decides which.

### Description Replaces Explanation — The Doorknob Warning
> "Description is that it should it should in some way replace it is the more advanced version of explanation... there's two pages describing a doorknob. Like that's where description tends to go awry."
> — Cole, 00:14:44-00:14:57, transcript

The failure mode named directly: decorative description that isn't standing in for a claim the writer could have made outright.

### Narration Reveals Worldview — The Divorce Passage
> "We hadn't talked all summer, not since his mom died and my parents got divorced. And that's the thing about divorce is it's selfish because it hurts the people around you more than it hurts the people getting divorced."
> — Cole's own written example, composed specifically to demonstrate the Explanation-into-Narration transition, 00:19:57-00:20:12, transcript

Cole's own gloss: the first two clauses are backstory/context (Explanation); "that's the thing about divorce is it's selfish" is where "all of a sudden it's voicing an opinion or a belief" (Narration) — a sentence "not necessarily... dialogue... not an action... not explanation or backstory... not description. That is narration."

### Practice As Isolation
Instead of "write a chapter," Cole describes drilling one block "for like weeks in a row," then pairing it with a second block, then a third, until reaching "explanation and description and action" woven together (00:29:54-00:30:41). His stated long game: "thousands of different individual combinations memorized" over "the next five or ten years" (00:32:33-00:32:41).

## Signature Moves

- Color-code or tag each sentence with E, D, A, or N.
- Count the block mix before judging the prose.
- Ask what each block is trying to make the reader know, infer, feel, see, or anticipate.
- Replace low-value explanation with meaning-bearing description.
- Add explanation only when an action or image needs context.
- Cut actions that do not change stakes, reveal character, or move the argument/story.
- Protect narration by making it specific, opinionated, and scarce.
- Build block sequences for a target effect before drafting.
- Study model passages by classifying the block order.
- Create deliberate practice reps for one block or combination at a time.

## Quality Gate

A Cole-grade EDAN pass satisfies these checks:

1. Every sentence or paragraph has a clear EDAN classification.
2. Explanation is necessary, timed, and paid off.
3. Description implies meaning instead of adding decorative clutter.
4. Action creates consequence, reveal, or momentum.
5. Narration reveals a distinct point of view, not generic commentary.
6. The block balance matches the format and reader experience.
7. The revision plan names which block to add, cut, replace, or move.
8. The target reader effect is explicit.
9. At least one reusable block sequence is captured for future use.
10. Sentence polish happens after block function and sequence are correct.
