# Fraser Cottrell — Genius Context

Co-founder of Fraggle, performance ad creative studio: 8,000+ creatives/month, ~$300-450M in tied Meta spend, 15 years in performance marketing, 10,000+ ads produced, 500+ static ads tested for 7-9 figure DTC brands. Fraggle makes the creative; clients run the spend — but Fraggle takes "massive interest" in the data because it drives every next decision.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Creative Strategy Is the Last Lever
Media buying is completely automated. Optimization is completely automated. AI can generate an entire ad in minutes, so raw production is no longer an edge either. The only thing separating you from everyone else is the thought that goes into the ad — creative strategy is "possibly the only lever left to pull."
**Execute**: Before making anything, ask what the strategic thought is that a competitor's AI pipeline can't replicate: a research insight, a story, an angle. If the answer is "faster production" or "more volume," you have no edge yet — go back to research.
**Success Metric**: Every ad in the batch can name the insight it was built on; the account stops depending on media-buying tweaks for performance changes.

### Pattern: Research Heavier Than Writing
Fraser spends more time researching than designing and writing — deliberately putting himself in the customer's shoes, especially in niches he doesn't know. A strategist at Fraggle spent days researching golf, interviewing the founders about their own play, before writing a word. And never blindly trust the brand's own audience read: brands are sometimes wrong about who buys. Research on a bra brand surfaced a disability audience the product team never designed for — which became the "put it on with one hand" ad.
**Execute**: Budget research time to exceed writing time. Mine reviews, Reddit, comments, competitor data (tools like Particle for competitor SKU/revenue truth); interview anyone with lived experience in the niche. Ask specifically: who is buying this that the brand doesn't know about?
**Success Metric**: At least one audience segment or use case in the brief that the brand did not hand you; ads built on it outperform the brand's assumed-audience ads.

### Pattern: Strategist as Director, Not Executor
The number one strategist mistake is stopping at ideation. A creative strategist should understand editing, design, production, camera movement, and lighting — how a lens choice shifts focus, how lighting changes mood, how static design changes thumb-stop — so they know what's possible and can review drafts against the vision. But understanding is not doing: brands that use their strategist as an editor/UGC creator are wasting the role.
**Execute**: Own the idea end-to-end — write the script, specify casting and location, hand to pre-production/production, then review every draft against the vision in your head. Learn just enough craft in each production discipline to direct it, never to replace it.
**Success Metric**: Final ads match the strategist's original vision; zero ideas die in production because they were technically impossible from the start.

### Pattern: Villain-vs-Hero Script Architecture
Fraser's most consistent winning video format, 5 parts: (1) Hook — grab attention; (2) Villain — an industry, competitor product, or habit, built up as the enemy; (3) Hero — your product swoops in to save the day; (4) Demo/proof — back every claim with a visual test, study, or social proof; (5) CTA — where to buy, how much, the offer. It works because it tells a story instead of selling a product — and people don't see stories in feeds full of pitch.
**Execute**: Name the villain before writing (clogged cartridge razors; plastic-compound chewing gum; the nail-biting urge). "Twist the knife": research the villain's worst side effects and make people genuinely hate it — Marvel-movie logic: the more they hate the villain, the more they love the hero. Then demo the hero's superiority on camera (clog test, rinse test).
**Success Metric**: Script contains all 5 beats; the villain section produces visible emotional stakes (comments arguing about the villain); format repeats as a consistent winner across niches.

### Pattern: Curiosity Hook, Relevance-Checked
A hook operates in two departments — the visual hook (what's happening on screen) and the verbal/copy hook (voiceover + on-screen text). Curiosity is the strongest psychological principle: "How the leaf thorn is saving people" works because you don't know what a leaf thorn is but somehow it's saving people. But the hook must be relevant to the customer: an incredible hook rate with a terrible hold rate means you baited the wrong people.
**Execute**: Engineer visual and verbal hooks separately, then pair them. Open a curiosity gap the product will close. After launch, read hook rate AND hold rate together — never celebrate a hook rate in isolation.
**Success Metric**: Hook rate strong AND hold rate holds — proof the curiosity was relevant, not clickbait.

### Pattern: Claim-Then-Prove
Whenever you make a claim, back it up immediately — a medical study, or better, a physical visual test inside the ad (the razor clog test, the rinse test). Saying it creates skepticism; showing it creates purchase confidence.
**Execute**: Audit every claim line in the script and attach a proof asset: on-camera demo, study citation, or social proof. If no proof exists, cut the claim or build the demo.
**Success Metric**: Zero unproven claims survive to final cut; proof moments correlate with hold-rate retention spikes.

### Pattern: Comment-Mining Objection Injection
The comment section is a free objection-research lab. Leaf Shave kept getting "it's too expensive" comments — so Fraggle built a section directly into the ad: "I know it's more expensive, but this pays for itself a dozen times over in refill savings — blades for cents vs $5 a cartridge."
**Execute**: Before scripting v2 of any ad, harvest the top 3 objections from comments (and reviews). Write a dedicated benefit block answering the #1 objection into the body of the script, right before the CTA.
**Success Metric**: The dominant objection visibly declines in comments on the new ad; conversion improves on the same audience.

### Pattern: One-Variable Testing Science
Testing is a science experiment: change one variable at a time so you know WHY something worked. Same podcast ad content shot two ways — clean podcast frame vs "phone filming a laptop screen" — isolated the vehicle: 47.5% vs 34% hook rate. If high-production ads flop, rerun the same messaging as UGC: if UGC wins, production style was the problem, not the message.
**Execute**: Structure every test round so exactly one element differs — message, format, production vehicle, hook, or CTA. Log the losing variant's lesson; it's a decision-saver for every future ad.
**Success Metric**: Every test result yields an attributable cause; no "it just didn't work" postmortems.

### Pattern: Metric-to-Fix Mapping
Each metric names its own fix. Poor hook rate + great hold rate → replace the hook, keep the body. Terrible click-through → add/strengthen the CTA. Great hook + poor hold → hook is irrelevant to the customer. These small iterations are fast enough that the brand can often run them without the agency.
**Execute**: Read hook rate, hold rate, CTR as a diagnostic chain, not a scoreboard. Prescribe the smallest surgical edit the weakest metric implies; re-test that single change.
**Success Metric**: Iterations are single-element edits with predicted outcomes; iteration cycles measured in days, not re-shoots.

### Pattern: The 6-Format Static Library
Six static formats that actually held five-figure spend: (1) **Color-block SKU callout** — recognizable best-selling SKUs on scroll-stopping block color; copy secondary; translate un-sellable senses into known references ("smells like cedarwood + menthol"). (2) **AI headline-image / mascot** — ridiculous AI-generated mascot (Nano Banana cartoon pizza) stops the scroll; headline qualifies ("Blood sugar hack for people who hate dieting"); body promises; escapes sick-of-stock-photo fatigue. (3) **Social-proof comment frame** — a real website review styled as a social comment over a vibrant product shot; "if it worked for J.S., maybe it'll work for me"; 10 minutes in Canva, top of the ad account. (4) **Headliner** — massive headline + one custom image that supports it ("Life's changing. So did our gummies."). Mid/bottom-funnel reminder ads. (5) **Collage/grid** — 4-square of product, headline, benefit callouts, CTA; stops scroll by showing multiple things at once; color-themed to cohere. (6) **Instagram organic disguise** — Stories-native font + a target-customer photo holding the product; fades into the feed so it's consumed before it's recognized as an ad; whitelisting makes it more organic still.
**Execute**: Pick formats by funnel stage and asset availability; produce several per day (statics are 10-minute-to-hours builds) and test by evening. Plan performance-first: Foreplay for references, competitor teardown, Pinterest for design — never "just open Canva."
**Success Metric**: Static batch ships same-day; at least one format holds spend at scale; winners are recognizable as one of the six formats plus a research insight.

### Pattern: Demographic Mirror Casting
The person (or even just the hand) in the creative must look like the target customer's own. A woman seeing a hand like hers holding the product thinks "this is for me"; Fraser's "hairy, nail-bitten fingers" would get swiped away. Same logic scaled the bra ad: cast a UGC creator with a visible disability to prove "easiest bra to put on" as product proof.
**Execute**: Cast creators and stage product shots to mirror the exact demographic — age, gender, body, context. When a use-case segment exists (accessibility, life stage), cast FROM it and let the casting itself be the proof.
**Success Metric**: Target-demo CTR beats generic-model versions of the same ad; comments say "this is me."

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: Storytelling Reversed Polarity Because of AI Slop
**Insight**: Fraser publicly reversed himself — years ago he said storytelling in ads didn't matter; now it matters more than ever, precisely because AI-generated ads flooded the feed and customers are fed up. The founder story is now the one asset a competitor cannot copy.
**Deploy**: When a brand has a genuine founder story, make it the campaign spine, not a footnote. Treat "everyone else is using the same AI pipeline" as the reason to go more human, not faster.

### Insight: Reps Are the Moat, AI Is the Amplifier
**Insight**: Fraser advises learning to write scripts BEFORE leaning on AI — "the better you understand what goes into a great script, the better you can guide an AI into writing one." His own AI prompting is thin by design: he enters knowing the villain and the hero, and the tool just drafts. He still reviews every concept out of his agency and writes ads himself, 15 years in — judgment comes only from being in the weeds.
**Deploy**: Use AI for research synthesis and drafting, never for the strategic call (villain choice, angle, insight). Feed AI the villain/hero frame explicitly; keep human judgment as the accept/reject gate.

### Insight: Sensory Products Sell Through Borrowed References
**Insight**: Scent, taste, and feel can't travel through a feed. The fix is borrowing references the customer already owns — name the tones (cedarwood, menthol, raw Irish honey) so they can simulate the smell; perfume-dupe brands win because "smells like [perfume you know]" makes the sale nearly free.
**Deploy**: For any experiential product sold online, write copy that maps the experience onto sensations the customer has already had, and let color/design carry the "tasty/luxurious" feeling the screen can't transmit.

### Insight: The Brand Can Run Its Own Micro-Iterations
**Insight**: Hook swaps and CTA additions are fast, cheap edits the brand can execute faster than any agency turnaround — Fraser explicitly hands these back. The agency's value concentrates in research, strategy, and net-new concepts, not in the tenth hook variant.
**Deploy**: Split the iteration backlog: single-variable micro-edits go to whoever ships fastest (often in-house); reserve strategist time for new villains, new formats, new segments.
