# The In-Love Test — Are You Burning to Tell This? (Susan Orlean)

Orlean once sat in a room and pitched twelve book ideas. Eleven and a half were fine — competent, plausible, the kind that get "incredibly enthusiastic rejections." Then she blurted the one she was actually burning to write, and the answer came back instantly: *great, done.* The triathlon book she half-wanted got polite passes; the Saturday-night-in-America idea she couldn't stop thinking about got a contract on the spot. The difference was never quality. It was *love.* "There's a stench of inauthenticity that is very easy to smell on people" — and editors, readers, and audiences smell the gap between a competent idea and a beloved one before they can name it. This workflow is the gate that stands *upstream of everything* — before the telling-subject hunt, before the prism, before research, before a single index card. It refuses to let the machinery run on a subject the writer doesn't love, because no lead, no structure, no read-aloud edit can install a fire that was never there. The deliverable is a blunt verdict: **GO** (you're burning — proceed), **NO-GO / RE-CHOOSE** (you're executing — the stench will be smelled, change the subject), and the reason, so the call is honest and the writer can't argue with it later.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this workflow fails the instant it's run as a permission-granting rubber stamp instead of an honest refusal gate):

- **Pattern 1 — The Telling Subject** — the love test is meaningless on a non-subject; confirm there's a real overlooked specific carrying a real theme to *be* in love with. The subject is the door; the theme is the house.
- **Pattern 2 — The Prism Test** — dimensionality over width: a subject you're "in love with" that turns only one direction is infatuation, not a marriage; the love has to have *somewhere to go*. "How many layers down does this go?"
- **Hidden Knowledge** — especially **The Inauthenticity Stench** (the 12-ideas meeting; the triathlon book's enthusiastic rejections; the instant "great, done" of the burned-for idea; "are you in love with the subject? deeply, intensely, genuinely curious? nothing replaces that"; "literally burning with the desire to do the book").
- **Decision Framework** — the gate below, **Q1** above all else (am I in love with this — or just executing?).
- **Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-6 (Forcing a Subject You Don't Love)** — the direct named violation this workflow exists to catch.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before producing a single line — and this workflow is itself a deeper interrogation of **Q1**. Specifically confirm you are running this as a *real gate that can say NO*, not a formality that always says yes. The honest reading of "am I deeply, genuinely, *burning* curious about this — or am I executing a marketable / 'it's about time' idea?" must be allowed to fail. **The honesty spine here is brutal:** the verdict has to reflect the writer's *actual felt state*, not the verdict they want. If you (or the client) are steering this toward GO because the deck is already built, the deadline is set, or the topic "should do well," you have already failed the test — that steering *is* the stench. If Q1's honest answer is no, the only correct output is NO-GO / RE-CHOOSE; no craft downstream survives it (AN-6). Joanie Evans walked away from a finished, sellable project because "this is not where my heart was." That is the standard.

## Input Required

- **The candidate subject** — the thing you're tempted to write about, as concretely as you can state it. (If you can only state it as a category — "something about AI," "a leadership book" — note that; vagueness is often the first symptom of executing rather than burning.)
- **The trigger** — *why* is this subject on the table right now? Honest answer. ("I can't stop thinking about it" vs. "it's about time I wrote a book" vs. "this would sell" vs. "a client asked for it" vs. "it's trending.") The trigger is the single most diagnostic input — it usually reveals the verdict before the analysis does.
- **Whose love is being tested** — you, or a client/founder whose voice the piece carries. (For ghostwriting, the test transfers to the *named author* — borrowed founder enthusiasm is the smelliest of all, per the cross-domain table.)
- **The stakes / context** (optional) — is there money, a deadline, a contract, a content calendar slot already committed? Name it — not to override the verdict, but because committed stakes are exactly what *pressures* a writer to fake a GO. Surfacing the pressure is how you keep it from corrupting the call.
- **The honest gut reading** (one sentence) — before any analysis: when you imagine spending the next six months / six years inside this subject, does something in you lean *toward* it or brace *against* it? Write it down first, so the analysis can't talk you out of your own gut.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Read the trigger; it usually names the verdict before you analyze (Hidden Knowledge: The Inauthenticity Stench)

Before assessing the subject, assess *why it's on the table.* Orlean's twelve-ideas meeting is the whole lesson: the same writer, in the same room, on the same day, pitched eleven-and-a-half competent ideas and one beloved one — and only the beloved one landed. The variable was never the subject's quality. It was the *source of the impulse.* So the first move is to name, honestly, what put this subject in front of you — because the trigger is diagnostic. Some triggers are the sound of love; most are the sound of obligation, ambition, or trend-chasing wearing love's clothes.

| The trigger (why it's on the table) | What it usually is | Leans toward |
|---|---|---|
| "I can't stop thinking about it / I'd research it for free / I keep telling people about it" | live, involuntary curiosity | **GO** |
| "It's about time I wrote a book / did a big piece on this" | obligation to a self-image, not the subject (the triathlon book) | **NO-GO** |
| "This would do well / it's marketable / it's what's selling" | market logic, not love — "enthusiastic rejections" territory | **NO-GO** |
| "It's trending / everyone's covering it" | trend-chasing; also fails Pattern 1 (spotlight subject) | **NO-GO** |
| "A client / boss / calendar asked for it" | assigned; love must be *found* downstream or honestly outsourced to structure | **CONDITIONAL** — go find the love or declare it craft-work |
| "I was skeptical / uninterested, then I got pulled in and now I can't let go" | *earned* fascination — Orlean's own pattern | **GO** (the strongest GO) |

> **The "great, done" test:** imagine blurting this subject to a sharp editor who knows you. Does your own voice carry the involuntary heat of the Saturday-night-in-America pitch — the one that got an instant *great, done* — or the careful, justifying tone of a pitch you're *talking yourself into*? You can hear the difference in your own mouth. If you're building a case for why you *should* write it, that case *is* the stench. Love doesn't argue for itself; it just won't shut up about the subject.

Write down the trigger and which way it leans. If it's the "I can't stop thinking about it" / "I got pulled in" trigger, you're likely heading to GO. Any other trigger, proceed to Step 2 with suspicion — the rest of the test exists to catch a NO-GO trying to pass as a GO.

### Step 2 — Run the burning-curiosity gate (Hidden Knowledge: "nothing replaces that"; AN-6)

This is the heart of the test, and it is not about marketability, importance, or even quality. Orlean's words: *"Are you in love with the subject? Are you deeply, intensely, genuinely curious? Nothing replaces that."* And: it's "pretty essential that they are literally burning with the desire to do the book." Put the candidate through the honest gate — and the gate must be allowed to fail.

| Question | PASS (burning) | FAIL (executing — stop and re-choose) |
|---|---|---|
| Am I *deeply, genuinely, intensely* curious about this? | I want to go find out; the unanswered questions itch | I already know roughly what I'd say; I'm filling it in |
| Did I *choose* this, or am I *drawn* to it? | drawn — I'd do the research for free | chose — I assigned it to myself out of logic or obligation |
| Is the enthusiasm real, or am I performing it? | real — it survives me being tired, bored, or unwatched | performed — it's the voice I'd use to *sell* it, not to *live* it |
| Could I stand to spend years inside it? | yes — the prospect feels like a gift, not a sentence | no — I'm already negotiating how fast I can get out |
| When I imagine the *research* (not the finished book), do I light up or sag? | light up — the hunt is the reward | sag — I want the output, not the immersion |

> **The crucial nuance — love is not the same as fandom (Pattern 3 interplay):** in-love does *not* mean you started as a fan. Orlean "doesn't even like Florida" and was never an orchid collector. She *became* fascinated — started exactly where the skeptical reader is and got pulled in, which is precisely what later lets her be the reader's proxy ("I didn't think I needed to know this either, and then —"). So the test is **not** "did I always love this?" It is **"is there live, earned, burning curiosity *now* — am I dying to go find out?"** Borrowed enthusiasm is the failure. *Earned* fascination — the skeptic who got pulled in — is the strongest possible pass. If you started cold and stayed cold, that's a fail. If you started cold and can't stop now, that's the gold standard.

> **The Joanie Evans standard (the courage to walk):** Orlean's mother, Joanie Evans, sensed of a project "this is not where my heart was" — and the lesson is that the test only means something if you're willing to *act on a fail.* A test that always returns GO because stopping is inconvenient is not a test; it's a permission slip. If the honest answers above are mostly FAIL, the correct, courageous output is NO-GO — even if the deck is built, the deadline is set, the contract is signed. Better to lose the sunk cost now than to ship something that reeks of a heart that wasn't in it.

### Step 3 — Confirm the love has somewhere to go: the prism/dimensionality check (Pattern 2)

A writer can be genuinely, burningly in love with something that is nonetheless *too thin to sustain the form* — infatuation with a single clever angle. Love is necessary but not sufficient; it has to have somewhere to *go.* This is where the in-love test borrows the prism test: turn the subject 4-6 directions and confirm there's depth for the love to travel through. Orlean: "ideally you can take it and turn it in many different directions... and yet they're all part of the same story." "Width is one thing, but really it's the depth. How many layers down does this go?"

This step is *not* the full prism workout (that's `orlean-telling-subject`) — it's a fast dimensionality reading, just enough to confirm the love isn't trapped in a single facet.

| Reading | What you find | Verdict signal |
|---|---|---|
| Turn the subject 3-4 ways quickly — do new faces appear, all of the *same* story? | yes — each turn opens a new layer, still one subject | love has room: **GO** holds |
| Can I see only *one* angle, however much I love it? | one rich angle, no depth behind it | infatuation, not a marriage — **resize the format** (it's a piece, not a book) or **dig for the deeper house** |
| Do the "facets" turn out to be different subjects sharing a keyword? | grab-bag, not a prism | the love is real but unfocused — narrow to the one true door before GO |

> **Love with nowhere to go = a magazine piece, not a book.** If you're burning but the subject turns only one direction, the love is real and the *form* is wrong — that's not a NO-GO, it's a *resize.* Declare it the feature it is rather than padding it into a book the subject can't carry (the magazine-vs-book call, Pattern 2). A genuine GO with a thin prism becomes: "GO, but as a 6,000-word piece, not a 90,000-word book." Honesty about scale is part of honesty about love.

### Step 4 — Run the inauthenticity-stench scan (Hidden Knowledge: "the stench is very easy to smell"; AN-6)

Now turn the test outward: even if the writer *believes* they're in love, will an audience *smell* otherwise? The stench is detectable from the outside before it's admissible on the inside — Orlean got "incredibly enthusiastic rejections" precisely because editors smelled the gap she hadn't yet admitted to herself. So this step pressure-tests the GO against the symptoms of borrowed enthusiasm, which leak into the work whether or not the writer cops to them.

| Stench symptom (flag if present) | What it reveals | Why audiences smell it |
|---|---|---|
| The pitch *argues for* its own importance ("this matters because…") | the writer is convincing themselves, not just the reader | real love states; manufactured love justifies |
| The subject was reverse-engineered from "what will perform / sell" | market logic dressed as curiosity | the seams between desire and strategy show in the prose |
| Enthusiasm is loud at the pitch and absent at the *research* prospect | excitement about *having written it*, not *writing it* | the iceberg under the tip is missing; the prose floats wrong |
| The "love" needs profound-sounding theme stapled on to feel worthy | a stapled house, not a carried one (AN-1) | over-reaching to impress reads as insecurity, not passion |
| The writer keeps citing *who else* wants it (the editor, the client, the algorithm) | the impulse is external, not internal | borrowed enthusiasm — "the smelliest of all" in ghostwriting |

> **The stench is a leading indicator, not a lagging one.** The whole point of catching it *here*, at the gate, is that you cannot deodorize it later. No lead, no read-aloud edit, no structure removes the smell of a subject the writer didn't love — it's in the choice itself, and it diffuses through every downstream sentence. If two or more symptoms flag, treat the prior GO as suspect and re-interrogate Step 2 honestly. A clean Step 2 with a clean stench scan is a *confident* GO. A passing Step 2 that nonetheless trips the stench scan means the writer is fooling themselves — believe the smell over the self-report.

### Step 5 — Render the verdict and the reason (the go/no-go output)

The test exists to produce one thing: a clear, honest call the writer can act on without re-litigating. Collapse the four prior steps into one of three verdicts, each with its reason stated plainly — so that if the writer overrides it, they do so knowing exactly what they're overriding.

| Verdict | When | What it means / next move |
|---|---|---|
| **GO** | Trigger is love or earned fascination · burning-curiosity gate passes · prism has depth · stench scan clean | Proceed. Run `orlean-telling-subject` to formalize door/house/prism, then the three-phase process. The love is real and has somewhere to go. |
| **GO (resize)** | Love is real (Step 2 passes) but the prism is thin (one rich angle) | Proceed *at the right scale* — declare it a feature/post/essay, not a book. Don't pad love into a form it can't fill. |
| **NO-GO / RE-CHOOSE** | Trigger is obligation/market/trend · burning-curiosity gate fails · or stench scan trips with no honest love beneath | Do **not** proceed. The stench will be smelled regardless of craft (AN-6). Either find a subject you're genuinely burning for, or — if it's assigned client work — route it honestly to structure-driven production and stop pretending it's passion. Joanie Evans walked; so can you. |

> **The verdict must name its reason in one sentence**, and the reason must be specific enough to act on. Not "it didn't feel right" but "NO-GO: the trigger was 'it's about time,' the research prospect made me sag, and the pitch kept arguing for its own importance — that's the triathlon book." A reasoned verdict is one the writer can't wriggle out of with motivated reasoning later.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | How the in-love test deploys |
|---|---|
| **Book / long feature** | The highest stakes for the test, because the immersion is *years* — nobody fakes burning curiosity across six-to-eight years (the iceberg of research demands it). Apply every step at full strength; a thin prism here is a hard resize-to-feature, not a maybe. The Joanie Evans courage-to-walk standard matters most here: the sunk cost of a started book is enormous, and the test is worthless if it can't override it. |
| **Marketing / brand content** | The test transfers to the *brand's* genuine obsession, not the marketer's task list. The fail mode is "we should post about X because it's our pillar / it's trending." The pass mode is the overlooked detail someone at the company is genuinely fascinated by. The stench scan is lethal here — audiences smell brand enthusiasm-by-committee instantly. If no one's actually in love, route to honest structure-driven content rather than performing passion. |
| **Copywriting** | Love attaches to the *offer's true mechanism / the real transformation*, not the campaign. The fail: copy reverse-engineered from "what converts" with no belief under it. The pass: a writer (or founder) genuinely burning about why this thing works. The stench scan maps directly onto the honesty spine — manufactured enthusiasm in copy reads as hype, and hype is the smell. A clean GO here means the conviction is real; tune the prose elsewhere. |
| **Ghostwriting** | The test transfers entirely to the *named author* — "borrowed founder enthusiasm is the smelliest of all." The fail mode: a client who wants the *credibility* of having written it but isn't burning to discuss the subject. The pass: a client who won't stop talking about it. Run Step 2 *on the client's behalf,* in their actual voice — if their gut sags, no ghostwriting craft installs a love they don't have. A NO-GO here is a service to the client: find the subject they're actually burning for. |
| **Content series / pillar** | The test gates whether the *love can recur.* A subject you're burning for once may not sustain a recurring slot — so Step 3's prism check doubles as a longevity check (do the facets give you *future editions* you'd be excited to write?). The fail: a pillar chosen for SEO/strategy that the creator dreads returning to. The pass: a subject the creator keeps voluntarily circling back to. Performed enthusiasm reads fastest at series cadence — the stench compounds over weeks. |

### Worked example A — A book a writer is tempted to write because "it's about time" (NO-GO)

**Candidate subject:** a sweeping book on the history of American optimism. · **Whose love:** the writer. · **Stakes:** a publisher has gently asked "what's next?"

> **Step 1 · Trigger.** Honest answer: *"It's a big, important subject and it's about time I wrote something with that kind of weight."* Trigger reading: **obligation to a self-image** (the writer wants to be the kind of writer who writes Big Important Books). "Great, done" test: the writer's own voice is *justifying* — "this matters because optimism is the American character…" — building a case, not bursting with it. That argument is the stench. Leans: **NO-GO.**

> **Step 2 · Burning-curiosity gate.** Genuinely curious? *Sort of — I know roughly the arc already* (FAIL). Drawn or chose? *Chose it, deliberately, for its heft* (FAIL). Real or performed? *It's the voice I'd use to pitch it* (FAIL). Years inside it? *Honestly, the prospect feels like a long obligation* (FAIL). Light up at the research? *I sag — I want to have written it, not to spend three years in archives* (FAIL). Earned-fascination note: never got pulled in; started cold, stayed cold. **Gate verdict: EXECUTING.** This is the triathlon book — competent, will get "enthusiastic rejections."

> **Step 3 · Prism.** Moot once Step 2 fails, but for the record: the subject is so wide it's *all* width and no door — "American optimism" is the house with no specific to enter it through. Even the love-with-somewhere-to-go question can't be asked, because there's no concrete subject to turn.

> **Step 4 · Stench scan.** Symptoms flagged: *argues-for-its-own-importance*, *loud-at-pitch-absent-at-research*. **TRIPPED.** Two symptoms; believe the smell.

> **Step 5 · Verdict. NO-GO — RE-CHOOSE.** Reason: *"The trigger was 'it's about time,' every gate question failed, the research prospect made me sag, and the pitch argues for its own importance — this is the triathlon book; the stench will be smelled."* Next move: stop. Don't pitch it. Go find the small, specific, dismissible thing inside American optimism that you can't stop thinking about — one motel, one failed utopia, one self-help pamphlet — and run the test again on *that.*

### Worked example B — A founder's origin story for a ghostwritten brand piece (GO, with a resize)

**Candidate subject:** the chipped tip on the founder's grandmother's knife and the afternoon he understood why she'd never replace it. · **Whose love:** the founder (named author; this is ghostwriting). · **Stakes:** a content calendar slot is open this month.

> **Step 1 · Trigger.** Honest answer (the founder's, in his voice): *"I bring up that knife unprompted. I've told the grandmother story to customers, at dinners, on a podcast — I can't not tell it."* Trigger reading: **earned fascination → live love** (he started just running a knife company and got pulled into what the chip *meant*). "Great, done" test: his voice carries involuntary heat — he's not selling the knife, he's *grieving and celebrating his grandmother.* Leans: **GO.**

> **Step 2 · Burning-curiosity gate** (run on the founder's behalf, in his voice). Genuinely curious? *Yes — I still wonder why exactly she wouldn't replace it* (PASS). Drawn or chose? *Drawn — I'd talk about this for free, I already do* (PASS). Real or performed? *Real — it survives me being tired; it's not a sales voice* (PASS). Years inside it? *I've been inside it for years already* (PASS). Light up at the research? *Yes — I want to go find out about her, the maker, the metallurgy-as-character* (PASS). Earned-fascination note: textbook — skeptic-of-his-own-sentimentality who got pulled in. **Gate verdict: BURNING.** Borrowed-founder-enthusiasm risk: *zero* — this is his, not the brand deck's.

> **Step 3 · Prism.** Turns: the *object* (why a knife earns a chip and keeps working) → the *people* (grandmother, founder, the customer who'll hand theirs down) → the *worldview* (a quiet case against disposability). Three real turns, same chipped knife. Depth reading: **deep enough for a rich piece and a recurring pillar** — but not 90,000 book-length layers. Form call: **GO (resize)** — a long brand essay + a content pillar of future editions, *not* a book.

> **Step 4 · Stench scan.** Symptoms: none. He's not arguing for importance (he's just telling it), it wasn't reverse-engineered from "what converts" (the calendar slot is incidental, not the source), the love survives the research prospect, no stapled theme, and the impulse is fully internal. **CLEAN.**

> **Step 5 · Verdict. GO (resize to a brand essay + content pillar).** Reason: *"Live, earned fascination on every gate question, the love is the founder's own and survives the research prospect, the prism turns three real ways — but it's pillar-deep, not book-deep, so write it at that scale."* Next move: run `orlean-telling-subject` to formalize the door (the chip) / house (loving things enough to keep them) / prism, then ghostwrite in his voice, letting the heirloom theme *vibrate* and never stating the slogan.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` — above all **AN-6 (Forcing a Subject You Don't Love)** — and § Decision Framework Q1. Flag and fix before delivering.

- **The gate could actually say NO (the core integrity check):** if this run was always going to return GO regardless of the honest answers — because the deck is built, the deadline is set, or stopping is inconvenient — the test was a rubber stamp and the result is void. Re-run it as a gate that can fail. A test that can't say NO isn't a test.
- **The trigger was read honestly (Step 1, Hidden Knowledge):** the *why-it's-on-the-table* answer is the real one ("it's about time" / "it'll sell"), not the flattering one. Obligation, market logic, and trend-chasing were named as what they are, not relabeled as curiosity.
- **The burning-curiosity gate was answered on felt state, not desired verdict (Step 2, AN-6):** the PASS/FAIL on each question reflects what the writer (or named author) actually feels — including whether the *research* prospect lights them up or makes them sag — not what would justify proceeding. "Nothing replaces" genuine curiosity, and nothing fakes it.
- **Fandom was not confused with love (Step 2, Pattern 3 interplay):** the test correctly distinguished "always was a fan" from "got pulled in" — earned fascination from a cold start is the strongest pass; performed enthusiasm is the fail. Not loving it *at first* was never held against a subject the writer can't stop thinking about *now.*
- **The love was confirmed to have somewhere to go (Step 3, Pattern 2):** real love on a thin, single-angle subject was called a *resize* (feature, not book), not padded into a form the subject can't carry — and a grab-bag was narrowed to one true door before any GO.
- **The stench scan was believed over the self-report (Step 4, Hidden Knowledge):** where Step 2 passed but the inauthenticity symptoms tripped, the verdict treated the smell as the leading indicator and re-interrogated the love — because audiences detect the stench before the writer admits it.
- **The verdict is clear, reasoned, and un-wrigglable (Step 5):** one of GO / GO-resize / NO-GO, with a specific one-sentence reason the writer can't motivated-reason their way out of later. "It didn't feel right" is not a verdict; "NO-GO: the trigger was obligation and the research made me sag — the triathlon book" is.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Running the test as a permission slip instead of a gate.** Going through the motions with the verdict pre-decided (GO, obviously — the contract's signed), so every honest FAIL gets quietly upgraded to a PASS. This is the single most common failure, and it defeats the entire purpose: a gate that always opens is a wall with a door painted on it. **Recovery:** before answering anything, explicitly grant the test the right to return NO-GO and name what you'd lose by stopping (the sunk cost, the slot, the deadline). Surfacing the pressure is how you keep it from corrupting the call — and remember Joanie Evans walked away from a finished, sellable project because her heart wasn't in it. If she can, you can.

- **Confusing "this is important / will sell" with "I'm in love."** Mistaking the subject's *worthiness* or *marketability* for the writer's *love* — choosing the big, dignified, high-demand topic and reading the resulting sense of obligation as passion. This is exactly the triathlon-book failure: a competent idea that earns "enthusiastic rejections" because the spark is missing. **Recovery:** separate the questions hard. "Should this exist / would it sell?" is a different question from "am I burning to spend years finding it out?" Only the second one passes the gate. Importance is the *house's* job (Pattern 1); it can't substitute for love, and audiences smell the substitution.

- **Treating not-loving-it-at-first as a disqualification.** Failing a genuinely promising subject because the writer wasn't a fan going in — when earned fascination from a cold start is Orlean's *own* pattern and the strongest possible GO ("I don't even like Florida"). **Recovery:** re-ask Step 2 in the present tense: not "did I always love this?" but "is there live, burning curiosity *now* — am I dying to go find out?" A skeptic who got pulled in is the ideal writer (and the ideal reader's-proxy). Cold-then-hot passes; cold-then-still-cold fails. Don't reject the former by holding the cold start against it.

- **Passing the self-report but ignoring the stench.** A writer talks themselves into a clean Step 2 ("yes, I'm curious, yes, I'd do it for free") while the stench scan in Step 4 lights up — arguing for the subject's importance, citing who else wants it, loud at the pitch and absent at the research. **Recovery:** believe the smell.
