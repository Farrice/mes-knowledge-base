# The Three Separated Phases — Research, Then Thinking, Then Writing (Susan Orlean)

Orlean does not start writing to figure out what she thinks. By the time she drafts, she is *delivering* a story she already understands, not *discovering* an unknown one — "I can't write yet. I don't know what I'm writing about." That sentence is the whole operating system. Three phases, three hard walls. Phase 1 is research with not one word of the book written — years sometimes, eight weeks to learn how arson is investigated for what reads as a single paragraph. Phase 2 is digestion: typing up notes, breaking everything onto cards, moving the cards until themes cluster — a phase that "doesn't look productive" because there is no prose to show for it, and which "we underestimate the amount of time we need" for. Phase 3 is writing, mechanically, to a quota: a thousand words a day, five days a week (six if behind), run like marathon pacing — don't look at the finish line, run the mile in front of you. This workflow builds that plan and holds the walls. The discipline is the liberation: a New Yorker editor's quota system "took all the mystery out of it," and taking the mystery out is exactly what frees you to relax into the sentence.

## Input Required

- **The project** — what you intend to write (book, long feature, multi-part series, deep brand narrative). One sentence is enough; the telling subject and theme can be sharpened separately via `orlean-telling-subject`.
- **The state** — *standing up new* (no words yet) or *rescuing stalled* (you've been drafting and it isn't working). The rescue path has an extra diagnostic step.
- **The scale** — target length and hard deadline if any (drives the iceberg targets in Phase 1 and the quota math in Phase 3).
- **The "why this matters" undertone** (optional) — the quiet universal the subject vibrates toward (memory/permanence for the library fire). Lets Phase 2 cluster toward theme, not just topic.
- **Resources** — how much reporting access, time, and runway you actually have (sets honest Phase 1 boundaries; the iceberg ratio is non-negotiable in *kind*, scalable in *degree*).

## Workflow

### Step 0 (RESCUE PATH ONLY) — Diagnose the wall collapse before building anything

If the project is *stalled because you're drafting to figure out what you think*, you have collapsed the walls between phases — the single most common nonfiction failure (AN-4). Do not "push through." Diagnose which wall broke, then re-enter at the correct phase.

| Symptom you're feeling | Wall that collapsed | Re-enter at |
|---|---|---|
| "I keep rewriting the opening; I don't know where this is going." | You started Phase 3 before Phase 2. No structure exists; you're inventing it inside the prose. | **Phase 2** — stop drafting, atomize what you have onto cards, cluster into themes. |
| "Every paragraph I hit a fact I don't actually have." | Phase 1 wasn't finished. You're drafting on the tip with no iceberg underneath. | **Phase 1** — list the gaps, report them out before another sentence. |
| "I have tons of material but it's a pile, not a story." | Phase 1 done, Phase 2 skipped. The digestion phase that "doesn't look productive" got cut because it produced no prose. | **Phase 2** — the cards are the missing step. |
| "I know the story cold but I keep stalling at the desk." | Phases 1–2 done; Phase 3 has no quota. You're staring at the void. | **Phase 3** — install the daily word quota. |

> **The rescue rule**: drafting-to-discover *feels like* progress (words on the page) but it is the most expensive way to find structure — every restructure means re-drafting prose, not re-moving a card. Name the broken wall, drop back to the prior phase, and the stall usually dissolves. "We underestimate the amount of time we need to just digest" — the rescue is almost always *more Phase 2*.

### Step 1 — PHASE 1: Research / Reporting (zero words of the piece written)

The hard wall: **not one word of the actual piece gets written in Phase 1.** You are reporting, gathering, learning the iceberg beneath the tip — and the iceberg is mostly submerged on purpose. Orlean spent 6–8 weeks learning how arson is investigated for a passage where "you're not going to go, boy, I bet that was eight weeks of research." The confidence on the page comes from the over-research *off* the page.

Set the iceberg ratio honestly. The visible prose is the tip; the submerged reporting is what makes the tip float right.

| Project scale | Real-world Orlean anchor | Iceberg-ratio research target (submerged : visible) | What "done" looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Book / 80k+ words** | *The Library Book*, *The Orchid Thief* — 3–4 years of research | Roughly **20–40× the visible word count** in raw reported material; saturation, not coverage | You stop finding surprises; new interviews confirm rather than overturn. |
| **Long feature / 5–15k** | New Yorker piece | **8–15×**; you know far more than survives the cut | You could write a second piece from the leftovers and choose not to. |
| **Series / recurring** | Per-episode reporting on a shared spine | **5–10× per installment**, on a single deeply-reported well | The well is deep enough that episodes draw from it without re-reporting. |
| **Deep brand / founder narrative** | The founding moment as a telling subject | **6–12×**; interview around the moment until the prism appears | The small moment has opened onto product + people + the human need it serves. |

Phase 1 outputs (these are *not* prose — they are inputs to Phase 2):
- **A reporting log** — interviews done, documents pulled, scenes witnessed, the technical thing learned (how arson is investigated; how orchids are smuggled).
- **A "still submerged" gap list** — what you don't yet know. You leave Phase 1 only when this list holds nothing load-bearing.
- **The iceberg check**: estimate raw material gathered vs. target length. Under the ratio = keep reporting. "Every time I'm stuck, I just haven't reported enough."

> **The Phase 1 wall, restated**: the urge to "just start writing" *during* research is the enemy. Capture observations and quotes raw, in notes — but write zero sentences of the piece. The instant you draft to organize your thoughts, you've collapsed into Phase 2/3 and forfeited the structure-before-prose advantage. Note, don't draft.

### Step 2 — PHASE 2: Digestion / Thinking (cards and clusters, still no prose)

This is the phase "we underestimate the amount of time we need" for, and the one that "doesn't look productive" — because at the end of a Phase 2 day there is no prose to show, only cards moved on a table. That is correct. This is where the story is *decided*, not written.

The mechanism is physical (Pattern 6). Orlean used ~700 5×8 index cards for *The Library Book* and *Rin Tin Tin*, then literally picked them up and moved them around until structure emerged.

**2a — Atomize into chunks of thought.** Break all Phase 1 material onto cards. A card can be tiny (one anecdote, one quote) or a pointer ("card 35: the 1986 arson report → fish it from the file"). The point is granularity: one idea per card, so it can move independently.

**2b — Cluster into themes, not topics.** Lay the cards out in a big open space and move them until they cluster. Orlean's *Library Book* clusters: *the fire/arson investigation* · *the library's history* · *the story of Harry Peak (the accused)*. The themes, once clustered, "began having within them the story I was going to tell." Cluster toward the universal undertone (memory/permanence), not just the surface topic.

| Move | What you're doing | Failure if skipped |
|---|---|---|
| **Atomize** | One chunk of thought per card; reacquaint yourself with the information | A pile, not parts — nothing can recombine |
| **Cluster** | Move cards until themes (not topics) emerge | Topical sections that inform but don't tell |
| **Re-cluster** | Move them again; the spine reveals itself in rearrangement | You lock structure too early on the first arrangement |
| **Find the seams** | Mark where one theme must hand off to the next (Pattern 7 connective tissue) | Visible joints in Phase 3; reads as a binder, not a yarn |
| **Re-ground time** | For non-chronological structure, note the time frame on each cluster | The reader (and you) loses the thread; "I always had to reground myself" |

**2c — Lock the spine.** When the clusters read as *one* story turned in several directions (the prism test, Pattern 2) rather than a grab-bag, the structure is decided. This is the deliverable of Phase 2: a theme-clustered outline that exists *before* a single sentence of prose, where rearranging cost nothing but moving a card.

> **The Phase 2 wall, restated**: the temptation is to skip this because it produces no words and "feels unproductive." Resist. The cheapest place to restructure a story is on a table where you slide a card; the most expensive is in finished prose. Every hour here saves a week of re-drafting in Phase 3. This is the phase that takes the mystery out — and "it's very liberating" to have done so before you draft.

### Step 3 — PHASE 3: Writing to a Daily Quota (mechanical, finite, liberating)

Only now do words of the piece get written — and they get written *to a quota*, against the spine Phase 2 decided. The quota converts the overwhelming whole ("write a book") into a finite daily task ("tell you a thousand words of the story today"). Orlean finds this *liberating*, not restrictive: "Instead of looking at the void and feeling like this is overwhelming… it's just, oh, I've got a thousand words to write."

**Set the quota and the math.**

| Lever | Orlean's setting | How to set yours |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily words** | ~**1,000 words/day** | Default 1,000; lower (500–750) for denser/more-reported prose, but keep it *finite and hittable* |
| **Days/week** | **5 days, 6 if behind** | Protect the cadence; the sixth day is the catch-up valve, not the norm |
| **Pacing mindset** | Marathon — "don't look at the finish line" | Run the mile in front of you; never compute "X words ÷ 1,000 = N days of dread" |
| **Quality posture** | Quota "does not remove the poetry — it enhances my ability to feel relaxed and sit there and work on a sentence" | The quota is the budget that *frees* the artistry; you're not racing, you're spending a known allowance |

**The daily ritual (Pattern 11 dovetails here).** Each session, before writing the new thousand, reread *yesterday's* thousand and edit it hard while it's fresh-but-slept-on — read it aloud, cut the "would," the extra adjective, the boring stretch. Then write today's against the spine. Leave the desk having *done it* — a finite goal hit — never defeated by the size of the whole.

**Timeline math (be honest about it once, then never look at it again).**
- 80k book ÷ 1,000/day × 5 days/week ≈ **80 writing days ≈ 16 weeks** of drafting (Phase 3 alone) — and that's *after* the years of Phase 1–2.
- 10k feature ≈ **10 writing days ≈ 2 weeks**.
- Compute this once to set expectations, write it on the plan, then run marathon-style: tomorrow is one more mile, not "13 miles to go."

> **The Phase 3 wall, restated**: do not let Phase 3 leak backward into "wait, I need to research this" (that's a Phase-1 gap you should have caught) or "I'm not sure of the structure here" (that's a Phase-2 card you should have moved). When those urges hit mid-draft, note the gap and keep writing to quota; resolve genuine gaps in a short bounded return to the prior phase, not by abandoning the quota. The walls run both ways.

### Step 4 — Assemble the phased project plan

Combine Steps 1–3 into one plan with the walls explicit. The plan is the deliverable. It must make the three phases *physically separate* on the calendar — when Phase 1 ends and Phase 2 begins is a date, not a vibe.

## Content Type Adaptations

The three-phase discipline scales down; the *kind* of each phase is non-negotiable (you always research before you think, think before you write), but the *degree* compresses.

| Format | Phase 1 (research, no words) | Phase 2 (digest, no prose) | Phase 3 (write to quota) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Social media** (LinkedIn/X/Substack post) | Hours, not weeks — but real: gather the true specific, the one jarring detail, the actual numbers. Iceberg ratio ~3–5×. | A handful of cards (digital is fine): the telling specific, the universal it opens onto, the wait-what lead. Cluster = pick the *one* angle. | Quota is "one post today," drafted plain; one vivid image, not five (Pattern 10). |
| **Marketing campaign** | Report the overlooked product/operational detail until the prism appears (~6×). | Cards cluster product + people + human-need into one story, not a feature list. | Daily output of assets against the clustered spine; lift facts into yarn (Pattern 8), never bullet-points. |
| **Copywriting** (VSL / page / email) | Voice-of-customer + proof + the true intriguing detail. Research the *real* particular — honesty spine. | Cluster proof, objection, belief, offer into the conversion sequence before drafting a line. | Draft to a section-per-session quota; plain copy with reserved hits (Pattern 9). |
| **Ghostwriting** | Interview the client to saturation — past the comfortable anecdotes to the iceberg only they have. | Cards in *their* voice; cluster around what they're genuinely burning to say (audiences smell borrowed enthusiasm). | Quota per session in their cadence; read aloud to catch what's false (Pattern 11). |
| **Content series / book** | Native habitat: deep, saturated reporting on a single well (years for a book). | ~700-card spread; cluster into themes; engineer connective tissue so episodes read as one show. | 1,000 words/day, 5 days/week, marathon pacing across months. |

### Worked example A — *The Library Book* (book; how Orlean actually ran it)

```
PROJECT: A book about the 1986 Los Angeles Central Library fire (400,000 books burned).
STATE: new build   ·   SCALE: ~95,000 words, no hard deadline
TELLING SUBJECT (small thing): one building, one fire  →  THEME: memory made permanent against the certainty of personal death — "books are our system of making permanent memory, and memory by definition can't be permanent."
DECISION-FRAMEWORK GATE: Q1 ✓ (fascinated by libraries) · Q2 ✓ (a fire nobody remembers) · Q3 ✓ (fire + library history + Harry Peak + the human need to be remembered) · Q5 ✓

━━━ PHASE 1 · RESEARCH (zero words of the book) ━━━
Iceberg ratio target: ~25× submerged : visible   ·   Anchor: book (3–4 years)
Reporting plan:
  • Learn how arson is actually investigated (the 6–8-week dig that yields a single trustworthy paragraph)
  • Reconstruct the day of the fire; interview librarians, firefighters, the accused (Harry Peak) circle
  • The library's full institutional history; what was lost
"Still submerged" gap list:
  • The forensic reality of whether the fire was even arson (held open for years)
EXIT CRITERION: the arson question understood well enough to write it honestly; surprises stopped.   WALL: not one word of the book drafted across these years.
PHASE 1 ENDS: after saturation — not before.

━━━ PHASE 2 · DIGESTION (cards, no prose) ━━━
Atomize: ~700 5×8 index cards (one chunk of thought each; some are pointers to the file)
Theme clusters:
  1. The fire / the arson investigation
  2. The history of the library itself
  3. The story of Harry Peak (the man accused)
Connective-tissue seams: braid the three so a chapter on Peak hands off to the institution's history without a visible joint; reground the time frame each handoff (non-chronological).
Spine locked: the three clusters turn as ONE prism — a fire that opens onto memory and permanence.
EXIT CRITERION: theme-clustered structure decided ON THE TABLE, before a sentence of prose.

━━━ PHASE 3 · WRITING TO QUOTA ━━━
Daily quota: 1,000 words · 5 days/week (6 if behind)
Timeline (computed once): ~95 writing days ≈ ~19 weeks of drafting — AFTER the years of Phase 1–2.
Daily ritual: each morning reread yesterday's 1,000 aloud, cut the "would" and the extra adjective, then write today's against the card spine.
Pacing: marathon — "a thousand words today, and then I did it." Never stared at the 95,000.

LIBERATION CHECK: the 95,000-word void became a daily thousand-word task; the New Yorker quota "took all the mystery out of it," which is what freed the sentence-level play.
```

### Worked example B — Rescuing a stalled brand-founder narrative (rescue path)

```
PROJECT: A 4,000-word founder origin story for a kitchenware company.
STATE: RESCUE — writer has rewritten the opening eleven times over three weeks; it reads as a feature list with a sad anecdote bolted on.
TELLING SUBJECT: the founder's grandmother's single warped cast-iron pan  →  THEME: the things we keep because someone's hands wore them in.
DECISION-FRAMEWORK GATE: Q1 ✓ · Q2 ✓ · Q3 ✓ · Q5 ✗ ← the broken one.

BROKEN WALL DIAGNOSIS: "I have tons of material but it's a pile, not a story" + "I keep rewriting the opening." → Phase 1 done, Phase 2 SKIPPED; writer jumped to Phase 3 and is inventing structure inside the prose.  RE-ENTER AT: Phase 2.

━━━ PHASE 2 · DIGESTION (stop drafting; cards, no prose) ━━━
Atomize: ~40 cards from the existing interview material (one anecdote/quote/fact each)
Theme clusters:
  1. The pan itself (the warped, worn, kept object — the telling specific)
  2. The grandmother's kitchen (the people, the hands that wore it in)
  3. Why the company exists (objects made to be inherited, not replaced)
Connective-tissue seam: the pan hands off to the kitchen hands off to the company's reason-to-exist — one prism, three turns.
Spine locked: the warped pan opens onto the universal — what we keep, and why.
EXIT CRITERION: outline on the table; the "feature list" disappears because features now hang off theme, not the reverse.

━━━ PHASE 3 · WRITING TO QUOTA (resume only now) ━━━
Daily quota: 750 words · 5 days/week  →  ~6 writing days ≈ ~1.5 weeks
Daily ritual: reread aloud, cut the "would," one vivid image of the pan — not five.
Pacing: one section a session against the new spine.

LIBERATION CHECK: the eleven-rewrites stall dissolved the moment structure moved to the table; the opening wrote itself once the pan was the door and the theme was the house.
```

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Susan Orlean Would Never… (Anti-Patterns)` (especially **AN-4 Drafting Before You Know What It's About**) and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Structure-Before-Prose**, **Lift Into Story**). Flag and fix before delivering the plan.

- **The three walls are physical, not advisory (Pattern 5, AN-4):** Phase 1 has zero words of the piece written; Phase 2 has cards/clusters and *no prose*; Phase 3 writes only against a spine that already exists. If any phase leaks into another, the plan has failed at its one job. The transition from Phase 1→2→3 is marked by a *date or criterion*, not a feeling.
- **The iceberg ratio is set and honest (Hidden Knowledge):** Phase 1 has a stated submerged:visible target and a gap list that must be empty to exit. A plan that lets drafting begin on the tip with no iceberg underneath is not Orlean's method — "every time I'm stuck, I just haven't reported enough."
- **Phase 2 produces structure, not prose (Pattern 6):** the deliverable of digestion is a *theme-clustered* outline (themes, not topics) decided on the table, with connective-tissue seams marked. If the plan skips Phase 2 because it "produces no words," it has cut the exact step that makes the difference — the unproductive-looking phase is load-bearing.
- **The quota is finite, hittable, and framed as liberation (Pattern 13):** Phase 3 has a daily word number, a days/week cadence, marathon pacing, and the daily read-aloud edit ritual. The timeline is computed once for expectations, then explicitly set aside. A plan that frames the quota as a constraint rather than the budget-that-frees has missed the counterintuitive core.
- **The subject earned the process (Decision Framework Q1):** the gate confirms in-love + telling-subject + prism *before* the phases. The three phases amplify; they do not rescue a forced subject. If Q1 is shaky, the plan routes to `orlean-telling-subject` first.
- **[Rescue path] the broken wall is named and re-entry is correct (Step 0):** a stalled project is diagnosed to a specific collapsed wall and dropped back to the prior phase — not "pushed through" with more drafting. The rescue is almost always *more Phase 2*.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Drafting to figure out what you think (AN-4 — the master failure).** Opening a document and writing prose to "discover" the structure. It *feels* productive (words accumulate) but it's the most expensive possible way to find a spine — every restructure means re-drafting, not re-moving a card, and you usually end with eleven rewritten openings and a pile that won't cohere. **Recovery:** stop drafting entirely. Run Step 0's diagnosis, drop to Phase 2, atomize what you have onto cards, and decide the spine on the table where rearranging is free. "I can't write yet. I don't know what I'm writing about" is not a confession of weakness — it's the discipline.

- **Skipping Phase 2 because it "doesn't look productive."** The digestion phase ends each day with cards moved and no prose to show, so it gets cut under deadline pressure — straight from a full research binder into drafting. The result is topical sections that *inform* but never *tell* (AN-3, the snoozefest), with visible joints between them. **Recovery:** honor the phase Orlean says "we underestimate the amount of time we need" for. Budget real calendar days for cards-and-clusters with zero prose expected. The hour you spend moving a card saves the week you'd spend re-drafting a chapter.

- **Under-researching the iceberg, then stalling in Phase 3.** Treating Phase 1 as "enough to start" rather than saturation, so mid-draft you keep hitting facts you don't have and grind to a halt. **Recovery:** when you stall mid-draft, suspect a Phase-1 gap before a writing problem — "every time I'm stuck, I just haven't reported enough." Set an honest iceberg ratio up front (20–40× for a book) and don't exit Phase 1 until the "still submerged" list holds nothing load-bearing.

- **Staring at the finish line instead of running the mile.** Computing "95,000 words ÷ 1,000 = 95 days" and feeling the whole weight every morning, which breeds the avoidance Orlean names ("I think I'll go organize my knife drawer"). **Recovery:** compute the timeline *once* to set expectations, write it on the plan, then never look at it again. Marathon pacing — the quota for *today* is the only number that exists. A thousand words today, and then you did it.
