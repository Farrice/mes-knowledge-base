# Be Brutal — The Read-Aloud Cut and the "Would" Hunt (Susan Orlean)

Orlean *loves* editing. Not endures — loves. "I'm so pleased to think I'm making it better that I don't mind the tedium." The computer is what turned cutting from a chore (you used to retype the whole page) into a pleasure, and she became "a very good editor of my own work, lean pretty hard on myself, remove extra flab." Her one named, almost-foolproof trick is to read every sentence *aloud*: it is how you hear a sentence that doesn't work, hear the repetition, hear the joint that grinds — and "sometimes when you're reading out loud and you get a little bored, that's a sign that it's maybe a little boring." That sentence is the whole engine. Your boredom is not a mood; it is data. This workflow runs the draft through stacked read-aloud passes, each turning the dial to "a little bit of a higher level of polish," and in the line pass it hunts the specific crutches Orlean hunts in herself: the habitual "would" a New Yorker editor flagged for her ("I went to the store," not "I would go to the store" — now "I see it now all the time"), the passive constructions, the too-many adjectives. The spirit is Enzo Ferrari's: make every part better and better. The output is not "feedback." It is a cut draft and the ledger of what you killed.

- **Expert-Specific Quality Rubric** — especially the **Editing Discipline**, **Restraint & Pick-One**, and **Lift Into Story** rows; the savant bar is "read aloud and ruthlessly cut pass after pass — no boring stretches, no habitual 'would,' no padding."

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before cutting — the editing-relevant gates are **Q7** (am I pulling my punches, or trying to impress? — the tell you're cutting *toward*) and **Q8** (did I read it aloud and cut the crutches? — the test this workflow performs). Two scope checks first. (1) **This is an editing pass, not a rewrite.** It assumes a draft exists that is fundamentally the right story, told in the right structure, on a subject the writer loves. If the draft is *boring because the subject is wrong* (Q1 fails), or *a pile because there's no structure* (Q5/Pattern 6), no read-aloud cut saves it — route to `orlean-telling-subject` or `orlean-three-phase-process` first. Brutality polishes a real thing; it cannot manufacture one. (2) **Cut, don't flatten.** The goal is to remove flab so the steak is à la carte — not to plane every sentence down to grey. The pulled punch (Pattern 9) means some lines *should* hit hard; the cut protects those by clearing the noise around them, never by sanding them off.

## Input Required

- **The draft** — the actual full text to be cut (not a summary; we read every sentence aloud, so we need every sentence). Paste it whole.
- **The format & target voice** — essay/Substack, feature, social post, ghostwritten client piece, marketing/brand narrative, converting copy. (Drives how hard to cut, how much "would" is defensible by tone, and how short the rhythm should run.)
- **Pass intent** — *full multi-pass polish* (the default — flab → would/passive → adjectives → rhythm/read-aloud), or *targeted* (just the "would" hunt, just the boredom flag, just the adjective consolidation). The dual-QA stage of a pipeline usually wants the full stack.
- **Lines that must survive** (optional) — any punch the writer knows is load-bearing and wants protected, so the cut clears *around* it rather than through it.
- **Freshness state** (optional) — was this written today, or has it sat? Orlean's reread-while-fresh-but-slept-on ritual works best on yesterday's words; if it's same-day, the read-aloud is doing extra work the overnight gap usually does.

## Workflow

> The governing instruction for every pass: **read it aloud.** Not in your head — aloud, or sub-vocalized sentence by sentence as if performing it for a listener across a dinner table. Every diagnostic below is an *ear* test, not an eye test. The eye forgives what the ear catches.

### Step 1 — Pass A: The Boredom Flag (read the whole thing aloud; mark where you drift)

Read the entire draft aloud, start to finish, **before changing a single word.** You are not editing yet — you are *listening* for the two things the ear catches that the eye misses: the place you get bored, and the place you stumble.

- **The boredom flag (the core trick).** Mark every passage where, reading aloud, your attention drifts — where you "get a little bored." Orlean's rule is literal: that boredom "is a sign that it's maybe a little boring." Do not rationalize it ("the reader needs this context"). Flag it. A bored *writer* reading their own beloved draft aloud is the most charitable possible audience; if *you* drift, the reader left two sentences ago.
- **The stumble flag.** Mark every sentence you trip over, have to re-breathe inside, or read twice to get the cadence. A sentence that doesn't work *aloud* doesn't work; the stumble is the ear catching a broken joint, a buried clause, or a repetition the eye glided past.
- **The repetition flag.** Mark words, sounds, and sentence-shapes that recur close together — the ear hears "began… beginning… begun" three lines apart instantly; the eye doesn't.

Tag each flag rather than fixing it yet (fixing mid-read breaks the listen). Use a fast diagnostic:

| Flag (heard aloud) | What it almost always means | Likely fix in a later pass |
|---|---|---|
| **You got bored** | The passage is informing, not telling — a stretch of bullet-points-in-prose, or scaffolding the reader doesn't need (AN-3) | Cut it, or *lift* it into story (mood, character, a stake) — see Step 5 |
| **You stumbled** | A broken joint, a buried main clause, or a sentence carrying too many ideas | Re-cadence; split; move the main clause to where the breath lands |
| **You heard a repeat** | Echoed word/sound/structure too close together — twin sentence-shapes, anaphora overstaying | Vary one instance; cut the weaker; restore plain prose between hits |
| **You heard yourself "performing"** | The prose is reaching to be admired — the ninth-grade-teacher tell (AN-1) | Plain it down; reserve the force for the one line that earns it (Pattern 9) |

Output of Pass A: a flagged map of the draft. Now you cut.

### Step 2 — Pass B: Remove the Flab (be brutal — "lean pretty hard on myself")

Work the boredom and stumble flags from Pass A. This is the brutal pass — the one Orlean means by "remove extra flab." The bar is not "is this sentence fine?" The bar is **"is this sentence earning its place, or merely surviving?"** Survivors get cut.

The flab kill-list, hardest hits first:

| Flab type | Diagnostic | The cut |
|---|---|---|
| **The bored passage** | You drifted reading it aloud | Delete it outright, or compress 5 sentences to 1. The instinct "but I researched this" is the iceberg fallacy — most research stays submerged (it's *why* the tip floats, not what goes on the page). |
| **The throat-clear** | The piece warms up for 2–3 sentences before it actually starts | Cut to the first sentence that does work. The real lead is usually buried under the run-up. |
| **The over-explained** | You make a point, then re-make it, then summarize it | Keep the sharpest single instance; cut the restatements. Trust the reader. |
| **The hedged sentence** | "It could perhaps be argued that, in some sense, this might…" | Strip the hedges; say the thing or cut the thing. |
| **The clever-for-clever's-sake** | A line that's there to show you thought about the writing, not because the story needs it | Cut it — even if you love it. *Especially* if you love it. (Murder the darling that's only a darling.) |

The brutality test, applied to any line you're unsure about: **read the paragraph aloud with the line, then again without it.** If "without" reads cleaner and loses nothing the reader needed — it was flab. The default verdict on a tie is *cut*. Orlean's whole edge is that she leans *hard* on herself; a soft editor of their own work keeps everything.

### Step 3 — Pass C: The "Would" Hunt (kill the habitual passive crutch)

Now the named line-level surgery. Hunt the habitual "would" — the placeholder that softens direct past action into a vague, recurring, deep-past haze. "Instead of saying *I went to the store*, we very often will say *I would go to the store*." It is "a little passive… a placeholder," and "when you remove it, it's really striking, and you realize you write a much stronger sentence without it."

Search the draft for every "would," and run each through the gate:

| "Would" instance | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Habitual past masquerading as a single event** — "Every morning I would make coffee and I would read the paper" | **CUT** → "Every morning I made coffee and read the paper" | Direct past is stronger; the "would" is a crutch. This is the default and most common case. |
| **Single completed past action** — "When she called, I would answer" | **CUT** → "When she called, I answered" | This isn't even habitual; it's flat-out weaker. Hard cut. |
| **True conditional / hypothetical** — "If the fire reached the stacks, the building would be lost" | **KEEP** | Genuine conditional. "Would" carries real meaning here. |
| **Deliberate deep-past / fairy-tale tone** — "Every summer of my childhood, we *would* drive to the lake" (and you *want* that nostalgic, time-blurred wash) | **KEEP — but earn it** | Defensible *only* if the recurring-haze tone is intentional and rationed. One or two in a piece for effect; not a tic. |

While you're in the grammar, sweep the two crutches that travel with "would":

- **The passive voice.** "Mistakes were made," "the decision was reached," "it was discovered that." Find the actor and let them act: "He made mistakes." Passive hides agency and drains the sentence. Keep it only where the actor is genuinely unknown or deliberately suppressed.
- **The adjective pile-up (preview of Pass D).** Note the noun-strings now; you'll consolidate them next. (Flag, don't fix — Pass D is where one clean punch replaces the mush.)

The "would" hunt has a measurable output: a **count** of woulds found, woulds cut, and woulds kept-with-reason. A draft that leaves "I would" and "I went to" both unmarked has not run this pass.

### Step 4 — Pass D: Pick One (consolidate the adjective and example stacks)

Orlean names "too many adjectives" as a bad habit, and the principle is the pick-one (Pattern 10): "each description you use should be a real punch, a really strong punch of visual imagery." Too many "get sort of mushy" and "cancel it all out." Run every descriptive cluster and every multi-example point through consolidation.

- **Adjective strings.** For each noun carrying two-plus adjectives, ask: which *single* one is the strongest, most specific, most picture-able? Keep that; cut the rest. "Ancient, weathered, melancholy cobblestones" → pick the one that does real work. "Mostly clean, fairly tidy, reasonably organized desk" → just *bare*, or just *spotless* — the one true word.
  - **The Laroche exception (the only license to keep a run):** a string survives *only* when each adjective is "clean and mean" and names one *distinct* quality, building a single jarring picture — "tall, skinny as a stick, pale-eyed, slouch-shouldered, and sharply handsome in spite of the fact that he is missing all his front teeth." That's not a pile-up; it's a portrait where every word adds a *different* fact. Test: if any two adjectives are doing the *same* job, it's mush — cut to one. If each does a different job and the picture sharpens, keep.
- **Example strings.** Where the draft proves a point with five examples, keep the *one* most vivid and cut to it. "People can only absorb a certain amount." One unforgettable example out-persuades six forgettable ones.
- **The flooding test.** Read the descriptive passage aloud. Can you actually *see* the thing — one sharp image — or did the modifiers blur into static? If it's static, you stacked. Pick one and hit it.

### Step 5 — Pass E: Restore the Pulled Punch (rebuild the passive/hit/passive rhythm)

The previous passes removed weakness; this one restores *shape*. Orlean's rhythm is plain confident prose where the reader listens "passively," then "really hit them with something very strong where they're going to go, whoa" — then back to quiet. Cutting can accidentally flatten a draft into uniform grey (all punches sanded off) or, worse, leave it "boom boom boom boom" (every line shouting, so none lands). Read the cut draft aloud one more time and tune the dynamics.

| What you hear aloud | The problem | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat grey throughout** | The cut removed the hits along with the flab; nothing lands | Find the one or two moments that *should* hit (the surprise, the stake, the turn) and let them be strong — clear the plain prose around them so they ring |
| **"Boom boom boom" — every line punchy** | No quiet to make any punch land; exhausting (Pattern 9) | Pull most of the punches back to plain; reserve force for the single moment that earns it. "It's hard to do that if you're like boom boom boom boom." |
| **A hit landing in a noisy paragraph** | The strong line is surrounded by other strong lines and gets lost | Quiet the neighbors. The hit lands *because* of the silence around it, not its own volume. |
| **A protected "must-survive" line buried** | The cut cleared around it but the rhythm still smothers it | Give it its own short sentence, its own breath; end-weight it where the ear naturally stops |

The success feel: passive comfort → sudden strong hit → comfort again → another hit. The reader should be lulled, then slapped, then lulled — never relentlessly intense and never uniformly flat.

### Step 6 — Pass F: The Final Read-Aloud (the dial-turn confirmation)

The closing pass exists to confirm the brutality worked — and to perform the "next-day" discipline if the draft was written fresh. Read the *whole thing* aloud once more, cold, as if you're reading it to a stranger across a dinner table for the first time.

- **The boredom re-check.** Did any Pass-A flag survive? If you still drift anywhere, that passage didn't get cut hard enough. Go back; this is non-negotiable — boredom aloud is boredom on the page.
- **The stumble re-check.** Can you read it through without tripping, re-breathing, or re-reading a line to get the cadence? If you stumble, the joint is still broken.
- **The dial-turn check (Orlean's "higher level of polish").** Each pass should have left the draft "a little bit of a higher level of polish." Name what *this* read-aloud improved over the last one. If a pass changed nothing, you've hit diminishing returns — stop. (Orlean read entire books "many times," each pass turning the dial; the dial does eventually max out. Don't sand past polish into deadness.)
- **The fresh-eyes rule.** If this draft was written today and you can afford it, *stop here and reread tomorrow* before declaring done — Orlean rereads yesterday's words first thing, fresh-but-slept-on, and edits them hard before continuing. The overnight gap catches what same-day ears can't. If you can't wait, note that the draft hasn't had the overnight pass — it's a known gap, not a finished one.

Then deliver the cut draft and the ledger.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social media** (LinkedIn / X / Substack note) | Brutal is *more* important at short length — every flab word is a larger fraction of the whole, and a bored reader scrolls in a heartbeat. Read the post aloud; if you drift even once in 150 words, cut hard. "Would" and passive are silent killers of a hook's punch. Restore exactly *one* hit per post (Pick-One is law here) and keep the rest plain so it lands. The throat-clear is the #1 social flab — the real first line is usually line three. |
| **Marketing / brand narrative** | The boredom flag catches the "feature-list-in-prose" snoozefest (AN-3) instantly — if you bore yourself listing what the product does, lift it into story or cut it. Consolidate the adjective-stacked product copy ("revolutionary, innovative, cutting-edge platform") to one true word, or none. Passive voice hides who does what ("results are delivered") — name the actor. |
| **Copy** (VSL / landing / email) | Read aloud as the *buyer*, not the writer — boredom here is a lost sale, not a lost reader. Kill every hedge (hedged copy doesn't convert). The passive-voice sweep matters double: "you'll be guided through" → "we walk you through." But protect the reserved hits — converting copy needs its punches; clear the flab *around* the CTA and the proof line, don't flatten them. (This pass tunes the prose's *ear*; it never touches the *claim* — substantiation is the copywriter's job.) |
| **Ghostwriting** | Read the client's voice aloud to catch what's *false* to them as fast as what's boring — a sentence that stumbles often stumbles because it isn't how they'd actually say it. The "would" hunt is voice-critical: an over-"would"-ed draft reads tentative, which is rarely the client's brand. Strip passive crutches to make them sound decisive. Flag any cut that risks changing *meaning* for client review — brutality on flab, deference on substance. |
| **Content / series** | Run the full multi-pass stack as the dual-QA gate before any installment ships. The repetition flag matters across the *series*, not just the piece — the ear catches a stock phrase or sentence-shape you reuse every episode (your tic), which the eye never does within a single draft. Each installment should clear the same boredom bar; a series is only as un-boring as its weakest read-aloud. |

### Worked example A — Essay / Substack passage (memoir line)

**Before (the writer's fresh draft, read aloud):**
> "Every single morning of that long, difficult, lonely summer, I would wake up before dawn, and I would walk down to the small, quiet, empty beach, and I would sit there and I would think about all of the many things that I had perhaps possibly lost."

**Pass A (boredom flag):** drifted by the second "I would" — the sentence is *one event* wearing a habitual costume, padded with adjective triples and hedges. **Pass C (would):** four habitual "woulds," all CUT (single morning ritual, stronger in direct past — though one *deep-past* "would" could survive if the nostalgic haze were the intent; here it's a tic, so cut). **Pass D (pick-one):** "long, difficult, lonely" → pick one (*lonely* does the emotional work); "small, quiet, empty beach" → *empty* (it already implies quiet and small); "all of the many things that I had perhaps possibly lost" → strip the hedge-pile. **Pass E (rhythm):** the cut lets the last clause finally *hit*.

**After (cut):**
> "That lonely summer, I woke before dawn and walked down to the empty beach and sat and thought about everything I'd lost."

**Why it's better:** direct past replaces the four-"would" haze; one adjective per noun makes each picture sharp instead of mush; the hedge ("perhaps possibly") is gone, so "everything I'd lost" lands as a quiet punch. Read aloud, you no longer drift — and the last three words are now strong *because* the sentence around them went plain. That's the pulled punch restored.

### Worked example B — Marketing / brand line (product copy)

**Before (read aloud as the buyer):**
> "Our innovative, cutting-edge, industry-leading platform was designed by a team of experts to deliver powerful, transformative, game-changing results that will be experienced by users across a wide variety of different use cases."

**Pass A:** bored immediately — this is a feature-list in prose (AN-3), no story, no actor. **Pass C (passive sweep):** "was designed… to deliver… will be experienced by users" — three passives hiding everyone who does anything; restore the actors. **Pass D (pick-one):** "innovative, cutting-edge, industry-leading" → mush, cancels out; pick zero (these are claims, not images) or one true thing. "powerful, transformative, game-changing" → same mush. **Pass B (flab):** "across a wide variety of different use cases" is pure padding. **Pass E:** lift it from a feature-list into a told thing with a stake.

**After (cut):**
> "We built this for the work you keep putting off. It does the part you dread, in minutes."

**Why it's better:** the actor is back (*we built*, *it does*); the adjective static is gone entirely (the claim is now in the *specific*, not the modifier-pile); the passive-voice fog lifts; and it's *told* — there's a reader, a stake (the dreaded work), a payoff. Read aloud, it doesn't bore. One quiet hit ("the part you dread") instead of six cancelled-out superlatives.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Editing Discipline**, **Restraint & Pick-One**, **Lift Into Story**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Read aloud, actually (Pattern 11):** every pass was an *ear* test, performed aloud (or fully sub-vocalized), not skimmed with the eye. If the draft was only read silently, this workflow did not run. The boredom flag is the engine and it only fires aloud.
- **No boredom survives (Pattern 11, AN-3):** there is no passage where the reader (you, aloud, the most charitable audience) drifts. Boredom aloud = boring on the page = cut. A remaining bored stretch is a failed gate, not a stylistic choice.
- **No stumbles survive:** the whole thing reads through aloud without tripping, re-breathing inside a sentence, or re-reading a line to get its cadence. A stumble is a broken joint the ear caught.
- **The "would" was hunted (Pattern 12, AN-5):** every "would" was found and judged; the habitual/single-event ones are cut to direct past; only true conditionals and *intentionally* deep-past ones survived, each defensible by tone. The ledger carries a real found/cut/kept count.
- **Passive crutches removed (AN-5):** passive constructions were swept and the actor restored, except where the actor is genuinely unknown or deliberately suppressed.
- **Pick-one enforced (Pattern 10, AN-2):** adjective strings and example stacks consolidated to the single strongest, picture-able one — unless a run survives the Laroche exception (each word names a *distinct* quality, sharpening one portrait). No mush that cancels itself out.
- **The punch is pulled, then hit (Pattern 9):** the cut restored a passive/hit/passive rhythm — plain confident prose with force reserved for earned moments — not uniform grey and not "boom boom boom." Protected lines ring *because* the noise around them is gone.
- **Cut, not flattened:** the draft is leaner *and still has its shape and its hits*. The brutality removed flab; it did not sand the steak off the plate. (If the draft now reads safe and lifeless, the cut went past polish into deadness — Pass E restores the hits.)
- **It's a cut draft, not feedback:** the deliverable is the revised text ready to ship plus a change ledger — not a list of suggestions for someone else to apply.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Reading silently and calling it a read-aloud.** The eye forgives boredom, repetition, and the "would" that the ear catches in a second — silent "reading aloud" is just reading, and it misses the one signal the whole method runs on. **Recovery:** actually vocalize it, sentence by sentence, as if performing for a listener. If you feel self-conscious, that *is* the test — the listener you imagine is the reader you're writing for. The boredom flag does not fire any other way.

- **Flattening instead of cutting — sanding every sentence to grey.** Mistaking "brutal" for "make everything plain and short," so the cut removes the hits along with the flab and leaves a competent, lifeless draft. Orlean cuts flab to *make the punches land*, not to eliminate them. **Recovery:** run Pass E. Find the one or two moments that should hit, protect them, and clear the plain prose *around* them. Brutality is removing the noise that smothers the signal — never removing the signal.

- **Cutting every "would" mechanically, including the ones that mean something.** Over-correcting the hunt into a find-and-replace that murders true conditionals and the *intentional* deep-past nostalgia tone, leaving sentences that are now grammatically wrong or tonally dead. **Recovery:** run each "would" through the Step-3 gate. Habitual-masquerading-as-single-event and flat single-past → cut. True conditional ("if X, then Y *would*") and a deliberately rationed nostalgic haze → keep, with the reason logged. The hunt is surgical, not scorched-earth.

- **Editing a draft that needs rewriting (wrong-tool failure).** Running the read-aloud cut on a draft that's boring because the *subject* is wrong, or a pile because there's *no structure* — and getting a beautifully polished version of the wrong thing. Brutality polishes; it cannot manufacture a story. **Recovery:** check the Pre-Flight scope gate. If the boredom is everywhere and structural (not local flab), stop cutting and route to `orlean-telling-subject` (is this even a story?) or `orlean-three-phase-process` (is there a spine?). Polish a real thing, not a wrong one.
