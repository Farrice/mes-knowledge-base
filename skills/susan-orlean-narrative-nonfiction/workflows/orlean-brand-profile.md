# The Orlean Brand Profile — Profile a Founder or Brand as a Telling Subject (Susan Orlean)

Nobody set out to read a company's "About" page. They tolerate it. The chronological founding story ("in 2014, frustrated by X, our founder set out to…"), the values list ("we believe in craft, transparency, and putting people first"), the mission statement — these are the brand-narrative equivalent of reading bullet points aloud, and Orlean's verdict on bullet points is *snoozefest*. This workflow profiles a founder or brand the way Orlean profiled a TV-show dog and a swamp full of orchids: it refuses to write *the company* and instead finds the **telling subject** — one small, real, founding specific (a chipped part, a refund, a single afternoon, an object the founder won't throw away) that secretly opens onto the company's whole reason to exist. It turns that specific as a prism (the product, the people, the human need it serves — all one story), opens on a *wait-what* lead that seduces a reader who doesn't yet care about the company, holds the voice as the reader's slightly-ahead proxy rather than the brand's hype-man, and lets the deeper "why this matters" *vibrate* through the telling instead of being stamped on as a thesis. It is the cross-domain application of her literary instinct to the place brand writing is most reliably dead. **Honesty spine, non-negotiable: every beat is built only on genuinely-felt, true material — the wait-what detail must be real (Laroche really had no front teeth), and the "reason to exist" must be one the company actually lives, not one stapled on to sound profound. The stench of inauthenticity is very easy to smell.**

Orlean supplies the *narrative spine and stance*. She is not the positioning strategist, not the founder's ghostwriter, not the visual-system designer — she's the writer who finds the small true thing the company is secretly about and tells it so a stranger leans in. Hand the spine she produces to `oren-brand` for positioning and visual system, to `ghostwrite` for the founder's first-person voice, to `stanton-brand-origin` for the change arc, and to `wright-thompson` when the myth/reality gap is the story.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this workflow fails the instant it's run as a chronology-generator or a values-list dressed up as a story):

- **Pattern 1 — The Telling Subject** — the core move: reject the obvious "here's our mission" framing; find the overlooked small founding specific (the door) that secretly carries the company's reason to exist (the house). *The subject is the door; the theme is the house.*
- **Pattern 2 — The Prism Test** — turn the founding specific 4-6 (here: 3-5) directions — **product + people + the human need it serves** — and confirm they're all *one* story, not a grab-bag. Depth, not width.
- **Pattern 4 — The "Wait, What" Lead** — for a brand a reader doesn't think they need, the lead does *all* the work: one true, jarring, picture-able detail (often a *person* — people are interested in people) that forces the involuntary "wait, what."
- **Pattern 3 — The Reader's Proxy** — write as the slightly-ahead proxy ("I didn't think a knife company could move me either, and then —"), never the brand's superior hype-man. Shared discovery seduces; the press release lectures.
- **Hidden Knowledge** — especially **The Inauthenticity Stench** (the in-love test, transferred to the founder: build only on what they're genuinely burning to discuss) and **The Memory-Made-Permanent Instinct** (the quiet undertone that *vibrates*, repurposed here as the brand's "reason to exist" that radiates, never states).
- **Cross-Domain Applications** — the **Entrepreneurship / founder story** row (telling subject · prism · memory-permanence undertone · inauthenticity test) and the **Marketing** row (the overlooked product/operational specific over the mission statement).
- **Decision Framework** — the gate below (Q1 in-love, Q2 telling subject, Q3 prism), plus Q4 (proxy not superior).
- **Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-3 (Bullet-Point Information / the Snoozefest)** = the values list and the chronology; **AN-6 (Forcing a Subject You Don't Love)** = the borrowed founder enthusiasm; **AN-1 (Overwriting to Impress)** = the inflated brand-poetry; and the honesty spine throughout.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before producing a single line. Specifically confirm **Q1** (is the *founder genuinely burning* to talk about this specific — or is this the marketing deck talking? Borrowed founder enthusiasm is the smelliest of all, AN-6), **Q2** (have I found a *telling subject* — a small, real, founding specific — instead of writing "the company" or its values?), **Q3** (does it pass the prism test — does the specific turn toward product, people, *and* the human need and stay one story?), and **Q4** (am I the reader's *proxy* — "I didn't expect to care either" — or the brand's *hype-man* — "you need to know how great we are"?). **Honesty spine gate (HARD):** every narrative beat must be built only on genuinely-felt, *true* material. The wait-what detail must be real and verifiable; the "reason to exist" must be one the company actually lives. If the only intriguing detail you can find is one you'd have to manufacture, stop — this pass tells a true story compellingly; it never invents a charming origin myth. A fabricated founding detail is the failure mode to kill on sight (route the brand back to its *real* history, not through Orlean's craft).

## Input Required

- **The company** — what it makes/does, and roughly who it's for. One or two sentences. (You're profiling it, so you need the real thing.)
- **The founder (if it's a founder profile)** — the actual person whose story this is, and *the single thing they get visibly alive talking about* — the detail, object, moment, or grievance they can't stop returning to off-script. This is the raw material for the door. (If the only thing they light up about is "the vision," you have a deck, not a story — see Q1.)
- **Raw founding material** — the real, specific, *true* particulars: the actual first product, the first customer, the room it started in, the object the founder kept, the complaint that started it, the thing that nearly killed the company. Specifics, not summaries. We are hunting for the door inside these.
- **The reason to exist** — your honest best guess at *why this company actually needs to exist* beneath the category language. Not "we sell knives" — the human need under it. (This becomes the vibrating undertone; we'll sharpen it in Step 5.)
- **Format & length** — long-form profile / About page / founder LinkedIn or essay / sales-page origin section / brand-film narration / pitch-deck story slide. (Drives prism depth and how much runway the lead gets.)
- **The current draft, if one exists** — the generic/chronological/values-list version that triggered this pass. We diagnose what made it dead, then rebuild on the telling subject.
- **Honesty-spine confirmation** — the founding specifics provided are *true* and verifiable. We tell a real story well; we do not manufacture a better one.

## Workflow

### Step 0 — Diagnose why the current draft is dead (the three brand-narrative snoozefests)

If a draft exists, name *which* failure it committed before you rebuild — because the rebuild is the inverse of the diagnosis. Almost every dead founder/brand narrative is one of three, and all three are AN-3 (bullet-point information that informs instead of tells) wearing different costumes.

| The dead pattern | What it reads like | Why it's dead (Orlean's lens) | The Orlean inverse (where you're headed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The chronology** | "Founded in 2014. In 2016 we raised X. In 2019 we launched Y." | A timeline informs; it never *tells*. Order of events is not a story — it's a résumé. No telling subject, no prism, no reason to lean in. | Find the *one founding specific* and open the whole company through it (Pattern 1). The reader doesn't need the order; they need the door. |
| **The values list** | "We believe in craft, transparency, and putting people first." | Stated values are the *thesis stamped on the wall* — the moral announced, never earned. A reader can't feel a bullet point. It's the snoozefest (AN-3) and the un-vibrated theme (AN-1). | Let the values *vibrate* out of a real scene (Step 5). Show the founder refusing the cheaper part; never write "we value quality." |
| **The hero-founder hype** | "Visionary founder set out to disrupt the industry…" | Written from above — the brand's superiority, not the reader's curiosity. The reader is being lectured by a press release, defenses up. | Reader-as-proxy stance (Step 4): "I didn't think I'd care about a [category] company either, and then —." Sympathy seduces; superiority bounces. |

> **The diagnosis-to-direction move:** circle the one that fits. If it's the chronology, your missing piece is the *telling subject* (Steps 1-2). If it's the values list, your missing piece is the *vibrating undertone* (Step 5). If it's hero hype, your missing piece is the *proxy stance and the wait-what lead* (Steps 3-4). Most dead drafts are all three at once — which is fine; the whole workflow fixes all three. (No prior draft? Skip to Step 1 — you're building it right from the start.)

### Step 1 — Reject "the company"; find the founding specific (the door) (Pattern 1)

You are not writing *the company*. "The company" is the spotlight subject — the dignified, abstract, everyone-already-frames-it-this-way version, and the reader arrives with defenses up and zero curiosity. Orlean never writes "Hollywood"; she writes one dog who built it. You will never write "our commitment to craft"; you'll write the one chipped part the founder wouldn't replace.

Mine the raw founding material for the **door**: the small, concrete, real, slightly-strange specific that nobody would expect a brand to build a story on — and which, opened, lets the whole company out.

| Move | The spotlight version (reject) | The founding specific (hunt for) | Why the specific wins |
|---|---|---|---|
| Go smaller than the company | "our mission to bring craft back to the kitchen" | the single chipped tip on the founder's grandmother's knife she used for forty years | The reader has no opinion about a chip; you get to seduce from scratch |
| Go to the object, not the abstraction | "quality and durability" | the actual thing the founder kept, fixed, refused to throw away, or built first by hand | A door must be a *physical* door — you can point at it; abstractions can't be opened |
| Go to the person | "the team" / "our customers" | one real person rendered through one true, specific contradiction (people are interested in people, Pattern 4) | A reader leans toward a human before they'll lean toward a logo |
| Go to the friction, not the triumph | "we set out to disrupt the industry" | the complaint, the refund, the part that nearly killed the company, the thing that went wrong | Tension and specificity carry a story; triumph-narration is a press release |

> **The "why would a [category] company tell *that* story?" test (Pattern 1 success metric):** say your candidate specific aloud to a skeptic. If their honest reaction is *"why would a knife company write about a chip?"* — good, that dismissibility is the engine. If their reaction is *"oh, the usual founder origin story"* — you picked the spotlight version; go smaller, stranger, more concrete. The gap between the trivial-seeming surface and the hidden richness *is* the seduction.

Write the chosen specific in concrete, picture-able, **true** terms. This is the **door**. (Honesty spine: confirm it actually happened.)

### Step 2 — Name the house: the company's reason to exist that the door secretly carries (Pattern 1; the memory-permanence instinct, repurposed)

A door is only worth opening if there's a house behind it. The founding specific is the *door*; the company's **reason to exist** is the *house* — and not the category sentence ("we sell knives"), but the human need underneath it. This is the direct repurposing of Orlean's memory-permanence instinct: the library fire's surface was arson, but the house it secretly carried was *memory and permanence*. Your brand's surface is its product; the house is the human thing it's secretly about.

Ask of your door: **what large, human need does this small founding specific secretly carry?** Push past the first answer — the first answer is still the category. Keep going until you hit something a stranger would feel in their chest, something the company genuinely *lives* (honesty spine).

| Door (the founding specific) | First-layer "what we do" (still surface — keep going) | The house (the reason to exist — the human need it secretly serves) |
|---|---|---|
| The chipped tip on grandmother's forty-year knife | "we make durable, high-quality knives" | the difference between owning a thing and being in a relationship with it — making something built to be *kept*, in an economy built to be replaced |
| The first customer who drove three hours to return a $40 product and left a fan | "great customer service" | being *seen* by a company in a world that processes you as a ticket number |
| The founder rebuilding the same broken part by hand at 2am for a year | "engineering excellence / reliability" | the refusal to ship something you'd be ashamed to hand a friend — dignity as a product spec |

> **The "secretly" rule (honesty spine + AN-1 guard):** the reason to exist is what the company *secretly* serves — so it must never be worn on the surface as a slogan. You name the house *here, for your architecture*, so it can later **vibrate through the telling**. You are **not** writing the mission statement the reader will see. "We make things built to be kept" is the *banned* sentence; the *forty-year knife* is what radiates it. If your house can only exist as a stated value ("we believe in durability"), it isn't a vibrating reason to exist — it's a values-list bullet wearing a story (Step 0, AN-3). And it must be one the company *genuinely lives*: a profound-sounding reason stapled onto a company that doesn't actually behave that way is the stench (AN-1, AN-6) — the gap between the claimed house and the lived one is exactly what audiences smell.

Write the house in one plain sentence — the reason to exist you'll let vibrate.

### Step 3 — Turn the prism: product + people + human need, all one story (Pattern 2)

A telling subject and a reason to exist aren't enough — you have to prove the founding specific has *depth*, that it opens in several directions and they're all *the same story*. This is the prism test, adapted to a company: the three (sometimes more) native facets of a brand are **the product, the people, and the human need it serves.** Turn the door toward each and confirm every turn reveals a new face of *the same* company — not three different marketing themes bolted together.

| Facet (turn the prism) | What this direction opens onto | The binding test (still the same story?) |
|---|---|---|
| **The product** | the object itself — but as *character*, not spec sheet. What the founding specific reveals about how the thing is made, what it refuses to be, the choice baked into it. | Does the product detail *embody* the house, or is it a feature list smuggled in? It must turn the *same* door. |
| **The people** | the founder, the team, the customer — the humans the founding specific touched. People are interested in people (Pattern 4); this is where the reader's heart enters. | Are these humans living *this* story, or generic testimonials? Each must be a face of the same prism. |
| **The human need** | the house from Step 2 — the reason to exist, seen now from inside the specific. The deepest turn; the one the others were quietly opening onto. | Do product and people both keep *opening onto this need*, or do they trail off into unrelated brand messaging? |
| **(Optional 4th-5th)** the moment / the stakes / the world | the founding scene rendered in full; what was at risk; the larger shift the company is a small true argument against (disposability, anonymity, mediocrity). | Only add a facet that turns the *same* door. A bolted-on "industry trend" facet is the grab-bag failure. |

> **The grab-bag failure (Pattern 2 detection):** if your facets are really *different brand themes* sharing a logo ("our product → also our sustainability → also our community → also our app"), the prism is fake — you've bolted marketing pillars onto a company. Each facet must turn *the same founding specific* and reveal a new face of *the same reason to exist*. The chipped knife turns toward the steel (product), the grandmother and the someday-heir (people), and the refusal to be replaceable (need) — and it's *the same chip* every time. That's a prism. That binds.

> **Depth-not-width rule:** three facets that each get one shallow sentence is a survey, not a story. The test is whether turning the prism keeps revealing *more* — does the product detail deepen the human need, which deepens what the people are doing? If you hit bottom on the first turn, the house is probably too small (go back to Step 2); a bigger, truer reason to exist deepens all three facets at once.

### Step 4 — Build the wait-what lead and lock the proxy stance (Patterns 4 + 3)

For a brand the reader doesn't think they need, **the lead does all the work** — and the *stance* of the whole piece is set in its first lines. Two moves, locked together.

**The wait-what lead (Pattern 4).** Build the opening as a juxtaposition jarring enough to stop a scroll — ideally a *person* rendered through one true, specific, picture-able contradiction. Orlean's benchmark: *"John Laroche is a tall guy, skinny as a stick, pale-eyed, slouch-shouldered, and sharply handsome in spite of the fact that he is missing all his front teeth."* Handsome *and* toothless = *wait, what.* Find the brand/founder equivalent: the true detail that creates productive confusion and forces "wait, I have to read more."

| The lead must… | Why | Brand/founder examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead on a real, specific detail** (often a person) | People are interested in people; logos and missions are not people | "She kept a knife with a broken tip for forty years and got angry when anyone offered to buy her a new one." |
| **Contain a true juxtaposition** | The collision is what triggers "wait, what" | "The company's best salesman that year was a customer who'd driven three hours to return something." |
| **Withhold the company** at first | Open on the *specific*, not the brand name; let curiosity arrive before the logo | Don't open "At [Company], we…". Open on the chip, the drive, the 2am part. |
| **Be true** (HARD honesty spine) | The jarring detail must be real — Laroche really had no teeth. A manufactured hook is the failure mode | Confirm the lead detail actually happened. No invented charm. |

**The proxy stance (Pattern 3).** Lock the voice as the reader's *slightly-ahead proxy*, not the brand's hype-man. You — or the founder, if it's first-person — are someone who *also* didn't think they'd care, became fascinated, and is now turning back to say *keep coming, you won't believe what's here.* Sympathy to the reader's skepticism *because you held it too.*

| Hype-man stance (reject — the press release) | Proxy stance (adopt — the seduction) |
|---|---|
| "We're the leading / most innovative / fastest-growing…" | "I didn't think a [category] company could matter to me either, and then I saw the chip." |
| "You need to know how great our product is." | "Here's the small thing that made me stop dismissing this." |
| Superiority, defenses up | Shared discovery, defenses down — the reader feels *accompanied*, and trusts you *because* you admit you started skeptical |

> **The two-move check:** read the lead aloud. Does a stranger's involuntary reaction become *"wait, what?"* (= they'll read more)? And does the voice make them feel *accompanied* rather than *sold to*? If the lead names the company before the specific, or the voice is announcing greatness, you've reverted to the press release — rebuild on the true founding detail and the proxy stance.

### Step 5 — Let the reason to exist vibrate; write the thesis you will NOT write (Hidden Knowledge: Memory-Made-Permanent; AN-3, AN-1)

The final move separates an Orlean brand profile from a clever ad. The reason to exist — the house — must *vibrate through the telling*, never be announced as a thesis. This is the entire fix for the values-list death (Step 0): instead of *stating* "we believe in craft," you let the forty-year knife radiate it until the reader feels it in the chest without ever being told.

Take the house from Step 2 and commit, in writing, to vibration over statement.

- **Name the undertone in one felt sentence** — the human need under the category. (Not "we make durable knives" → "the things we refuse to replace are the ones holding our dead close.")
- **Write the thesis you will NOT write.** State the slogan/value plainly, once, *for yourself* — then mark it banned from the page. ("Our knives are built to last a lifetime and become heirlooms" = the slogan. Banned.) Naming the forbidden thesis is how you guarantee it vibrates instead of preaching. This is the single most reliable defense against the values list creeping back in.
- **Confirm it's true and lived, not stapled (honesty spine).** The undertone must be one the company *genuinely lives* and the founder *genuinely feels* — not a profound idea bolted on to dignify a transactional business. A stapled reason reads as overwriting-to-impress (AN-1) and borrowed enthusiasm (AN-6); a lived one radiates without effort. If the company doesn't actually live it, find the one it *does* — even a smaller true house beats a grand false one.

> **The vibrate test:** could a reader finish the profile, feel *exactly why this company needs to exist*, and yet not be able to point to the sentence where you *said it*? If yes, it vibrates — you've written a story, not an About page. If they could highlight your mission statement, you stated it; dissolve it back into the chip, the drive, the 2am part. The reason to exist is the heat under the floor, not the sign on the wall.

### Step 6 — Restraint pass: pull the punches, kill the brand-poetry, read it aloud (Patterns 9-12)

Brand writing's besetting sin is the opposite of Orlean's: it *overwrites to impress* — inflated adjectives, "boom boom boom" intensity, every line reaching to sound visionary. Default instead to plain, confident prose with force reserved for the moments that earn it.

- **Pick one (Pattern 10).** One clean, strong, picture-able image per beat — the chip, hit hard — not five mushy adjectives ("artisanal, premium, timeless, heritage, crafted"). Stacking cancels itself out. Find the single detail that carries the weight and stop.
- **Pull the punch, then hit (Pattern 9).** Let the reader sit in plain prose, then land one strong line where they go *whoa*. If it's relentless intensity, nothing lands. The forty-year detail hits *because* the surrounding sentences were quiet.
- **Read it aloud; cut the "would" and the crutches (Patterns 11-12).** Boring aloud = boring on the page. Hunt the habitual "would" ("she would keep the knife" → "she kept the knife"), the passive constructions, the adjective pile-ups, and the clever-for-clever's-sake brand-speak. Each pass turns the dial to a higher polish.
- **The ninth-grade-teacher check (AN-1).** Is any line reaching to be *admired* before it serves the story? Brand-poetry ("a testament to the relentless pursuit of perfection") is exactly this. Cut it to plain. When you really know what the company is about, you can say it simply.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | How the brand profile deploys |
|---|---|

One vivid image per post; the prism collapses to *one* turn per post (a series can rotate product → people → need across posts so they read as episodes, not standalone brags). The hype-man stance reads instantly at social length — kill it. |
| **Marketing** (About page / brand film / campaign) | Reject the mission-statement About page outright. Build the page *on the door*: the small founding specific, turned as the prism, with the reason to exist vibrating. Brand-film narration is native Orlean territory — the yarn told across a dinner table. The values list is the failure mode here above all others; if a line states a value, dissolve it into a scene (Step 5). |
| **Copywriting** (sales-page origin section / VSL backstory) | The origin section's job is to make a *cold* reader care before the offer. Wait-what lead does the seducing; the reason-to-exist vibrates so the *product* becomes the proof of the house, not a feature dump. **Honesty spine is sharpest here** — the founding detail and the reason to exist must be true, because copy converts on them. ### Worked example A — Premium kitchen-knife brand (About page + brand film)

**Company:** a small company making premium kitchen knives. · **Founder:** Marco, who can't stop talking about his grandmother. · **Format:** About page + brand-film narration.
**Honesty spine:** the grandmother's chipped knife is real; Marco genuinely refuses to design anything he wouldn't hand down. [confirmed]

> **Step 0 — Diagnosis:** the existing About page was the values list ("We believe in craftsmanship, sustainability, and timeless design") *and* the chronology ("Founded in 2018, Marco set out to…"). Missing pieces: a telling subject and a vibrating undertone.

> **Step 1 — The door:** Not "our commitment to craft" (spotlight). The founding specific: **the chipped tip on Marco's grandmother's knife — the one she used every day for forty years and got *angry* when anyone offered to replace.** *Why would a knife company write a page about a chip?* — exactly. Dismissible. Good. (True: she really kept it; the anger really happened.)

> **Step 2 — The house:** First-layer, rejected: "we make durable, high-quality knives." The house it secretly carries: **the difference between owning a thing and being in a relationship with it — making something built to be kept, in an economy built to be replaced.** (Lived: Marco actually designs for handing-down.)

> **Step 3 — The prism (three turns, one story):**
> - *Product* → the steel and the geometry, but told as *why a good knife earns a chip and keeps working* — character, not spec sheet. (Same chip.)
> - *People* → the grandmother who wouldn't replace it; Marco who finally understood why; the customer who'll someday hand theirs to a daughter. Knives as the things that outlive the hands that hold them. (Same chip.)
> - *Human need* → the quiet refusal of disposability — loving a thing enough to keep it. (Same chip.)
> Every turn is the *same* forty-year knife. Binds. Depth, not width.

> **Step 4 — Wait-what lead + proxy stance:**
> Lead: *"My grandmother kept a knife with a broken tip for forty years, and the one time I offered to buy her a new one, she didn't speak to me for the rest of the afternoon."* (Withholds the company; person + true juxtaposition — a broken tool she defended like an heir. *Wait, what?*)
> Proxy stance: *"I started this company thinking I was in the knife business. I was wrong about that for a long time."* (Slightly-ahead proxy; not "we're the leading artisanal knife brand.")

> **Step 5 — Reason to exist (vibrate, never state):**
> Undertone: *The things we refuse to replace are the ones holding our dead close.*
> The thesis NOT written: "Our knives are built to last a lifetime and become heirlooms." (The slogan — banned.) Instead: the chip, the forty years, the silent afternoon, and the reader feels the heirloom without being sold one.

> **Step 6 — Restraint:** one image (the chip) carries it; no "artisanal/premium/timeless" pile-up; the line *"she didn't speak to me for the rest of the afternoon"* lands *because* the sentences around it are plain. Read aloud, "would"s cut.

> **Handoff:** → `oren-brand` for positioning ("the knife you don't replace") and the visual system; → `ghostwrite` for Marco's first-person voice on the brand film; → `stanton-brand-origin` for the arc of Marco discovering what business he was actually in.

### Worked example B — B2B logistics-software startup (founder LinkedIn essay)

**Company:** a startup making freight-tracking software for small trucking outfits. · **Founder:** Dana, ex-dispatcher. · **Format:** founder LinkedIn essay.
**Honesty spine:** the 3am phone call is real; the lost load and the driver's name are real. [confirmed]

> **Step 0 — Diagnosis:** the draft was hero-founder hype ("As a visionary in logistics tech, I saw an opportunity to disrupt an outdated industry…"). Missing piece: the proxy stance and a wait-what lead. (Also no telling subject — pure abstraction.)

The founding specific: **the 3am call, back when Dana was a dispatcher, from a driver named Ray who'd been parked for six hours not knowing if the load he was carrying still existed — because nobody could tell him.** *Why would a software founder open on one driver's bad night?* — exactly. (True.)

> **Step 2 — The house:** First-layer, rejected: "real-time freight visibility." The house: **the human cost of being kept in the dark while your livelihood sits on a truck — software as the difference between a person knowing and a person guessing at 3am.** (Lived: Dana built the company specifically so no driver gets another Ray night.)

> **Step 4 — Wait-what lead + proxy stance:**
> Lead: *"At 3am a driver named Ray called me to ask if the load he'd been hauling for six hours still existed. I was the dispatcher, and I had no idea."* (Person + true juxtaposition — the guy whose job was to know, knowing nothing. *Wait, what?*)
> Proxy stance: *"I didn't start a software company because I love software. I'm honestly still suspicious of most of it."* (Proxy, not visionary.)

> **Step 5 — Reason to exist (vibrate, never state):**

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Telling-Subject Selection**, **Reader-as-Proxy Stance**, **Lead / Hook Seduction**, **Lift Into Story**, **Restraint & Pick-One**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **A telling subject, not "the company" (Pattern 1, AN-3):** the profile is built on one small, concrete, true founding specific — not the chronology, the values list, or "the company." A skeptic should ask "why would a [category] company tell *that* story?" If the profile opens on the mission or the timeline, you reverted to the snoozefest; rebuild on the door.
- **The reason to exist is carried, not stated (Pattern 1, AN-1, AN-3):** the house is what the founding specific *secretly* serves and it *vibrates* — never a stated value or mission sentence. The "thesis I will not write" is explicitly named and banned. A reader feels exactly why the company must exist yet can't find the sentence where you said it. If they could highlight a value, you wrote a values list.
- **The prism binds — product + people + need, all one story (Pattern 2):** each facet turns the *same* founding specific and reveals a new face of the *same* reason to exist — not separate brand pillars sharing a logo (the grab-bag failure). And depth, not width: turning the prism keeps revealing more.
- **The lead does all the work and it's true (Pattern 4, HARD honesty spine):** the opening is a real, jarring, picture-able detail (often a person) that triggers "wait, what" and withholds the company. The detail is *verifiably true* — manufactured charm is the failure mode killed on sight.
- **Proxy stance, not hype-man (Pattern 3):** the voice accompanies the reader ("I didn't expect to care either, and then —") rather than announcing the brand's greatness. The reader feels *accompanied*, not *sold to*.
- **Restraint — one image, pulled punches, plain prose (Patterns 9-12, AN-1, AN-2):** no adjective/value stacking; one clean image per beat; force reserved for earned moments; read aloud with the habitual "would" and crutches cut. No reaching-to-be-admired brand-poetry.
- **Honesty spine intact (HARD):** every narrative beat — the door, the lead detail, the reason to exist — is built only on genuinely-felt, *true* material the company actually lives. The craft makes a true story seduce harder; it never invents a charming origin myth. If a beat needs a detail or claim that isn't real, route it back to the company's real history (or to copy-engine/luke-iha for a real proof point) — never gild it with Orlean's craft.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Rebuilding the values list as a "story" (AN-3).** Taking "we believe in craft, transparency, and people" and wrapping it in narrative phrasing — "This is the story of a company that believes in craft…" — which is the same stamped thesis wearing a costume. The reader still feels lectured. **Recovery:** run Steps 2 and 5. Find the *founding specific* that the value lives inside (the chip, the 3am call), write the value as the *thesis you will NOT write*, and dissolve it into the scene. The value must vibrate out of a real moment, never be announced — even narratively.

- **Inventing or inflating the founding detail to make it more charming (honesty-spine breach — the most dangerous failure).** A wait-what lead so good it's tempting to "improve" the truth — sharpen the grandmother's anger, invent the 3am driver, round a real $40 refund into a tearjerker. The better the craft, the more convincing the lie. **Recovery:** before tuning a single line, confirm the founding detail actually happened and is verifiable. Orlean's whole spine is that Laroche *really* had no front teeth. If the true story isn't as charming as you'd like, that's a *selection* problem (go find a *different* true specific in Step 1), never a license to fabricate. A fabricated origin is the failure mode to kill on sight.

- **The hero-founder voice creeping back in (AN-3 / Pattern 3).** Starting in proxy stance, then sliding into "and that's why we became the industry leader in…" by the second paragraph — the press release reasserting itself once the lead's done its job. **Recovery:** re-read the *whole* piece aloud as a skeptical stranger, not just the lead. Every time the voice announces greatness instead of sharing discovery, rewrite it as the slightly-ahead proxy. The stance has to hold the entire way down, not just at the open.

- **Faking the prism with brand pillars (Pattern 2 grab-bag).** Listing "facets" that are really the marketing deck's themes — product, sustainability, community, app — and calling it depth, when none of them turn the *same* founding specific. **Recovery:** apply the binding test from Step 3. Every facet must turn the *same* door (the chip, the call) and reveal a new face of the *same* reason to exist. If a facet doesn't turn the founding specific, it's a separate brand message — cut it, or find the deeper house (Step 2) that makes the real facets cohere.
