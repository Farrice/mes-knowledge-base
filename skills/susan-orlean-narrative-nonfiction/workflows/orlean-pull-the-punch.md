# Pull the Punch — Restraint, Pick-One, and the Passive/Hit/Passive Rhythm (Susan Orlean)

A great steakhouse serves the steak nearly à la carte. No foam, no tweezered microgreens, no seven sauces — just the one thing, cooked perfectly, and the confidence to let it sit on the plate alone. Most overwritten prose is the opposite: every clause reaching to be admired, every noun wearing three adjectives, every paragraph at full volume — and the result is that nothing lands, because *everything* is landing. Orlean's hardest-won lesson is that this is what a writer does when they're still "trying to impress their ninth-grade English teacher." The cure is not more craft. It's restraint: pull your punches, write plain and confident, let the reader listen *passively* — and then, when you have something genuinely strong, hit them once, hard, where they'll go *whoa.* This workflow finds the punches you're throwing nonstop, consolidates the description-piles down to the single strongest image, and re-paces the prose so the surrounding quiet makes the one real hit detonate. It does not add anything. It is subtraction, the way Orlean edits: read it aloud, feel where it's exhausting, and cut until the meat hits.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; restraint is the genre's most-violated principle and this pass is dangerous *because* every instinct to "make it better" produces exactly the overwritten Substack piece Orlean closes after a hundred words):

- **Pattern 9 — Pull Your Punches, Then Hit** — the core rhythm: default to plain, confident, unflashy prose; let the reader listen *passively*; reserve force for the moment that earns it; *passive comfort → sudden strong hit → comfort again*. "It's hard to do that if you're like boom boom boom boom."
- **Pattern 10 — The Pick-One Principle** — one clean, strong, picture-able image hit hard beats five mushy ones. "Each description you use should be a real punch." Too many "get sort of mushy" and "cancel it all out." One vivid example beats six.
- **Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-1 (Overwriting to Impress — the ninth-grade-teacher tell, "look at me" prose, the closed-after-100-words Substack piece)** and **AN-2 (Adjective / Example Stacking — flooding cancels it all out; the Laroche exception: a run is allowed only when each item is "clean and mean" and names one distinct quality)**.
- **Decision Framework** — the gate below (especially Q7: *am I pulling my punches, or trying to impress?*).
- **Expert-Specific Quality Rubric** — the row **Restraint & Pick-One** (Score 4 = overwritten / stacked-until-mushy; Score 10 = plain confident prose, one clean image hit hard, passive/hit/passive rhythm so the punch lands *because* of the surrounding quiet).

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before touching a single line. Specifically confirm **Q7** (the prose is reaching to be *admired* — flowery, "look at me," boom-boom-boom — rather than plain and confident with reserved hits) and that **the underlying story is sound** (a *telling subject*, Q2; *reader-as-proxy* stance, Q4; *lift into story*, not bullet points, AN-3). This workflow fixes *overwriting*, not *underbuilding*. If the draft is exhausting because the prose is lush, run it. If the draft is flat because there's no telling subject or no real reporting beneath it, **stop** — restraint will only make a thin piece quieter. Route to `orlean-yarn-engine` (lift the facts into a story) or back to the reporting first; an empty plate served à la carte is still empty.

## Input Required

- **The draft** — the actual prose, whole, not a summary. We are cutting specific sentences, so we need the real sentences. (A paragraph, a post, a section, a chapter — any length; restraint scales.)
- **The format** — social post / marketing / converting copy / ghostwriting / long-form essay or book chapter. Drives how much quiet runway exists between hits and how many punches the piece can bear before it's boom-boom-boom.
- **The one thing that must land** (the keeper punch) — if the reader remembers a single image, line, or beat from this draft, what should it be? This is the punch we *protect* and clear the runway for. (If you can't name it, that's diagnostic: a piece with no single thing to land is a piece swinging at everything.)
- **Felt verdict from reading it aloud** (optional but ideal) — where did *you* get bored, exhausted, or embarrassed reading it out loud? Orlean's foolproof test: boredom-while-reading = a boring passage; the cringe of "I can feel myself trying" = AN-1 firing. Mark those spots.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Read it aloud and mark every place it's reaching (the AN-1 sweep)

Before cutting anything, do what Orlean does: read the whole draft *aloud, cold.* You are listening for two distinct failures, and you mark them differently because they get fixed differently.

| What you hear | The failure | The tell | Mark it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A sentence reaching to be *admired* — flowery, ornate, "look at me," you can feel the writer performing | **AN-1 — Overwriting to impress** | The "ninth-grade English teacher" cringe; you'd "read a hundred words and close it"; can't say a simple sentence | `[IMPRESS]` |
| A noun wearing three-plus adjectives, or a point made with five examples | **AN-2 — Stacking** | The images "get mushy" and "cancel it all out"; you lose track of which one mattered | `[PILE]` |
| Everything at full intensity, hit after hit after hit, no quiet | **Pattern 9 violation — boom-boom-boom-boom** | You're exhausted reading it; no single moment stands out because they all do | `[BOOM]` |
| You get *bored* reading aloud | A genuinely boring passage (Orlean's foolproof signal) | Your attention drifts mid-sentence | `[BORE]` |

The single most reliable diagnostic is your own embarrassment. Orlean: "Stop trying to impress your ninth-grade English teacher. I can feel you trying to do that." If, reading aloud, you feel yourself *trying* — trying to sound literary, trying to be deep, trying to be admired — that sentence is `[IMPRESS]` and it is almost always cuttable to something plainer and better. Do not fix yet. Just mark. The map comes before the surgery.

### Step 2 — Cut the [IMPRESS] lines to plain, confident prose (the steakhouse pass)

Take every `[IMPRESS]` mark and rewrite it plain. The instinct to resist: *plain feels like giving up.* It isn't — "the confidence of plainer, subtler writing is something you have to grow into." Plain prose is not weaker; it is the steakhouse that trusts the steak. The ornate version is the insecure one.

The translation move, line by line:

| The reaching version (AN-1) | The plain, confident version | Why plain wins |
|---|---|---|
| "the gossamer light fell, achingly luminous, upon the ancient weathered cobblestones" | "the light came down on the old stones" | The reach was decoration; the meaning survives plainer, and now the reader trusts you |
| "I found myself irrevocably, helplessly transfixed by the sheer ineffable wonder of it" | "I couldn't stop looking" | One true human action beats four words insisting you felt something |
| "a symphony of color exploded across the canvas of the evening sky" | "the sky went orange" | A cliché dressed as poetry; the plain image is more pictureable than the dressed one |

The test for each rewrite, read aloud: do you still feel the writer *trying*? If yes, you didn't cut deep enough — cut again. The goal is a sentence a reader would never *notice* as a sentence, only as the thing it says. Confidence is the absence of reaching.

> **Keep the verb, kill the costume.** Most `[IMPRESS]` damage is in adjectives and adverbs, not verbs and nouns. A strong, plain verb ("the sky went orange") usually survives the cut intact; the costume around it ("a symphony of color exploded across the canvas of") is what goes. When in doubt, keep the noun and the verb, strip everything draped on them, and read it again.

### Step 3 — Consolidate every [PILE] to one clean punch (the Pick-One pass)

For every `[PILE]` — a noun with a stack of adjectives, or a point propped up by a row of examples — you are going to throw away the good ones to make the best one land. This is the hardest cut emotionally and the highest-leverage one. "If you use too many and they get sort of mushy, it's just not as potent." Flooding "has the effect of canceling it all out."

The Pick-One procedure:

1. **List the candidates.** Write out all the adjectives (or all the examples) the pile is using. Five adjectives, six examples — whatever's there.
2. **Score each on "punch."** A real punch is *specific, picture-able, and surprising* — the reader sees it and feels something. A weak one is generic, abstract, or expected ("beautiful," "amazing," "important"). Keep only the score-leaders.
3. **Pick the single strongest.** Not the safest — the strongest. One clean, mean, picture-able image. Cut the rest entirely; do not relocate them elsewhere (that just moves the flood).
4. **Hit it hard.** Now that the one image stands alone, you can give it room and weight — a full clean sentence, the best position in the paragraph. The power you stripped from the four you cut gets concentrated into the one you kept.

| Before (PILE) | After (Pick-One) |
|---|---|
| "He was a tall, lanky, gaunt, wiry, thin man" | "He was skinny as a stick." |
| "The library held knowledge, history, culture, memory, civilization itself" | "The library held memory." |
| "We've helped doctors, lawyers, founders, coaches, consultants, and dozens of others scale" *(claim-stack)* | "We helped one overwhelmed cardiologist go from 60-hour weeks to 40." *(one vivid case)* |

> **The Laroche exception (when a run is allowed).** Orlean's own famous lead stacks adjectives — "tall, skinny as a stick, pale-eyed, slouch-shouldered, and sharply handsome in spite of the fact that he is missing all his front teeth." That run survives *only* because each adjective is "clean and mean" and names *one distinct quality* — and because the run resolves into a single jarring punch (handsome *and* toothless). A run is a pile when the words restate the same quality in slightly different clothes (*tall, lanky, gaunt, wiry, thin* — all "thin"). If every item in your run names a genuinely different, specific, picture-able thing and the run *lands* on a punch, keep it. If two items mean roughly the same thing, you have a pile. Cut to one per quality.

### Step 4 — Re-pace into the passive/hit/passive rhythm (the [BOOM] fix)

Now address the marks where it's all intensity — `[BOOM]`. After Steps 2–3 the volume has already dropped, but a draft can be free of purple prose and *still* be exhausting because every beat is a strong beat. The fix is not more cutting; it's *pacing*. Orlean lets the reader "listen passively" through plain, comfortable prose, then "really hit them with something very strong where they're going to go, whoa" — then settles back to quiet. The rhythm is the point: **passive comfort → strong hit → comfort again → strong hit.** "It's hard to do that if you're like boom boom boom boom."

Map the draft's intensity beat by beat, then sculpt it:

| Beat type | What it does | How much of the draft | Voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Passive comfort** | Lets the reader settle, breathe, trust you, listen without bracing | The *majority* — the connective tissue, the setup, the quiet observation | Plain, confident, unhurried |
| **The hit** | The *whoa* — the one strong image, the turn, the line they'll quote | Rare, deliberate, *earned* by the quiet around it | Full force, but still clean (a hit is not the same as ornate) |
| **Comfort again** | Lets the hit echo; gives the reader room to feel it before the next | Right after every hit | Quiet again — do not crowd the punch with another punch |

To re-pace a `[BOOM]` stretch: identify which beats are the *real* hits (often you can keep only one or two per section — protect the **keeper punch** from Input above). Demote the rest. A demoted hit becomes passive comfort: state it plainly, let it be setup rather than climax. The reader needs the quiet to register the loud. Remove the quiet and the punches stop working — not because they got weaker, but because the ear stops hearing the difference.

> **The contrast law.** A punch lands by *contrast with its surroundings,* not by its own volume. The exact same sentence that detonates inside plain prose disappears inside loud prose. This is why "adding emphasis everywhere" is self-defeating: emphasis is a *difference*, and if everything is emphasized, nothing is. The most powerful thing you can do for your best line is make the three lines before it quieter.

### Step 5 — Read the whole thing aloud again; protect the keeper, confirm the rhythm

Now read the rewritten draft aloud, cold, start to finish, as a first-time reader. This is the step that separates a tuned piece from a piece that's merely been trimmed.

- **The boredom test (Orlean's foolproof signal):** anywhere you get bored reading aloud is still a boring passage. Mark it `[BORE]` and either cut it or find the one live thing in it and lead on that. Boredom in the *passive* stretches is fine if it's brief breathing room; boredom that drifts is a dead patch — cut it.
- **The keeper-punch test:** does the one thing you named in Input land? Is there genuine quiet around it? Read the three sentences before your keeper — if any of them is *also* a hit, demote it; the keeper needs the runway.
- **The "am I still trying" test (AN-1 re-check):** any sentence where you still feel yourself reaching to be admired survived Step 2 and needs another cut. The reader should feel the *story*, never the writer performing it. "A story that announces its own craft is exhausting as a reader."
- **The rhythm test:** can you *hear* the passive/hit/passive pattern — settle, whoa, settle, whoa — or is it still flat-out (boom-boom-boom) or now flat-down (all quiet, no hits)? Restraint that removes *all* the punches is a different failure; you want fewer, harder hits, not none. If you cut so much there's no *whoa* left, restore the single best one and give it full force.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social media** (LinkedIn / X / Substack note) | The most violation-prone format — every line fighting to be the hook, so the whole post is boom-boom-boom and nothing lands. **One** vivid image per post; the rest plain. The opening can be a hit (the wait-what), but then *settle* — let the body be plain confident prose so the closing line detonates. Resist the urge to make every line quotable; a post of all-quotables is a post of none. Pick-One is brutal here: one example, one image, one punch. |
| **Marketing** (brand story / about page / launch post) | The pile shows up as the *benefit-stack* and the *adjective-laden mission statement* ("innovative, world-class, cutting-edge, transformative"). Cut the adjective pile to one true claim; cut the six-customer name-drop to one vivid case told as a yarn. Plain, confident brand prose reads as a company secure in itself; the over-decorated version reads as one that doesn't trust its own offer. Reserve the one hit for the genuinely remarkable thing. |
| **Copywriting** (VSL / landing / email) | Honesty spine first: this pass tunes the *prose*, never invents the proof. The classic `[BOOM]` failure is the all-caps, all-bold, every-line-a-promise page — emphasis everywhere, so the reader's emphasis-detector burns out before the CTA. Make the proof and setup *plain* so the one big claim and the close land hard. Pick-One the proof: one unforgettable case beats six logos going mushy. The contrast law *is* conversion here — quiet copy makes the offer line ring. |
| **Ghostwriting** | The client's real voice is almost always *plainer* than the draft "trying to sound like a thought leader." Read the client's actual speech aloud; people don't talk in adjective-piles or boom-boom-boom. Cut the borrowed literary reach back to how the client genuinely says it — plain, confident, one strong image. The `[IMPRESS]` sweep is where ghostwritten slop hides: it's the writer performing literariness on the client's behalf, and audiences smell it. |
| **Long-form content / essay / book chapter** | The format with the *most* room for the passive/hit/passive rhythm — and the most to lose to relentless intensity across a long span (exhausting by page three). Build long quiet stretches deliberately; place hits at section turns and let them echo. Read whole sections aloud; the boredom test is the real editor here. This is Orlean's native habitat — the steakhouse plate held across 80,000 words. |

## Output Format

Deliver exactly this:

```
DRAFT: __________ (format)
THE KEEPER PUNCH (the one thing that must land): __________
HONESTY SPINE (copy/marketing only): every kept claim is still true/substantiated [confirmed / N/A]

DIAGNOSTIC MAP (what the read-aloud sweep found):
  [IMPRESS] reaching-to-be-admired lines: __ found
  [PILE]    adjective/example stacks:     __ found
  [BOOM]    all-intensity / no-quiet stretches: __ found
  [BORE]    dead passages:               __ found

— REWRITES —

1 · IMPRESS → PLAIN (the steakhouse pass)
  BEFORE: "[the reaching line]"
  AFTER:  "[the plain, confident line]"
  Why: the reach was decoration; the meaning survives plainer, and now the reader trusts you.
  [repeat per [IMPRESS]]

2 · PILE → PICK-ONE (consolidation)
  CANDIDATES: [all the adjectives/examples that were stacked]
  KEPT (the one clean punch): "[the single strongest]"
  AFTER:  "[the line, with the one image hit hard, the rest cut]"
  Why: flooding cancels it all out; one picture-able image carries the weight.
  [repeat per [PILE]]

3 · BOOM → PASSIVE/HIT/PASSIVE (re-pacing)
  THE HITS KEPT: [1–2 per section — including the keeper]
  THE HITS DEMOTED TO COMFORT: [the rest, restated plainly as setup]
  Why: the punch lands by contrast; the quiet around it is what makes it detonate.

REWRITTEN DRAFT (clean, read-aloud-passed):
  [the full tuned draft]

READ-ALOUD CONFIRM: no [BORE] drift · keeper lands with quiet around it · no "still trying" lines · the rhythm is audible [confirmed]
```

### Worked example A — Substack opening (the overwritten-essay rescue)

**Format:** Substack essay. · **Keeper punch:** the boy didn't cry — that's the line that should land.

**BEFORE (the draft, as it came in — AN-1 and AN-2 firing on every line):**
> "The gossamer morning light fell, achingly luminous and sorrowful, upon the ancient, weathered, melancholy cobblestones of the silent street, as if the very heavens themselves wept tears of liquid amber. And there, in that profound and devastating stillness, stood a small, fragile, trembling boy, his enormous luminous eyes brimming with an ocean of unspeakable, world-ending grief, and yet — miraculously, impossibly, heartbreakingly — he did not cry."

*Read aloud, this is exhausting by the second clause. Every noun has three adjectives; every beat is at full volume; you can feel the writer reaching for the ninth-grade teacher. Orlean would close it after a hundred words.*

**AFTER (the tuned version):**
> "It had rained in the night, and the cobblestones were still wet. A boy stood alone in the empty street. He looked about six. He did not cry."

*Why it works:* The `[IMPRESS]` reach ("gossamer," "achingly luminous and sorrowful," "the very heavens themselves wept tears of liquid amber") is gone — cut to plain, confident observation a reader never *notices* as writing. The `[PILE]` ("ancient, weathered, melancholy"; "small, fragile, trembling"; "unspeakable, world-ending grief") is consolidated to nothing-extra: just "a boy," "alone," "empty." Three flat, quiet, passive sentences set the runway. Then the keeper — *He did not cry* — lands as the only hit on the plate, and it detonates *because* everything before it was quiet. The reader feels the grief more in four plain words than in the original's ocean of adjectives, because the flood had canceled itself out. Passive, passive, passive, *whoa.*

### Worked example B — Landing-page proof block (the boom-boom-boom copy fix)

**Format:** landing page. · **Keeper punch:** the one client result. · **Honesty spine:** the cardiologist case is real and substantiated [confirmed].

**BEFORE (every line a promise — [BOOM], and a [PILE] of logos):**
> "TRANSFORM your practice with our REVOLUTIONARY, game-changing, world-class system! We've helped doctors, lawyers, founders, coaches, consultants, dentists, and HUNDREDS of elite professionals SKYROCKET their results and DOMINATE their markets! Don't wait — your BREAKTHROUGH is ONE CLICK AWAY!"

*Read aloud, it's boom-boom-boom-boom — all caps, all promise, no quiet — so the reader's emphasis-detector burns out and nothing lands. The customer list is a pile that cancels itself out.*

**AFTER (plain runway, one hit):**
> "Most of the doctors we work with are drowning in hours they can't bill. One cardiologist came to us at sixty-hour weeks, missing his kids' dinners, sure there was no way down. We built him a system. He's at forty now, home by six. That's the whole offer: your time back. [Build it]"

*Why it works:* The `[PILE]` of six professions and "hundreds of elite professionals" is cut to *one* vivid, specific, picture-able case (Pick-One) — a real cardiologist, real numbers, real dinner table. The `[BOOM]` of caps-lock promises is dropped to plain, confident prose that lets the reader listen passively. The proof is *told as a small yarn* (sixty hours → home by six) instead of asserted. Then the one hit — *your time back* — lands clean, and the Saxon close (*Build it*) detonates because the page got quiet first. The contrast law doing the conversion: quiet copy makes the offer line ring.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (row: **Restraint & Pick-One**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **No reaching left (AN-1):** read the whole thing aloud — is there a single sentence where you feel the writer *trying to be admired*? The "ninth-grade English teacher" cringe? If yes, it survived Step 2; cut it plainer. The reader should feel the story, never the writer performing.
- **No piles left (AN-2):** every noun wears at most the adjectives it earns; every point stands on its one strongest example. Any remaining stack must pass the Laroche exception — each item "clean and mean," naming a *distinct* quality, resolving into a punch. Two items meaning the same thing = a pile; cut to one.
- **The rhythm is audible (Pattern 9):** you can *hear* passive comfort → hit → comfort, not boom-boom-boom-boom and not flat-all-quiet. The majority of the draft is plain; the hits are rare, deliberate, and earned by the surrounding quiet.
- **The keeper lands (Pattern 9 + Pattern 10):** the one thing that had to land has genuine quiet around it and detonates. No competing hit crowds it in the three lines before. One image carries the weight; adding a second would weaken it.
- **Plain ≠ flat:** restraint removed the *over*writing, not the life. There is still at least one real *whoa.* If the cut left no punches at all, you over-corrected — restore the single best hit at full force. Fewer, harder. Not none.
- **The boredom test (Pattern 11):** read aloud start to finish — no passage where your own attention drifts. Boredom while reading = a boring passage. Cut it or find its one live thing.
- **Honesty spine (copy/marketing):** every kept claim is still true and substantiated. Pick-One *consolidated* the proof to the strongest real case; it never invented one or inflated it. Restraint tunes the ear; it never manufactures the belief.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Over-correcting into flat, lifeless prose (the restraint trap).** Reading "pull your punches" as "remove all the punches," cutting until the piece is competent, plain, and *dead* — no *whoa* anywhere. Orlean pulls punches *so that the real one lands*, not to eliminate impact. **Recovery:** Step 4 + Step 5 — name the keeper punch and protect it; restore the single best hit at full force; the goal is fewer, harder hits with quiet between them, never a uniform murmur. Restraint is contrast, not silence.
- **Relocating the pile instead of cutting it (AN-2, disguised).** Doing Pick-One but moving the four cut adjectives or five cut examples *elsewhere* in the draft to "not waste them" — so the flood just spreads out instead of going away. The cancellation effect still fires; you've only thinned it. **Recovery:** the cut adjectives and examples are *gone*, not relocated. The power you want is in concentration, not preservation. Throw the good ones away to make the best one land.
- **Mistaking an underbuilt draft for an overwritten one.** Running restraint on a piece that's flat because it has no telling subject, no reporting beneath it, or reads as bullet-point information (AN-3) — quieting it only makes a thin piece thinner and quieter. **Recovery:** the Pre-Flight Gate catches this. If the problem is *no story* (not *too much prose*), stop and route to `orlean-yarn-engine` to lift the facts into a story, or back to reporting (the iceberg ratio). Restraint sharpens a real plate; it can't fill an empty one.
- **Keeping a [IMPRESS] line because it's "your favorite sentence."** The most ornate, most-admired-by-its-author line is usually the most AN-1 — the one most clearly written to impress. Sentimental attachment to your own cleverness is the single hardest thing to cut. **Recovery:** apply the "am I still trying" test honestly — read it aloud and ask whether you love it because it *serves the reader* or because it shows *you* off. If it's the second, it's a "darling," and the steakhouse cuts it. The story you're telling is more important than the sentence you're proud of.
