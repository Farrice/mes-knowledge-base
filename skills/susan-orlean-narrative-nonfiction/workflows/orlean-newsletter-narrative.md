# The Literary Newsletter — A Substack Essay That Reads Like Fiction (Susan Orlean)

Most newsletter essays open the way a brochure opens: with the thing you're supposed to find interesting, announced. *Here's why X matters. Here are three things I learned about Y.* And the reader, who didn't ask, scrolls. Orlean spends her whole career on the harder problem — making a stranger care about something they had no reason to care about — and she solves it the same way every time: she finds a small overlooked subject (a dog, an orchid, a building), she opens on one true detail jarring enough to make you stop, she stands *beside* you as the person who also didn't think they needed this, and she tells it plainly enough that when the real moment comes, it lands. This workflow runs that method end to end for a newsletter: it picks the telling subject, writes the wait-what lead, sets the proxy stance, drafts in restrained prose with the punches pulled, and finishes with the read-aloud cut. It produces a small overlooked subject *opened into a yarn* — a piece a reader who "maybe didn't think they'd have any particular interest" stays at the table for. And it is engineered against the one failure Orlean names by hand: the overwritten essay you close after a hundred words because you can feel the writer trying.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the literary-newsletter genre is the one most easily ruined by mechanical maximalism — every instinct to "add depth," "make it lyrical," "layer detail" produces exactly the piece Orlean closes after a hundred words):

- **Pattern 1 — The Telling Subject** — the small thing that carries the large theme. Reject the subject everyone already cares about; pick the overlooked specific, then name the universal it secretly carries. The subject is the *door*; the theme is the *house*. (Reader's first reaction: "why would anyone write about *that*?" — by the end they can't believe how much was in there.)
- **Pattern 4 — The "Wait, What" Lead** — for a subject the reader doesn't think they need, the lead does *all* the work. A juxtaposition jarring enough to stop a "sidewalk pigeon" attention span — usually a *person* rendered through one true, specific, picture-able contradiction (Laroche: handsome *and* missing all his front teeth). The reader's involuntary "wait, what" means "wait, I'll read a little more."
- **Pattern 3 — The Reader's Proxy** — sympathy, not superiority. Never "you need to know this." Always "I didn't think I needed this either, and then —." You started where the reader is, got fascinated, and are now *just a step ahead*, turning back to say *keep coming.* That shared discovery is the whole seduction.
- **Pattern 9 — Pull Your Punches, Then Hit** — default to plain, confident, unflashy prose; let the reader listen *passively*; reserve force for the one moment that earns it. *Passive comfort → strong hit → comfort again.* "It's hard to do that if you're like boom boom boom boom." (Plus Pattern 10 — Pick-One: one clean image hit hard beats five mushy ones.)
- **Pattern 11 — Be Brutal: Read Aloud, Then Cut** — the editing love. Read every sentence aloud; boredom-while-reading = a boring passage; hunt the "would," the extra adjective, the clever-for-clever's-sake bit. Each pass turns the dial to a higher level of polish.

Also load the **Anti-Patterns** — especially **AN-1 (Overwriting to Impress — the ninth-grade-teacher tell, the closed-after-100-words Substack piece)**, **AN-2 (Adjective/Example Stacking — flooding cancels it all out)**, **AN-3 (Bullet-Point Information — the snoozefest)**, **AN-6 (Forcing a Subject You Don't Love — the inauthenticity stench)** — and the **Expert-Specific Quality Rubric** (every row applies; this workflow is graded on all seven).

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before writing a single line. Specifically confirm **Q1** (am I — or the client whose voice this is — genuinely, burningly curious about this subject? no love → the inauthenticity stench, and the newsletter is the format where it's smelled fastest), **Q2** (is this a *telling subject* — a small overlooked thing opening onto a universal — and not the obvious take everyone's already publishing?), **Q4** (am I the reader's *proxy* — "I didn't think I needed this either" — or am I lecturing from above?), and **Q7** (is the prose plain and confident with reserved hits, or reaching to be admired?). **Honesty spine, non-negotiable for this workflow:** the wait-what detail, the curiosity, and every fact in the yarn must be *true* — Laroche really was missing his teeth. The craft makes a *true* small subject seduce harder; it never manufactures intrigue or fakes an enthusiasm you don't have. If the honest answer to Q1 is "no, but it'd do numbers," **stop** — change the subject before any craft can save it.

## Input Required

- **The raw subject (or the territory)** — the thing you want to write about, or the rough area you're hunting in. Can be a specific small thing already ("the last payphone in town") or a vague pull ("I keep thinking about libraries"). Step 1 sharpens it into a telling subject.
- **The newsletter / publication** — which Substack or newsletter this is for, and its established voice (your own, or a client's in ghostwriting). Drives length, register, and how much the audience already trusts you.
- **What you actually know / have reported** — the real material on hand: facts, an interview, a memory, a thing you noticed, a number. (Honesty spine — the yarn is built only from true material. If the well is dry, this workflow routes you back to reporting, not to invention.)
- **The genuine curiosity** (the love test) — in one honest sentence: *why can't you stop thinking about this?* If you can't answer, that's diagnostic — the inauthenticity will be smelled. Fix the subject, not the prose.
- **The vibrating theme** (often found late, that's fine) — the quiet universal the small subject opens onto (memory · permanence · belonging · what we keep · who gets remembered). You don't state it in the essay; you let it *vibrate.* Naming it here tells you what the yarn is secretly about.

## Workflow

The order is load-bearing. Orlean's method dies when blended: you do not draft to discover the subject, and you do not open the prose until the subject, lead, stance, and theme are decided. **Find the subject → write the lead → set the stance → draft restrained → read aloud and cut.** Five steps, hard walls.

### Step 1 — Find the telling subject (the small thing, the large theme)

A literary newsletter does not win on the subject everyone's already posting about. It wins on the overlooked specific — the gap between "why would anyone write about *that*?" and "I can't believe how much was in there" *is the engine.* Before you write anything, pressure-test what you've got against Orlean's selection logic.

| Test | The question | Pass | Fail (and the fix) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overlooked** | Is the spotlight already on this? | A small specific thing no one's clamoring to read about (a payphone, a stray cat colony, a typeface) | The obvious trending take → go smaller, weirder, more specific |
| **In love** (Q1) | Can I say in one honest sentence why I can't stop thinking about it? | A real burning — you'd tell a friend about it unprompted | Polite interest → the stench will be smelled; change the subject |
| **The prism** (Pattern 2) | Can I turn it 3–4 directions and have them all be *one* story? | A dog → Hollywood + a breed's arc + WWI's use of animals, all the same yarn | One flat angle → it's a list, not a telling subject; dig for the layers |
| **The vibrating theme** | What universal does this small thing secretly carry? | A library fire → *memory made permanent against the certainty of death* | No undertone → you have a fun fact, not an essay; find what it's *about* |

The output of Step 1 is one line you can say out loud: **"It looks like a piece about [the small specific thing], but it's really about [the universal]."** (*"It looks like a piece about the last payphone on Main Street, but it's really about who we still leave a way to reach."*) If you can't fill both halves, you don't have a telling subject yet. Do not proceed — the lead, stance, and yarn all hang off this line.

> **The newsletter-specific telling subject.** A Substack essay is shorter than Orlean's books, so the prism can be shallower (3 turns, not 6) — but the *gap* and the *vibrating theme* are non-negotiable. The thing that separates a literary newsletter from a blog post is not the prose; it's that the small subject opens onto something larger and the writer never says so out loud.

### Step 2 — Write the wait-what lead (the hook that seduces the uninterested)

The reader did not ask to read about a payphone. So the first line has to do *all* the work — not by announcing importance ("payphones tell us something about modern life") but by triggering the involuntary *wait, what.* Orlean's move: lead on a **person** (people are very interested in people) rendered through **one true, specific, picture-able contradiction.** "John Laroche is a tall guy, skinny as a stick… and sharply handsome in spite of the fact that he is missing all his front teeth." Handsome *and* toothless = *wait, what* = *I'll read a little more.*

Build it in this order:

| Move | What you do | Guard |
|---|---|---|
| **Find the person** | Who is the human at the center of, or beside, the small subject? Lead on them, not on the concept. | If the subject has no person, find the one closest to it (the man who repairs the payphone, the woman who still calls it) |
| **Find the true contradiction** | The one real, specific, jarring juxtaposition about them or it — the detail that doesn't fit. | **Honesty spine:** it must be *true.* Don't manufacture a contradiction for shock; mine the real one. |
| **Make it picture-able** | One concrete image, not an abstraction. "Missing all his front teeth," not "unconventional." | If you can't see it, the reader can't either. Cut to the thing you can point at. |
| **Stop before you explain** | The lead creates productive confusion and *leaves it open* a beat. Don't resolve it in the same sentence. | The "wait, what" *is* the engagement; explaining it immediately spends it. |

The success test, read aloud: does a first-time reader's involuntary reaction land on **"wait, what?"** — meaning "wait, I'm going to read a little more"? If the lead instead lands on "okay, I see where this is going," it's an announcement, not a seduction. Rewrite. "The very importance of a lead has never been greater" in a feed full of competing attention — and in a newsletter, the lead is also the email-preview line, so it's doing double duty.

> **What a wait-what lead is NOT.** It is not clickbait (clickbait promises and the body fails to pay off — the dishonesty Orlean's whole stance is built against). It is not a rhetorical question ("Have you ever wondered…?"). It is not the banned "Here's what I learned about…" opener. It is one *true, specific, picture-able contradiction* that makes the reader stop because something genuinely doesn't compute yet — and the essay then *honestly* pays it off.

### Step 3 — Set the reader-as-proxy stance (sympathy, not superiority)

Now decide *who is talking.* The newsletter essay lives or dies on stance. The failure mode is the expert-from-above ("let me tell you why this matters"); the seduction is the proxy beside you ("I didn't think I needed to know this either, and then —"). Orlean: "I'm really in the same position as the reader. I'm just a little ahead of them. So I can turn back and say, oh my god, keep coming." The reader trusts you *because* you admit you started skeptical — you held their not-caring, so you're not condescending to it.

Set the stance explicitly before drafting:

| Stance lever | The proxy version (do this) | The superior version (avoid this) |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening posture** | "I have never cared about payphones. I want to be clear about that." | "Payphones are a fascinating window into society." |
| **The turn** | "And then I noticed one was still ringing." (shared discovery) | "What most people don't realize is…" (you knew, they didn't) |
| **Authority** | Earned by *what you found*, shown not claimed | Asserted up front ("As someone who's studied this…") |
| **The reader's right to not-yet-care** | Honored — you were there too | Overridden — they're told they should care |

Pick the **honest entry point**: where were *you* before this subject hooked you? Skeptical? Indifferent? Mildly annoyed ("I don't even like Florida")? Start there, out loud, in the reader's exact position — then let the fascination happen *on the page,* so the reader feels accompanied into it rather than lectured about it. The whole emotional arc of a literary newsletter is *we didn't care → we got pulled in together → look what was here.*

> **Ghostwriting note (honesty spine).** When the voice is a client's, the proxy stance is "I was wrong about this too" / "I learned this the hard way" — and it must be a thing the client *actually* didn't know or *actually* came around on. Audiences smell borrowed enthusiasm. Build the proxy arc only on a genuine before/after the client really lived.

### Step 4 — Draft the yarn in restrained prose (lift the facts; pull the punches)

Now, and only now, write. The job of the draft is to take your true material and *lift* it into a story — "let me tell you this amazing story, sit back, I'm going to spin a mood, engage you in the characters, and very importantly but subtly explain why this matters" — never to read the reader bullet points of information (AN-3, the snoozefest). And the prose register is **plain and confident with reserved hits**, not lush. This is the step where the overwritten-Substack failure mode lurks, so it has its own discipline.

**4a — Lift, don't list.** Take your reported material and turn it as a yarn across a dinner table: foreground the *people*, spin a *mood*, and convey *why it matters* by showing, never by stating a thesis. The test: could a reader get the same value from a fact sheet? If yes, you've listed, not lifted — go back and find the human and the scene inside the facts.

**4b — Pull your punches, then hit (the rhythm).** Default to plain, unflashy, confident prose — the steakhouse that serves the steak nearly à la carte. Let the reader listen *passively* through the quiet stretches, then, when you have something genuinely strong, hit them once where they go *whoa* — then settle back to quiet. The pattern is **passive comfort → strong hit → comfort again,** never boom-boom-boom. A punch lands by *contrast* with the quiet around it; remove the quiet and the punch stops working.

**4c — Pick-One (consolidate every pile).** For any description, choose the *single* strongest, most picture-able image and hit it hard — then stop. Too many adjectives "get mushy" and "cancel it all out." One vivid example beats six. This is the move that most separates a literary newsletter from an overwritten one.

**4d — Watch the newsletter-specific overwriting tells.** The Substack format *invites* AN-1, because the writer is performing literariness to justify the "literary newsletter" label. As you draft, refuse these on sight:

| Overwriting tell (AN-1 / AN-2) | Plain, confident replacement |
|---|---|
| "the gossamer light fell, achingly luminous, upon the ancient weathered cobblestones" | "the light came down on the old stones" |
| "I found myself irrevocably, helplessly transfixed" | "I couldn't stop looking" |
| stacked adjectives ("small, fragile, trembling boy") | one clean image ("a boy, alone in the street") |
| the habitual "would" ("I would walk there every evening") | direct past ("I walked there every evening") |
| a thesis statement of the theme ("this teaches us that memory is fragile") | let the theme *vibrate* — show the burned book, don't explain it |

Draft to a finite goal, not the whole intimidating essay at once — Orlean's quota move: "a thousand words of the story today." Get the yarn down. The cutting comes next; do not edit while drafting (the walls hold).

> **The vibrating-theme rule.** The universal you named in Step 1 ("who we still leave a way to reach") must *never* be stated as a thesis. Orlean's *Library Book* is about memory and permanence — but the book never lectures you about memory; it *vibrates* with it through the telling. State the theme and you've written a blog post with a moral. Let it vibrate and you've written literature. If you catch yourself explaining what the essay "is really about," cut that sentence — the whole essay is supposed to be that sentence, unspoken.

### Step 5 — Read it aloud and cut (be brutal, then ship)

The draft is not the deliverable. The edit is. Orlean *loves* this part — "I love catching stuff" — and her one foolproof trick is to read every sentence aloud. Run the whole essay aloud, cold, as a first-time subscriber, and cut until it's clean. Each pass turns the dial to a higher level of polish.

| Pass | What you listen for | The cut |
|---|---|---|
| **The boredom pass** | Anywhere your own attention drifts while reading aloud | Orlean's foolproof signal: bored reading it = boring to read. Cut the dead patch or find its one live thing and lead on that. |
| **The "still trying" pass (AN-1)** | Any sentence where you feel yourself reaching to be admired | The ninth-grade-teacher cringe. Cut it plainer. The reader should feel the story, never the writer performing. |
| **The "would" / crutch pass (AN-5)** | Habitual "would," passive constructions, padding, repetition | "I would go" → "I went." Repetition the ear catches. Each crutch removed makes a stronger sentence. |
| **The pile pass (AN-2)** | Any noun wearing 3+ adjectives, any point on 5 examples | Consolidate to the one clean image (Pick-One). The cut ones are *gone,* not relocated. |
| **The rhythm pass (Pattern 9)** | Is the lead's wait-what paid off? Does the one keeper hit land with quiet around it? | If it's boom-boom-boom, demote hits to comfort. If it's flat-all-quiet, restore the single best hit at full force. |

Two newsletter-specific final checks before you ship:

- **The lead-as-preview check.** Reread the first line as it'll appear in the email subject/preview. Does it still trigger wait-what *out of context*, stripped of the essay around it? If it only works once you're already reading, sharpen it — in a newsletter the lead is also the open-rate.
- **The honest-payoff check.** Does the essay *honestly* deliver on the wait-what the lead promised? A lead that intrigues and a body that doesn't pay it off is clickbait — the exact dishonesty the proxy stance is built against. The seduction must be *true* all the way down.

When you can read the whole thing aloud without getting bored or stumbling, with the wait-what paid off, the theme vibrating (never stated), and one clean punch landing in the quiet — it's a publish-ready literary newsletter. Ship it.

| **Social media** (the note / teaser that drives to the essay) | The Substack Note or LinkedIn teaser is *one wait-what line + one true picture-able detail*, written as the proxy ("I never cared about payphones until…"), pointing to the essay. One image only — at note length the machinery shows in a heartbeat. Do NOT summarize the essay; *seduce* into it. The note is the lead, standing alone. |
| **Marketing** (a brand's narrative newsletter / founder letter) | The telling subject is a small overlooked *operational detail or founding moment* that opens, prism-like, onto the company's whole worldview — and you *tell* it as a yarn, not as a mission statement. The vibrating theme is the brand's real reason to exist, let to vibrate, never stated. Honesty spine: the detail is real, the curiosity genuine; a manufactured origin story is the loudest stench there is. |
| **Copywriting** (a story-led sales newsletter / email) | The wait-what lead does all the work for a low-demand offer; the body runs plain and confident so the one proof and the close land hard (contrast = conversion). **Honesty spine hard rule:** this workflow tunes the *telling* of a true claim — it never manufactures the claim. ### Worked example A — A personal literary newsletter (the overlooked-object essay)

**Telling subject:** "It looks like a piece about the last payphone on Main Street, but it's really about who we still leave a way to reach." · **Love test:** *I walked past it for ten years and never once used it, and last week I realized I'd be sad if it were gone, and I don't know why.* · **Vibrating theme:** the people we keep a line open for. · **Honesty spine:** the payphone is real, the man is real, I talked to him [confirmed].

**The wait-what lead:**
> "There is a payphone on Main Street that still works, and exactly one person still calls it. He calls it every Sunday at four, and the man who answers it does not own a phone, has never owned a phone, and would like to keep it that way."

*Why it lands:* It opens on a *person* (people are interested in people), through a true, specific, picture-able contradiction — a working payphone with one caller, a man who answers a public phone because he refuses a private one. The reader's involuntary reaction is *wait, what,* and it's left open a beat. No "payphones tell us something about connection." Just the strange true thing, doing all the work.

**The proxy stance (opening):**
> "I have walked past this payphone for ten years and never once used it. I assumed it was dead, the way you assume about payphones. I want to be clear that I did not set out to care about it. And then, on a Sunday, I heard it ring."

*Why it works:* Pure proxy — "I did not set out to care about it" honors the reader's right to not-yet-care, because the writer held it too. The turn ("And then… I heard it ring") is *shared discovery,* not "what most people don't realize." The reader is now walking *beside* the writer toward the man, not being lectured about civic infrastructure.

**A restrained beat from the body (plain prose → one hit):**
> "His name is Walter. He is eighty-one. He worked forty years for the gas company and retired the day they gave him a cell phone, which he left on the counter and never turned on. Every Sunday his daughter, who lives in Oregon and worries, calls the only number she knows will reach him: the payphone on the corner he can see from his window. He walks down at four. He picks up on the first ring. They talk for exactly the length of a long-distance call he refuses to let her pay for, and then he hangs up and walks home."

*Why it works:* The facts are *lifted into a yarn* — Walter is a person, the mood is spun, and the theme (a line kept open for the one who worries) *vibrates* without a single sentence stating it. The prose is plain and confident — no gossamer light, no adjective-piles. The reader listens passively. And the one quiet hit — *he picks up on the first ring* — lands because everything around it stayed quiet. The essay never says "this is about love and distance and the people we stay reachable for." It doesn't have to. That's the whole essay, unspoken.

### Worked example B — A brand's narrative newsletter (the founder letter, marketing)

**Telling subject:** "It looks like a piece about a broken espresso machine, but it's really about why we refuse to ship anything we wouldn't use ourselves." · **Love test:** *the machine that almost killed the company is the reason it's still alive, and I think about that every time someone asks why we're slow.* · **Vibrating theme:** patience as a form of respect for the customer. · **Honesty spine:** the machine broke, the launch slipped, the numbers are real [confirmed].

**The wait-what lead:**
> "We delayed our launch by four months because of a single espresso machine that none of our customers will ever see, that does not appear in any product, and that, by every reasonable business metric, we should have ignored."

*Why it lands:* A true, specific contradiction — a four-month delay over a machine no customer will ever see — triggers *wait, what,* and resists the obvious "we care about quality" brand-line. The reader leans in because something genuinely doesn't compute yet, and the letter will *honestly* pay it off.

**A restrained beat (proxy stance + plain prose + one hit + vibrating theme):**
> "I'll be honest: when the machine failed the third test, my first instinct was to ship anyway. The customers wanted it. The investors wanted it. I wanted it. Then our head of roasting made us a cup from the batch we were about to sell, and set it down, and said nothing. We tasted it. We didn't ship. The delay cost us a quarter we won't get back. I'd do it again."

*Why it works:* The founder is the *proxy,* not the hero — "my first instinct was to ship anyway" admits the wrong impulse, which is exactly why the reader trusts the right one. The prose is plain; the one hit — *set it down, and said nothing* — lands in the quiet. The theme (patience as respect) *vibrates* through "I'd do it again" without ever being stated as a value-proposition. Honesty spine intact: the delay, the cost, the decision are all real — the yarn makes a *true* commitment seduce; it doesn't manufacture one.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Susan Orlean Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and the full § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (all seven rows — this workflow is graded on every one). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Telling subject, not the obvious take (Pattern 1, AN-6):** the subject is a small overlooked specific that opens onto a real universal — the "why would anyone write about *that*?" gap is the engine. Not the trending take everyone's publishing. And the love test (Q1) is honestly passed: there is a real burning, or the inauthenticity will be smelled.
- **The lead triggers wait-what (Pattern 4):** the opening is one *true, specific, picture-able contradiction* (usually a person) that forces the involuntary "wait, what" — not an announcement of importance, not a rhetorical question, not "Here's what I learned…", not clickbait. And it works *out of context* as the email-preview line.
- **Reader-as-proxy, not superior (Pattern 3):** the essay accompanies the reader from a shared starting skepticism ("I didn't think I needed this either, and then —"), never lectures from above. The reader trusts the writer *because* the not-caring was shared.
- **The facts are lifted, not listed (Pattern 8, AN-3):** the material reads as a yarn — people foregrounded, mood spun, significance shown — not as bullet-point information a fact sheet could replace. If a reader could get the same value from a list, it failed.
- **Restraint, with reserved hits (Pattern 9, Pattern 10, AN-1, AN-2):** plain confident prose with the punches pulled; one clean picture-able image per description (no piles); the passive/hit/passive rhythm audible; no sentence reaching to be admired (no ninth-grade-teacher cringe). The overwritten-Substack tells are all absent.
- **The theme vibrates, never stated:** the universal is felt through the telling, never announced as a thesis or a moral. If a sentence explains what the essay "is really about," it's cut.
- **Read aloud and cut (Pattern 11, AN-5):** the whole essay reads aloud without boredom or stumble; no habitual "would," no padding, no repetition the ear catches. Each pass found something to polish.
- **Honesty spine intact:** the wait-what detail is true, the curiosity is genuine, every fact was reported (not invented), and the lead is *honestly* paid off. For copy/marketing: the yarn tunes the telling of a true claim — it never manufactures one.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Performing literariness to earn the "literary newsletter" label (AN-1).** The Substack format itself tempts the writer to overwrite — gossamer light, achingly luminous, adjective-piles — to *prove* it's literary, which produces exactly the piece Orlean closes after a hundred words. **Recovery:** literary is not lush. The most literary thing on the page is a plain confident sentence a reader never notices as a sentence. Run Step 5's "still trying" pass; cut every reaching line to plain. The steakhouse serves the steak nearly à la carte.
- **Announcing the theme instead of letting it vibrate.** Ending the essay (or worse, opening it) by stating the universal — "and so we see that the things we keep say something about who we are." It collapses a piece of literature into a blog post with a moral. **Recovery:** the vibrating-theme rule (Step 4). Delete the thesis sentence. If you've built it right, the *whole essay* is that sentence, unspoken — the burned book, the ringing payphone, the cup set down in silence carry the meaning so the explanation is redundant. Trust the reader to feel it.
- **Manufacturing the wait-what — clickbait, not seduction (honesty-spine breach).** Inventing or inflating a contradiction the body can't honestly pay off, because a real one wasn't found. The better the false hook, the worse the betrayal when the essay underdelivers. **Recovery:** mine the *true* jarring detail (Step 2) — Laroche really had no teeth; the payphone really has one caller. If the true material has no wait-what in it, that's not a prose problem, it's a *reporting* problem (the iceberg ratio) — go find more real material before you write, never fabricate intrigue. A seduction that lies is the one thing the proxy stance exists to prevent.
- **Drafting to figure out what it's about (AN-4).** Opening the prose before the telling subject, lead, stance, and theme are decided — writing into the fog and hoping the essay reveals itself. It produces structure-less, theme-less, overwritten prose (you compensate for not knowing what it's about with adjectives). **Recovery:** respect the walls. Steps 1–3 are decided *before* a word of the essay is drafted. If you're three paragraphs in and still don't know what it's really about, stop drafting and go back to the Step 1 telling-subject line — you can't pull a punch you haven't aimed.
