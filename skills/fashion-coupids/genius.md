## THE CORE — THE CAMPAIGN CHAIN (tool-transcendent)

Seven links. Reconstructed from the 6450 lesson spine, cross-checked against the two published
campaigns and the final-project specs of the other seven courses. **Nothing in this section names a
model, an app, or a parameter.** Everything that does lives in Appendix A, quarantined and dated.

```
1 INTENT      purpose · audience · emotion · constraints
     ↓
2 CULTURE     trend + cultural read — what moment does this sit in
     ↓
3 BRAND       aesthetic identity + positioning — what is non-negotiably theirs
     ↓
4 CONCEPT     ONE sentence, containing one metaphor          ← the hinge
     ↓
5 BOARD       moodboard + LOOSE storyboard — tone, colour, material, theme
     ↓
6 DIRECTION   art direction & styling → shot list → camera → real-life visualisation
     ↓
7 EXECUTION   generate → composite → UNIFY → channel cut-down
```

The order is load-bearing and reversal is the classic failure. **Concept before board; board before
shot list; shot list before generation; unify after everything.** In both published campaigns,
generation is *step 2 of 4* — roughly a quarter of the work, sandwiched between a research stage and
two finishing stages. [STRUCTURAL]

## GENIUS PATTERNS

### 1. The concept is one sentence, and it contains a metaphor [STATED]
Both published campaigns state their concept as a single declarative sentence with a figure inside it:

> *"a woman running through the city, a metaphor for the fast-paced rhythm of modern life"*
> — Onalaja / Zaza Bag

> blending *"fashion luxury with surrealist storytelling"* — models in dreamlike environments that
> elevate the accessory into an **artistic centerpiece** — Versace / Greca Goddess

**Mechanism.** A campaign concept has to survive being handed to a shot list, a stylist, a colourist
and a caption writer without any of them re-interpreting it. A mood ("elevated, feminine, editorial")
cannot do that — four people will render it four ways. A sentence with a metaphor in it can: *running
through the city* dictates motion, urban environment, dynamic pose and daylight, all at once, and it
does so identically for every downstream stage. Note the second half of the Onalaja concept — *stylish
women stay stylish however busy* — the metaphor comes with **a claim about the buyer**. That is what
makes it a campaign rather than a picture.

**Test:** if you cannot say the concept in one sentence, you do not have one, and the shot list you
are about to write will be a list of nice images with nothing holding them together.

### 2. Research is a stage with an artifact, not a warm-up [STATED]
Both campaigns open identically: research the brand's **aesthetic** (Onalaja: *"bold patterns,
sculptural silhouettes"*; Versace: *"brand identity, color palettes, and aesthetic"*) **and its
positioning**, then produce mood + storyboards. Research terminates in a **board**, never in a
feeling. [STATED]

**Mechanism.** The reason AI campaign work reads generic is that the research stage is skipped and
the model supplies its own average of "luxury." Naming two or three non-negotiable brand
signatures — Onalaja's sculptural silhouette, Versace's Greca — before any generation is what stops
the output being interchangeable with every other brand in the category.

### 3. The storyboard is deliberately **loose** [STATED]
Verbatim: *"a moodboard and **loose** storyboard in Canva that captured the intended tone, colors,
materials, and surrealistic themes."*

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** A tight storyboard in a generative pipeline is a promise you cannot keep —
you will spend the whole budget forcing a frame that the model will hand you for free two variations
later. Loose sets the *tone, palette, material and theme* (all four named) and leaves composition
negotiable, so generation gets to contribute instead of being fought. It is the storyboard equivalent
of St. Pierre's "reference over adjective": constrain the things that must not drift, delegate the
rest deliberately.

### 4. The board tool is cheap on purpose [STRUCTURAL]
Canva and Pinterest, in a body of work whose finishing stage is Photoshop and Lightroom.

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** The board is a **decision artifact**, not a deliverable. Its whole job is
to be approved or killed in one look and then be superseded. Time spent making the board beautiful is
time not spent making the campaign beautiful, and a beautiful board is harder to kill — which is
exactly the wrong property for the stage where killing is cheapest.

### 5. Generation iterates across **named axes**, never freehand [STATED]
Onalaja: *"lighting, urban environments, and dynamic poses."* Versace: *"lighting, poses, styling,
and surreal backgrounds."* The same three axes appear in both, with a fourth swapped in.

**Mechanism.** This is the same discipline as St. Pierre's controlled sweep and Flynn's handle
characterisation, arrived at from the campaign side: if you change four things between attempts you
learn nothing, and the "iteration" is just rerolling with extra steps. Declaring the axis set *before*
generating turns exploration into a grid you can read.

**House note:** her axis list is the fashion-campaign default —
**lighting · environment · pose · styling.** Add a fifth only when the brief demands it, and name it.

### 6. The hero object gets a dedicated fidelity pass — in post, not in the prompt [STATED]
Onalaja step 3, verbatim: enhance *"the Zaza Bag's texture and color accuracy."* Versace step 3: bag
enhancement, layer masking, colour adjustment.

**This is the most transferable professional move in the whole extraction.** The generative model
will not render your client's actual product correctly — not the hardware, not the weave, not the
Pantone. Every AI campaign that dies in brand review dies here. Her answer is not a better prompt;
it is to **plan for the product to be wrong and budget a compositing pass to fix it.** Product
accuracy is a post-production line item.

**Corollary [INFERRED]:** this means the generation stage should be judged on *light, pose,
environment and mood* only. Rejecting a great frame because the bag's clasp is wrong is a category
error — the clasp was always going to be fixed downstream.

### 7. The retouch stage has a fixed three-target order [STATED]
Both campaigns: **product → subject → background**, in that order. (Bag texture/colour → model
retouch → seamless/corrected backgrounds.)

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** Product first because it is the only element with an objective right answer
and the only one that can fail brand review outright; subject second because it is the emotional
carrier; background last because it is the most forgiving and the most likely to be re-cropped per
channel anyway.

### 8. The **unify pass** is a separate, series-level operation [STATED]
Versace step 4, verbatim: colour grading to *"unify the series and elevate the atmosphere."*
Onalaja step 4: cohesive grading, contrast, palette matched to the brand.

**Mechanism.** This is the difference between *a folder of images* and *a campaign*. Individually
graded frames drift — each one is graded to look its best alone, and collectively they look like six
photographers. One grade applied across the set, decided at the set level, is what makes a viewer
read them as one body of work. **It happens once, at the end, across everything.**

Also note *what* the grade is matched to: **the brand's palette**, not the image's taste. The final
creative decision in both campaigns is an act of brand compliance.

### 9. The four-move production spine [STRUCTURAL — strongest finding in this extraction]

> **RESEARCH → GENERATE → COMPOSITE → UNIFY**

Identical, in order, in two independently posted campaigns, with the same stage vocabulary. That
repetition is what makes it an operating chain rather than a story told after the fact. Generation is
**one of four moves.** Any workflow in which generating *is* the work has already failed her standard.

### 10. Intent is defined on four axes before anything is prompted [STATED]
*Purpose · audience · emotion · constraints* (6452, public outcome copy) — with "From Spark to
Direction: Clarifying Creative Intent" existing as its own lesson.

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** *Emotion* is the axis most systems omit, and it is the one that decides
lighting and pose. *Constraints* is the axis most creatives omit, and it is the one that decides
whether the work is usable. Both belong in the brief, not in the revision round.

### 11. Camera is directed **in language**, as a discipline of its own [STATED]
"Direct the Camera Using Language" is a 12:33 lesson — the longest teaching block in that course.
The public outcome copy names shot type, angle, depth cues and motion.

Backfill honestly: she teaches that camera language is a lever; **`dave-clark` and `nick-st-pierre`
carry the actual vocabulary.** Do not simulate depth here.

### 12. Lighting is named from a closed vocabulary [STATED]
Publicly listed: *soft diffused · hard direct · studio · golden hour · blue hour.* The lesson is
titled *"Lighting: The Secret Weapon of Prompting."*

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** A closed list is a forcing function — it makes "beautiful lighting"
unsayable. You must pick one, which means you have made a decision that a colourist and a shot list
can both act on. Five named options beat infinite adjectives.

### 13. Consistency is its own named problem with its own toolkit [STATED]
"Consistency and Face Control" is a standalone lesson; the levers publicly named are **reference
images, seed numbers, anchor keywords.**

**Mechanism.** She treats a campaign as needing *one person* across many frames, and she does not
expect prose to deliver it. Consistency is architecture (a reference, a locked seed, an anchor), not
persuasion. Compare Flynn's frozen-backbone/variable-head split — same instinct, less system.

### 14. The **real-life visualisation** test [STATED — from the lesson title]
Lesson: *"Shot Lists, Camera Direction & **Real-Life Visualization**."*

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** The shot list must describe a shoot that could physically be executed —
a location, a time of day, a lens, a person who can stand there. This is the single cheapest
anti-slop check available in fashion AI: impossible geometry, impossible light and impossible fabric
physics are what "looks AI" actually means most of the time. If a human crew could not shoot it, the
frame will read wrong even when nobody can say why.

### 15. The chain terminates in a **channel application**, never in an image [STATED]
Look at what her eight final projects actually are: an Instagram campaign · a magazine cover with
typography and cover lines · a mini editorial spread with layout, credits and narrative copy · a
brand aesthetic playbook applied across social, packaging, website and email · a cohesive collection
presented as an editorial board.

**Mechanism.** Every deliverable is *placed* — it has a format, a crop, type on it, or a channel it
lives in. A campaign that stops at "here are six images" has stopped one stage early. This is the
stage most AI creative work skips and the one that makes work look professional.

### 16. Brand aesthetic is a **system with a document**, not a set of assets [STATED]
6451: brand layers = *purpose, positioning, audience, values, personality, narrative*; outputs =
palette **with hex codes**, type, marks, boards; deliverable = a **playbook** applied across channels.

**House note:** the strategy layer here is thinner than what `oren-brand-archetypes` and
`grace-liu` already carry. Use Jen for the *aesthetic-to-channel translation* and the playbook
shape; do not use her for positioning strategy.

### 17. Keep / Kill / Push [STATED name · INFERRED mechanics]
Publicly named in 6452 as her creative-evaluation method: **"the keep, kill, push method."** The
mechanics are behind the paywall.

The house reading, marked plainly as **INFERRED** — use it, but never attribute it to her: run every
generated set through three verdicts. **Keep** = it serves the concept as-is, lock it. **Kill** = it
is good but off-concept; delete it now, because off-concept keepers are how campaigns become folders.
**Push** = it is nearly right on one axis; re-run *that axis only* (Pattern 5). The discipline is that
"kill" must actually be used — a set where nothing was killed was never evaluated.

### 18. She limits AI before she uses it [STATED]
Course 6440 has a lesson titled *"Capabilities and **Limitations** of AI in Fashion Design"* — placed
second, before any technique. 6453 has *"Guardrails and Professional Credibility."* Every course
description carries an ethics/human-judgment line.

**Mechanism [INFERRED].** Knowing in advance which parts of the job the model will fail at is what
lets you *budget* for them (Pattern 6) instead of discovering them in the client review. The
limitation inventory is a production-planning tool, not a disclaimer.

## SIGNATURE MOVES

| # | Move | Do it like this |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The One-Sentence Metaphor** | Write the concept as one sentence containing one figure, plus a claim about the buyer. If it takes two sentences, it is two campaigns. |
| 2 | **The Loose Board** | Lock tone · colour · material · theme. Leave composition open. Build it somewhere cheap. Approve or kill in one look. |
| 3 | **Fixed Iteration Axes** | Declare the axis set before generating — default **lighting · environment · pose · styling** — and move one at a time. |
| 4 | **The Hero-Object Fidelity Pass** | Assume the product renders wrong. Budget a compositing pass for texture and colour accuracy. Never judge generations on product accuracy. |
| 5 | **Product → Subject → Background** | Fixed retouch order. Objective failure first, emotional carrier second, forgiving element last. |
| 6 | **The Series Grade** | One grade, applied once, across the whole set, matched to the brand palette. Never per-image. |
| 7 | **Keep / Kill / Push** | Three verdicts per generated set. Push re-runs one axis. If nothing was killed, nothing was evaluated. |
| 8 | **The Real-Life Visualisation Check** | Could a crew shoot this? Location, hour, lens, a body that fits there. If no, the frame will read AI. |
| 9 | **Place the Deliverable** | Finish in a channel: crop, type, cover lines, layout, playbook page. Never hand over loose images. |

## QUALITY RUBRIC — how she'd separate good from bad

Ordered by what fails most often. A campaign passes only if every line is yes.

1. **Can you say the concept in one sentence, and is there a metaphor in it?**
   No → everything downstream is decoration.
2. **Does the set read as one campaign?** Cover the captions and look at the six frames together.
   One light logic, one palette, one person, one world — or six photographers.
3. **Is the product *accurate*?** Texture, hardware, colour, proportion. This is the line that fails
   brand review and it is not negotiable by taste.
4. **Could this have been shot?** Name the location, the hour, the lens. Impossible physics is what
   "looks AI" means.
5. **Is anything in it specifically the brand's?** Name the two or three signatures you researched and
   point to them in frame. If you cannot, the work is interchangeable.
6. **Did you kill anything?** A set with no kills was never evaluated.
7. **Is it placed?** Crop, type, format, channel. Loose images are one stage short.
8. **Did a human make the final call, and can you say what the call was?**
