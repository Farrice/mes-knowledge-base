# Tom Segura — Source Quotes

Verbatim transcript anchors, one per genius pattern. Calibration ground truth so the skill never drifts from what Segura actually said. Quote when scoring or producing; if your output can't trace to one of these, you've drifted. Source: "How I Write" interview, 13,322-word transcript.

## Pattern 1 — The "That's a Thing" Antenna

> "The more you do that and you're in conversation and you say something you're like, 'That's a thing.' That becomes like the honestly the biggest muscle of it all I think — recognizing, 'Oh, that's something.' Versus I'm just kind of shooting the [expletive], just talking with you. Sometimes you get something and it has legs and you develop the skill of going, 'That's a thing.'"

> "The best thing to do if you're a comic is honestly talk a lot. You're going to say more funny things if you're talking more."

## Pattern 2 — Complaining is the Engine, Indifference is Death

> "Being annoyed is funny. My 7-year-old is annoyed all the time and he's hilarious. He's just like, 'What's this table doing in the middle of the room? I keep running into it.' To be bothered by furniture — that's a funny perspective to have."

> "It's not funny to be like, 'It's fine. I don't care.' It's funny if you go, 'This tastes like [expletive], like somebody [expletive] into a cup and boiled it.' Or if you're like, 'This is God's gift to the world. I wish I could bathe in this.' But it's not funny to not have an opinion."

## Pattern 3 — The Universal-but-Unarticulated

> "You land the airplane, you get to the gate, the pilot does the ding and everyone stands up. And then you don't get off the airplane for like 15 minutes. It was one of those things I noticed probably 60, 70 times. Now all of a sudden someone makes a joke about this. They nail it and it's just hilarious."

> "You go, 'Why didn't I just say it?' You had the same observation but you never articulated it. And then some other comedian does and you're like, 'Of course.' We've all experienced this but that person was the first one to actually say it."

## Pattern 4 — The "Way In" (Reason-for-Telling)

> "You have a story, you go, 'But what's my way in?' Because almost like your reason for telling the story is as important if not more so than the story itself. Because it gives it weight."

> "I would start by saying, 'I'm so grateful to be performing here. I never thought this was in the cards for me. I thought the highlight of my career was when I booked the campaign to be the spokesman for Subway.' So right there, you gave yourself a reason to tell the story. They're engaged before you start it."

## Pattern 5 — Just Say Them All, the Editor Can't Come Too Early

> "His advice — Jeff Tate, great comic — he goes, 'Just do them all. Write down all the thoughts, even the dumb ones. And take the dumb ones on stage. Just say them all.'"

> "When you write down the dumb one and you go, 'Ah, that's not worth saying,' when you get on stage, something happens between the idea and the performance where you take it somewhere you can't anticipate without an audience. The editor can't come too early."

## Pattern 6 — Surprise is the Core Mechanism

> "The best feeling of a joke always is the element of surprise. If they are anticipating, anticipating, and then you turn somewhere else — you say the thing they were not expecting — that is the biggest rush."

> "I like to upset them and then have them be like shaking their head, but you still manage to get them to laugh. So they're reluctantly laughing. They're laughing against their own judgment. You can point it out: 'Okay guys, there's clearly something here. You guys are holding back. You actually do think this is funny.'"

## Pattern 7 — Earned Provocation Arc

> "Minute 5 and 10 and 15, they're building trust. They're getting to know you, they're like, 'Yeah, I like this guy.' And then minute 25 you could be like, 'Here's the most awful thought,' and they're on your side. So you get away with more."

> "The statement comes first and you go, 'That can't be your statement.' And then you follow it up with jokes... you know they don't like it, then follow it up with your jokes to support it, to the point where you're just going to either give in or be like, 'I'm leaving the room.'"

## Pattern 8 — Play It Straight, Ground the Absurd

> "We play it like this is the most sincere thing, which is the most fun thing to do — to take an absurd idea and really play it seriously."

> "Get the dramatic person to deliver this thing because it gives it weight, it grounds it, it makes it real. The more ridiculous it is the less I would want the comedy person. It's funnier if they're not trying to be funny. That's the key to it all — both of them playing it super real."

## Pattern 9 — Hold On The Real, Duration as Tension

> "The longer you hold on the real amount of time, it's funnier. American studio films have an edit every 3 seconds. A European film, the guy making coffee, it'll be a one shot... you're watching the whole thing fill up. The waiting makes it feel like it's real."

> "You want to sit in the real tension of a 25-second door hold. Which is so long to watch, but you're like, 'That's why it's funny.' The realness of it is what's funny."

## Pattern 10 — Long-Version-First, Edit-Second

> "The first time you do it, you just tell the super long version of it. Don't go into it with 'it's got to be like this.' And you realize the story is fat. Then you begin the edit process: you don't need this information, you don't need this. Now you have it tighter."

> "Then you go in and go, 'I need something here, I need something here' — putting in jokes and punchlines throughout. Ultimately you love to end one of those on a big punchline."

## Pattern 11 — Word-Choice as Sound Engineering

> "'Yanked' is a way funnier way to say that than 'my kid pulled.' A thousand percent. There's words and sounds that are funnier. Yiddish things are just universally hilarious. Somebody got clipped by a car — the clip is a funnier word than hit."

> "Offstage conversations — there's just funny people who describe things in a funny way. The guy didn't get hit, he got tugged. That's funnier. Your ears just pick up on it."

## Pattern 12 — Voices & Accents Paint the Picture

> "When you're painting a picture of a story versus somebody being like, 'Are you looking for something?' — you imagine a different person. It adds color to the story. Doing accents and voices is a huge part of making it funny."

> "I talk about my mother all the time, who — her accent is strong. She's from Peru. She's still, 'How are you?' And I'm like, 'Jesus, I'm Catholic, you know?'"

## Pattern 13 — Story Ownership, No One Else Has This

> "You see like 15 people doing AI bits. The rule is, if your bit is far superior to these other ones, then you do it. Otherwise — it's what led me to doing so much storytelling about life. Stuff with my kids, my mom, friends, because no one else has that story. No one else has that perspective."

> "I report. People go, 'I know you made that [expletive] up about your kids.' I don't make up a single thing about those guys. My kid yanked a [expletive] duck out of a pond by its neck and was like, 'Can we take this home?'"

## Pattern 14 — Silence as Power

> "Good seasoned performers are not scared of silence. They use silence to their advantage. New comedians are terrified of silence. They get really loud, frantic, screaming. The super seasoned people — if they're actually going somewhere with what they're saying, the audience slowly leans in."

> "The best mindset for me to go on stage with is silly. When you're backstage goofing off, really [expletive] around with your friends, saying dumb jokes — you take that energy on stage. Those are the best sets."

## Pattern 15 — Callbacks Build a World (But Don't Overdo)

> "Callbacks are fantastic, but what you get aware of and don't want to fall into is the overdoing it. There's comics who are just like callback, callback, callback. No, no, no. When you're cleverly placing them — the fine line of the perfect amount of them."

> "In conversation, when you do a callback it shows the person you're paying attention. It becomes charming and smart. 'Oh, this person's witty, they remembered what we said 5 minutes ago.'"

**The Dig (signature move, anchors Hidden Knowledge 3):**

> "Sometimes the thing that's too obvious isn't the joke because it's too obvious. You digging in on the feeling of what's bothering you is where the joke lies. The joke is in expanding on why it upsets you... There's the joke — you're bothered by hardly any time. You're spending 15 minutes and you're [expletive] annoyed. It's like somebody yelling at the trailer before the movie. Bro, just eat some popcorn."

**Prose strips the crutches (anchors Hidden Knowledge 6):**

> "It was the most exposed and vulnerable I've ever felt. As a stand-up I control so much with my delivery, my cadence, my facial expressions. With the written word, all you got is words. You can't take a week — you have immediate response on stage."
