# LENS CARD — Ward Farnsworth (rhetoric professor; engineer of why sentences sound eloquent)
> Compiled 2026-07-27 from genius.md (canonical). FIRING RULE: this card decides WHETHER this lens has something to say about a draft line; when a lens fires, READ the matching genius.md section before treating. Never treat from the card alone.

## Lenses
### The Saxon Punch (The "Chord Change")
Use larger, multi-syllable Latinate "idea" words in the first half of the sentence, and end the sentence strictly with one-syllable, hard-consonant Saxon words. The Latinate builds the intellectual tension the Saxon words shatter — "If everything is loud, nothing is loud."
**Test:** The sentence feels like a physical punch at the end; the final word is impossible to ignore.

### The Apistrophic Echo (Epistrophe)
Identify the single most important word you want remembered. Structure 2–3 consecutive clauses to end with that exact word.
**Test:** The repeated word feels like the inescapable conclusion of the argument.

### The Chiasmus Reversal (ABBA)
When the market has a concept backwards, frame it: "[Concept A] does not dictate [Concept B]. [Concept B] dictates [Concept A]."
**Test:** The reversal feels "wise" and closed-loop, admitting no logical interruption. (The "wisdom illusion": beautifully constructed sentences sound inherently more true, circumventing critical defenses.)

### The "Two Languages" Translation
Write the conceptual statement; immediately restate the same idea in entirely different, shorter, visceral words.
**Test:** Intellectual validation followed instantly by an emotional gut-punch.

### The Anaphoric Drumbeat (Anaphora)
Open 3 (or 2–4) consecutive units with the stakes-carrying phrase verbatim, varying only what follows; the climax lands on whatever *breaks* the pattern. This is structural priming — the announcer getting louder as the play nears the goal.
**Test:** By the third opening the reader is leaning forward; the pattern-breaking line hits hardest.

### The Switch — Anaphora ↔ Epistrophe
Run two or three units of repetition in one position, then shift the repeated phrase to the opposite position, then snap back. "Repeat, then relief, then back to it, because the ear *wants* it."
**Test:** The return of the repetition *satisfies* — "you got used to it, you stopped, you came back."

### The Rule of Three
Default any parallel run to exactly three beats. Two feels incomplete; four+ drags and announces itself.
**Test:** The three-beat lands as "rhetorically perfect" — complete, balanced, nothing to add or cut.

### Start at the End (End-Weight Engineering)
Decide the final word you want left hanging in the air, then construct the sentence so it arrives last and lands clean (usually short, hard, Saxon). "Always start at the end. Work backwards from the end of the sentence."
**Test:** Moving any other word to the end would weaken it.

### The Conceptual (Imperfect) Pattern
Aim for the *feeling* of the closed loop, not geometric symmetry. If the literal device sounds archaic, invert the *ideas* and let the pattern resonate structurally.
**Test:** The line still "rings" with balance even though no two words are identical.

### Reading the Rhetorical Soil (Occasion-Sensing)
Diagnose intent first: they have it backwards → chiasmus. Must linger → epistrophe. Building to climax → anaphora. Thesis that must land like a punch → Saxon close. "Anytime what you mean is *and vice versa*, you probably could write that as a chiasmus."
**Test:** You can name *why this device, here* before writing the line. If you can't, you're decorating, not deploying.

### The Master Rule — A Good Reason, Not a Rule
Follow style rules by default; break one (passive voice, repetition, Latinate stretch) only when you can state the reason out loud (rhythm, contrast, juxtaposition, end-weight). "There's no rule except: make sure you've got a good reason for what you're doing."
**Test:** One-sentence justification a reader would accept. No reason = revert to the rule.

### The Apprenticeship Loop
Pick one master worth apprenticing to (survived "the tournament" of time); rewrite modern pieces as they would have written them; then throw the imitations away — keep the trained ear, not the costume. "Memorization, imitation — these things are underrated."
**Test:** Your drafts show the master's instincts with none of the cadence. If a reader could name who you trained on, the loop failed.

### The Hand-Transcription X-Ray
Write great lines out by hand — never copy-paste — then x-ray them: name the devices, the register run, the last word.
**Test:** For each line, one sentence on the pattern under it and why the last word is last. "It's beautiful" = you read it, didn't x-ray it.

### The Expandability Test
Latinate words expand (acquire → acquisition; "you can often put a *-tion* on" them); Saxon words won't and carry hard consonants.
**Test:** Sort any suspect word in under five seconds without a dictionary.

### The Politeness Register (Kill vs. Execute)
Choose register by intended social distance, not impressiveness: deniability/diplomacy/softening → Latinate; accountability/urgency/felt impact → Saxon. "If you want to say it bluntly, you say *kill*... to be polite... *execute*." "When you really know what you're talking about, you can explain yourself in Saxon words."
**Test:** The register matches the intended distance ("headcount reduction" vs. "we cut jobs" is a targeting decision).

### The Sustained Saxon Run
Reserve an all-Saxon run for the one passage per piece where stakes are absolute — "when you really want to strike deep, you stay there." Two gates: (1) the substance must be genuinely profound; (2) writing 32 Saxon words in a row that say something worth saying is much harder than it sounds.
**Test:** The words "burst at their seams" — weight of meaning strains against humility of words; otherwise it flatlines.

### Devices as Punctuation for the Silent Reader
A device works "like an exclamation point would, or like a rising tone of voice would." When adapting spoken material, ask where the voice would have risen and install the structural equivalent there.
**Test:** A draft flat *only* on the page usually lacks structural punctuation, not stronger words.

### The Counter-Immersion Diet
The ear is trained by whatever it soaks in; "social media is like a campus where rhetoric is taught — it's taught by bad example." Schedule deliberate immersion in one or two chosen masters as counterweight to the feed.

## Anti-patterns / calibration
- **Master calibration:** "If they notice the technique, it's bad technique." Restraint over saturation — one well-placed device beats five, used "sparingly... like an exclamation point." Earn the device with the line. Influence, never imitation. Never enumerate or label devices in output.
- **AN-1 Device-stacking:** more than one device firing in a single line → cut to the strongest.
- **AN-2 Rhetoric on a trivial line:** if the device removed, the sentence still does its job → it was decoration; cut it. No payload → no device.
- **AN-3 Breaking clarity for sound:** if a cold first-time reader must re-read to parse, clarity lost to sound; rebuild plainly first, then add music.
- **AN-4 Latinate with no Saxon payoff:** conceptual stretch never resolving into short, hard, picture-able words = tension with no release. Land the punch or simplify.
- **AN-5 Imitation instead of influence:** "Anybody who imitates anybody sounds like a fool." Costume rhetoric → say it plainly in the native voice.
- **AN-6 Relentless repetition:** more than three consecutive repeats with no switch or break → trim, or switch position. "Lay off."
- **Decision gate (pre-device):** (1) Does this line carry the thesis? (2) What soil is this? (3) Where is the end — is the sentence built backwards from it? (4) Is there a chord change? (5) Am I stacking? (6) Would Farnsworth notice the technique — or say "that was well said"? (7) Can each broken rule be justified in one sentence? (8) Does this sound like *this* voice?
