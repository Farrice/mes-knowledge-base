# Ward Parallelism — Anaphora, Epistrophe, Isocolon & the Switch (Ward Farnsworth)
Churchill did not say "we will keep fighting everywhere." He said *We shall fight on the beaches. We shall fight on the landing grounds. We shall fight in the fields...* — and then he broke his own drumbeat with *we shall never surrender*. That is the whole engine in one passage: a repeated front-phrase primes the ear like a sports announcer getting louder near the goal line, and the line that *snaps the pattern* is the goal. This workflow builds that run deliberately — front-repeat (anaphora), end-repeat (epistrophe), or the switch between them — and lands the climax on the place the repetition lets go.

## Input Required
- **The payload line** — the one sentence/claim the whole run exists to deliver (the climax). The run is the runway; this is the landing.
- **The repeated phrase candidate(s)** — the 1–5 words you suspect should recur. (You will refine these; the front-phrase and the end-Anchor are chosen differently — see Step 2.)
- **The 2–4 angles / stakes** the run can march through before it lands (what changes between beats while the repeated phrase holds).
- **Occasion**: is this *building to a climax* (→ default anaphora) or does a single concept need to *linger after the reader stops* (→ default epistrophe)? If both, you want the switch.
- **Format** (social hook / headline / converting copy / tagline / essay line / speech) — sets length and how many devices the medium can carry before the machinery shows.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Diagnose the soil — which repetition, and where
Farnsworth's front-door move is occasion-sensing: pick the device the *intent* calls for, then build it. Anaphora and epistrophe are not interchangeable — they do opposite jobs.

| If you are trying to... | Use | Why | Repeat lives at the... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Build toward a climax** — prime the ear, make the reader lean in before the goal | **Anaphora** (front-repeat) | The repeated opening is the announcer getting louder; the line that *breaks* it is the goal | **start** of each unit |
| **Make one concept linger** after they stop reading | **Epistrophe** (end-repeat) | The end is the most powerful real estate in English; the last word is the one carried away | **end** of each unit |
| **Both** — a long build that needs a memorable kicker, or pure repetition that's starting to feel mechanical | **The switch** (Pattern 6) | Pattern, relief, return: the ear *gets used to it, you stop, you come back, and that creates satisfaction* | front for a stretch, then **end** (or vice-versa) |

Write the verdict in one sentence: *"This is a [climax-build / must-linger / both], so the spine is [anaphora / epistrophe / switch], and the climax lands on ____."* If you can't, fix the payload line before going further.

### Step 2: Forge the repeated phrase
The front-phrase (anaphora) and the end-Anchor (epistrophe) are chosen on different rules. Get the one your spine needs.

- **Anaphoric front-phrase**: a phrase that carries the *stakes* and can survive verbatim repetition while everything after it changes ("*We shall fight*..." / "*I have a dream that*..."). It should sound like a banner, not a throat-clear. Test: read it three times in a row — does it gather force, or does it sound like a stutter?
- **Epistrophic end-Anchor**: refine the core concept down to **1–3 highly resonant words** the reader must keep. Bias toward short, hard, Saxon, picture-able words — the end is where the punch lands ("...of the *people*, by the *people*, for the *people*"). Test: is this the *only* word that could end every clause, or just *a* word?

Lock the phrase now. The discipline of the device is that it repeats *verbatim* — if you find yourself wanting to vary the repeated words "for flow," you have the wrong phrase, not the wrong device.

### Step 3: Build the run-up — three beats, rising
Default to **three** (Pattern 7: two feels incomplete, four+ drags and announces itself). Construct the beats so the repeated phrase holds while the *angle or stakes* escalate — beat 3 should sit at higher stakes than beat 1, so the run is a climb, not a list.

| Beat | Repeated phrase (verbatim) | What changes (angle / stakes — should escalate) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | (front-phrase or end-Anchor) | lowest stakes / nearest case |
| 2 | (same, verbatim) | wider / higher |
| 3 | (same, verbatim) | highest stakes — sets up the break |

For anaphora, keep the repeat at the **front** and let the tails differ. For epistrophe, build "distinct clauses that build varying angles or progressively higher stakes" and **lock every ending** into the Anchor (the legacy Anchor / Run-up / Lock-endings move). For isocolon, hold the beats to roughly **equal length and grammatical shape** so the parallel is felt in the body, not just the edges — symmetry is what lends the run the "hue of wisdom."

### Step 4: Land the climax — break the pattern (or switch it)
This is the move that separates a run from a list. The run is priming; the payoff is what *breaks* the priming.

- **Anaphora → broken pattern**: after three front-repeats, the climax line does NOT start with the repeated phrase. The break IS the climax. ("We shall fight... / We shall fight... / We shall fight..." → "*we shall never surrender*.") Land that final line on a short, hard, Saxon close so the broken pattern also punches.
- **Epistrophe → the lock holds, then releases**: three clauses lock into the Anchor; the kicker either lands the Anchor one last time as the standalone sentence, or steps off it onto a Saxon punch.
- **The switch (Pattern 6)** when pure repetition is starting to feel mechanical, or you want a takedown kicker: run repetition in one position, shift it to the opposite position, then snap back. Bentsen's takedown is the canonical map — **end, end, beginning, end**:
  > "I served with *Jack Kennedy*. I knew *Jack Kennedy*. *Jack Kennedy* was a friend of mine. Senator, you're no *Jack Kennedy*."

  Churchill's switch rides a tricolon into a doublet: "...*giving all, daring all, enduring all*, to the utmost, to the end." Three end-repeats (anaphoric front "all") resolve into a two-beat close. The return is what satisfies — *you got used to it, you stopped, you came back.*

### Step 5: Relentlessness check (AN-6) — relief and return
Before you finalize, count the consecutive repeats. **Three is the ceiling.** More than three identical beats with no break or switch and the drumbeat becomes a tic — "the relentless use of too much repetition where you feel like, lay off." If you have four or five, either trim to three or deploy the switch (Step 4) so the ear gets relief and return. Then re-read once at full speed: if you can feel the seams of the device, it's showing — bury it or cut a beat.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Social hook** (LinkedIn / X) | One run per post — at this length a three-beat anaphora *is* the post. Front-repeat to prime, break on the last line, Saxon close. Don't also run a chiasmus in the same post; the machinery shows fast at hook length. |
| **Headline** | Usually too short for a full run; use a two-beat isocolon or a single epistrophic echo into the last word. End on the short, hard, concrete word. |
| **Converting copy** (VSL / landing / email) | Epistrophe is the workhorse here — hammer the one belief that must survive the close into the same Anchor across three clauses ("...it is your fault"). Use anaphora to build into the CTA, then break the pattern *on* the CTA line so the offer is the goal the announcer was driving toward. |
| **Tagline / brand line** | Rule-of-three isocolon for the quotable, closed-loop feel ("of the people, by the people, for the people"). Symmetry lends the line the "wisdom illusion" — it feels inevitable rather than written. |
| **Essay line / Substack** | A single anaphoric run at a section's turn, or an epistrophic tricolon to close a paragraph so the concept lingers into the white space. One run per section — let the prose breathe between. |
| **Speech / manifesto / keynote** | Native habitat and the only place to deploy the full switch. Build with anaphora, switch to epistrophe for the kicker, land the climax on the broken pattern, close in Saxon — but still sparingly. The announcer gets loud *near* the goal, not for the whole game. |

## Output Format
Deliver three options so the user can choose the build that fits the medium — a 3-part run, a 2-part long-runway version, and a switch.

```
SPINE: [anaphora / epistrophe / switch] — chosen because [climax-build / must-linger / both]
REPEATED PHRASE: "______"  (front-phrase or 1–3-word Anchor)
CLIMAX LANDS ON: "______"  (the broken-pattern line / final Saxon punch)

OPTION 1 — Three-beat run (rule of three, climax on the break)
  [Beat 1 — repeated phrase + lowest-stakes angle]
  [Beat 2 — repeated phrase + wider angle]
  [Beat 3 — repeated phrase + highest-stakes angle]
  → [CLIMAX — breaks the pattern / lands the Anchor / Saxon close]

OPTION 2 — Two-beat, long-runway (more setup before the repeat lands)
  [Beat 1 — longer clause, repeat at the chosen position]
  [Beat 2 — longer clause, repeat]
  → [CLIMAX]

OPTION 3 — The switch (front-repeat → end-repeat, then snap back)
  [Beat 1 — repeat at start]
  [Beat 2 — repeat at start]
  [Beat 3 — repeat moves to the end]
  → [KICKER — return of the repetition, the satisfying snap-back]

WHY THIS LANDS: [one line on what the break/switch does to the ear — no device-name labeling in the delivered copy itself]
```

**Worked example A — anaphora, climax on the break (manifesto / converting copy)**
> *Before (flat):* "You're responsible for a lot of outcomes in your business, including the market's reaction, your team's performance, and your own growth over time."
>
> *After:*
> If the market rejects the product, that is on you.
> If the team misses the vision, that is on you.
> If you wake up five years out and nothing has changed —
> **that, too, is on you.**
>
> *Why it lands:* three beats prime the ear with the same front-frame ("If... that is on you"); the climax keeps the Anchor but *breaks the rhythm* with the dash and the standalone "that, too" — the snapped pattern is the punch, and "on you" closes hard and Saxon.

**Worked example B — epistrophe, the Anchor lingers (essay close / tagline)**
> *Before (flat):* "Good writing is mostly about cutting unnecessary words and being concise wherever you can."
>
> *After:* "The first draft adds; the second draft *cuts*. The good editor cuts. The great one cuts, then *cuts*."
>
> *Why it lands:* the end-Anchor "cuts" recurs at the close of each unit until it is the inescapable last word; the final clause switches — adds a beat, then returns to "cuts" — so the repetition releases instead of droning (AN-6 relief-and-return), and the reader carries one Saxon verb out of the line.

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (esp. *Structural Priming & Flow*, *Impact Velocity*, *Economy of Impact*). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Three is the ceiling (AN-6).** Count consecutive identical beats. Four+ with no switch or break → trim to three or deploy the switch. The drumbeat must never become a tic.
- **The repeat is verbatim.** If the "repeated" phrase quietly varies word-to-word for flow, it isn't anaphora/epistrophe — it's the wrong phrase. Re-forge in Step 2.
- **The climax breaks the pattern (or the Anchor releases).** A run that ends on the same rhythm it set up is a list, not a crescendo. The reader should feel the *snap*. If nothing breaks, the goal was never scored.
- **The run carries the thesis, not ornament (AN-2).** If you removed the parallel structure and the passage still did its job fine, the device was decoration on a trivial line — cut it. Reserve the architecture for the line that matters.
- **One device, not a stack (AN-1).** If a chiasmus or a second run is firing inside the same passage, the machinery is showing — cut to the run that does the most work.
- **The last word lands clean.** The climax (broken pattern or final Anchor) should end on a short, hard, Saxon, picture-able word. Moving any other word to the end should weaken it.
- **Influence, not pastiche (AN-5).** It primes and breaks like Churchill *without sounding like* Churchill. If a reader would think "this is doing a Churchill impression," rewrite in the native voice. The reader should feel the build, never see the scaffold.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Building a list instead of a climb.** Three beats at the *same* stakes is a list; the run must escalate so beat 3 sits higher than beat 1 and the break has something to launch off. *Recovery:* re-rank the angles in Step 3 by stakes and reorder smallest → largest; if they don't escalate, you have parallel facts, not a crescendo — find the rising stakes or drop to a single epistrophic echo.
- **No break — the run just stops.** The most common failure: three lovely repeats and then a fourth in the same shape, so the "climax" is indistinguishable from the runway. *Recovery:* return to Step 4. The payoff is the line that does NOT match the pattern. If everything matches, nothing climaxes.
- **Relentlessness — five beats because "more is more" (AN-6).** Sustained repetition past three reads as a tic and the reader thinks "lay off." *Recovery:* trim to three, or switch the repetition to the opposite position so the ear gets relief and return — *the return is what they remember decades later.*
- **Wrong soil — epistrophe where the occasion wants a climax (or vice-versa).** End-repeat makes a concept linger but does not *build*; front-repeat builds but does not leave one word ringing. *Recovery:* re-run Step 1. If you need both — a build that also leaves a kicker — that's the switch, not a single device used twice.
