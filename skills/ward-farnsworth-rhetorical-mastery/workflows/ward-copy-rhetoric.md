# Ward Copy Rhetoric — Converting Copy on Classical Devices (Ward Farnsworth)

A VSL, landing page, or email already has its bones — the proof, the offer, the mechanism. What it usually lacks is the *ear*. The objection-handling argues instead of closing the loop; the one belief that has to survive the close evaporates because nothing made it ring; the CTA trails off on a soft Latinate word ("Get started today and *transform* your *productivity*"). This pass does not rebuild the copy and does not add claims. It finds the four or five lines where the conversion actually turns and installs the one device each moment calls for — chiasmus on the objection, epistrophe on the must-linger belief, Two Languages on the logic-plus-gut beat, a Saxon punch on the button — then buries the machinery so the reader feels *wise* and *certain*, never *sold to with a rhetorical kit*. Ward supplies the rhetorical layer inside copy-engine / luke-iha's proof-and-offer architecture; he is the man tuning the lines, not the man writing the page.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this workflow is dangerous *because* it's tempting to deploy all four devices at once):
- **Pattern 3 — The Chiasmus Reversal (ABBA)** — the objection-handler: "[A] does not dictate [B]. [B] dictates [A]." The closed loop that makes arguing against it feel like arguing against gravity.
- **Pattern 2 — The Apistrophic Echo (Epistrophe)** — repeat the must-remember word at the *end* of consecutive clauses, so the one belief that has to survive the close lingers after the reader stops reading.
- **Pattern 4 — The "Two Languages" Translation** — say the idea once conceptually (logical brain signs off), then once stripped to the gut (emotional brain buys). The logic+gut beat.
- **Cross-Domain Applications** — the **Converting copy (VSL/landing/email)** row: which device wants to live at which moment of the funnel.
- **Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-2 (rhetoric on a trivial line — keep devices off filler)**, **AN-1 (device-stacking that announces itself)**, **AN-3 (breaking clarity for sound)**, **AN-4 (Latinate with no Saxon payoff)**.
- **Decision Framework** — the gate below.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before touching a single line. Specifically confirm Q1 (each device lands on a line that carries a *conversion payload* — an objection, the core belief, the offer — never on connective filler; AN-2), Q2 (you can name *why this device, here* — chiasmus because they have it backwards, epistrophe because it must linger, Saxon because it's the punch), and Q5 (you are NOT stacking — one device per moment; if two are firing on the same line, the machinery is showing). **Honesty spine: this pass tunes the prose, never the proof.** If a line needs a real claim or a real proof point to convert, that is copy-engine / luke-iha's job, not a rhetorical device. Devices make a *true* line ring; they cannot make a *missing* one exist. If the copy converts on a claim you can't substantiate, stop — route the claim back through the copywriter, not through Ward.

## Input Required

- **The copy as it stands** — the actual VSL script, landing page, or email. Whole thing, not a summary. We're tuning specific lines, so we need the real lines.
- **The format** — VSL / landing page / email (drives how much Latinate runway each beat gets and how many devices the piece can bear without announcing itself).
- **The single core belief** — the one thing the reader *must* believe by the time they hit the CTA, or the sale dies. (This becomes the epistrophe anchor.)
- **The #1 objection** — the strongest "yes, but…" the reader is holding. (This becomes the chiasmus reversal.)
- **The offer / CTA line** — the exact words on the button or the close. (This becomes the Saxon punch.)
- **Proof status** (honesty spine) — confirm the claims at these moments are already true/substantiated. We are tuning the *ear*, not manufacturing belief.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Map the conversion turns; do NOT carpet-bomb the page (AN-2 gate)

A converting page has dozens of sentences and maybe five that the sale actually turns on. Devices belong only on those five. Read the copy and mark the *high-stakes moments* — the beats where the reader either crosses or bails. Everything else stays plain on purpose; plain prose is the floor that makes the tuned lines audible.

| Conversion moment | Where it lives in the copy | What has to happen there | Device the soil calls for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The objection** (the strongest "yes, but…") | usually mid-body, after the mechanism | shut it so cleanly that arguing back feels like arguing against gravity | **Chiasmus** (Pattern 3) |
| **The core belief** (the one thing that must survive the close) | the spine, restated across the page | linger in the ear after the reader stops reading | **Epistrophe** (Pattern 2) |
| **The logic+gut beat** (the proof/desire pivot) | where you justify *and* make them want | satisfy the logical brain, then gut-punch the emotional one | **Two Languages** (Pattern 4) |
| **The CTA / offer line** | the button, the close | land the *do-it* word like a slammed door | **Saxon close** (Pattern 1) |

> **The AN-2 rule for copy:** if you removed the device from a line and the copy still did its job fine, that line was filler — the device was decoration, and decoration on filler *trains the reader to ignore your emphasis* exactly where you'll need it. Mark **at most one moment per device**. A VSL can carry all four (it's long); a landing page usually two or three; a single email often *one*. More devices than conversion-turns = machinery showing.

### Step 2 — Chiasmus on the objection (Pattern 3): close the loop, don't argue

Find the #1 objection. Most copy *argues* against it — stacks counter-evidence, which invites the reader to keep score. Chiasmus doesn't argue; it inverts the reader's frame so the objection dissolves. Extract the two poles, state the frame as the reader currently holds it (A→B), then cross it (B→A). The closed loop borrows the "hue of wisdom": a perfectly balanced sentence makes the brain assume the *idea* inside it is balanced and true, circumventing the critical defense that an argument would have triggered.

| Step | Move | Example (premium service, "I can't afford it") |
|---|---|---|
| Extract the poles | A = the reader's lever, B = your standard | A = *budget* · B = *standards/work* |
| Forward frame (their assumption) | "[A] dictates [B]" | "Your budget decides the quality you get." |
| The reversal (the killing blow) | "[B] dictates [A]" | "Your budget doesn't determine our standards; our standards determine your budget." |
| The knockout (shortest version) | strip to the bone | "We don't lower the work to meet the price. We raise the price to meet the work." |

Deliver the reversal in two registers and let the copy pick: the **direct ABBA** (full crisscross) and the **conceptual chiasmus** (Pattern 9 — the ideas invert but the exact words don't repeat, so it reads conversational, not archaic). In modern copy the conceptual version usually wins; a literal ABBA can sound like a fortune cookie. Aim for the *feeling* of the closed loop over geometric perfection. The success metric: arguing against the line should feel like arguing against gravity.

### Step 3 — Epistrophe on the must-linger belief (Pattern 2): make the spine ring

Take the single core belief. Pick the *one word* that is the belief — the word you'd tattoo on the reader if you could. Then find two or three clauses, already near each other in the copy, and end each on that exact word. The repetition at the *end* makes the word feel like the inescapable conclusion of the argument — it's the last thing the ear holds before moving on, and it's still ringing at the CTA.

```
CORE BELIEF, in one word: __________
Three clauses (built from copy that's already there), each landing on it:
  "… ________ [word]."
  "… ________ [word]."
  "… ________ [word]."
```

Two guardrails from the genius:
- **Three, not five (AN-6 / Pattern 7).** Three beats is "rhetorically perfect." Past three, epistrophe stops being a drumbeat and becomes a tic — the reader thinks *lay off*. If the copy needs the belief hammered more than thrice, switch one instance to the *front* of a clause (anaphora) and snap back (the Switch, Pattern 6) so the ear gets relief and return.
- **The word must be Saxon-hard if it can be.** A belief landing on *transformation* floats; landing on *free*, *yours*, *safe*, *gone*, *home*, *true* rings. The epistrophe word and the Saxon close (Step 5) often want to be the *same* word — that's fine, that's the spine and the punch agreeing.

### Step 4 — Two Languages on the logic+gut beat (Pattern 4): justify, then gut-punch

Find the beat where the copy is doing double duty — proving the thing works *and* making the reader want it. This is where Two Languages earns its place. State the idea once in the conceptual/Latinate register (the logical brain gets its justification and signs the permission slip), then immediately restate the *exact same idea* in short, visceral, street-level words (the emotional brain gets the gut-punch and actually buys). Intellectual validation, then desire — in that order, back to back.

| The idea | Latinate / conceptual (logic signs off) | Saxon / gut (desire buys) |
|---|---|---|
| (the true thing the copy claims here) | (how a consultant would justify it) | (how it feels at 2am — short, hard, picture-able) |
| e.g. *the system removes operational friction* | "It eliminates the operational overhead that compounds across your week." | "You stop drowning. You get your nights back." |
| e.g. *the union will not be dissolved* (Lincoln) | "I do not expect the Union to be dissolved—" | "I expect it will cease to be divided." |

The success metric is a felt sequence: the reader *agrees* (logic), then *aches* (gut). If both halves are conceptual, you've only sold the logical brain and the reader leaves to "think about it." If both are Saxon, you skipped the justification the skeptic needed. The *change* between registers is the whole point — the same chord-change principle as the Saxon punch, run across two sentences instead of one.

### Step 5 — Saxon close on the CTA (Pattern 1 + Pattern 8): land the button like a door

The CTA is the most-wasted line in copywriting — it floats off on *transform / discover / unlock / elevate your potential*. Engineer it backwards (Pattern 8): decide the last word first — the short, hard, picture-able Saxon word the reader carries to the button — then run the value *conceptually* just before it so the logical brain signs off, and drop the close on the stony word.

- Run the value Latinate (one clause is enough — you have almost no runway at the CTA): *"You've seen the system, the proof, the entire architecture of how this works—"*
- Then the punch, ending on the chosen Saxon word: *"now build."* / *"start."* / *"it's yours."* / *"come home."*
- The button text itself wants to *be* the punch. *"Build it"* > *"Get started transforming your business today."*

> **The chord-change guard (AN-4):** the Latinate value-run exists *only* to make the Saxon close land. A long impressive CTA that never drops to the stony word is pomp — tension with no release. And: "when you really know what you're talking about, you can explain yourself in Saxon words." A button that needs *transform* to feel important is a button that doesn't trust its own offer.

### Step 6 — Weave so the devices stay invisible (AN-1 + AN-3 gates): the final read-aloud

Now read the *whole copy aloud, cold,* as a first-time buyer — not the four lines in isolation, but how they sit in the flow. This is the step that separates "tuned copy" from "a rhetorical-device demo reel."

- **The one-device-per-moment check (AN-1).** Confirm no single line is doing chiasmus *and* epistrophe *and* a Saxon punch at once. If the objection line is also trying to be the epistrophe anchor and also closing in Saxon, the machinery is screaming. Cut each line back to the one device that does the most work there.
- **The spacing check.** The four tuned lines must be *spread across* the copy with plain prose between them. Four eloquent lines back to back read as "trying too hard to sound impressive… a staple of bad political speeches." The announcer gets loud near the goal, not for the whole game.
- **The clarity-before-music check (AN-3).** Read each tuned line as a skeptic in a hurry. If they have to re-read to parse it, sound beat clarity — and a confused buyer doesn't buy. Lesson one (clear) before lesson two (memorable). Rebuild plain, then re-add the music.
- **The "that was well said" test (Q6).** Would a reader *notice* the technique, or just feel that the line was true and certain and move toward the button? If they'd notice the device, it failed — bury it harder or cut it. The reader should feel wisdom, not see scaffolding.
- **The honesty re-check (AN-2 + spine).** Confirm every tuned line still says something *true*. A device makes a true line ring; it must never be the reason a false or unsubstantiated line *sounds* convincing. If the chiasmus made an empty claim feel airtight, that's the failure mode to kill on sight.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **VSL (long-form video sales letter)** | The piece that can bear all four devices — it's long, with plenty of plain runway between turns. Chiasmus at the objection block (read aloud, so the closed loop *sounds* inevitable on the ear). Epistrophe on the belief, restated across acts so it's still ringing at the pitch. Two Languages at the proof-to-desire pivot. Saxon close on the spoken CTA. Spread them across acts — never two tuned lines in the same 30 seconds. |
| **Landing page** | Scannable, so devices land on the lines the eye *stops* on: the headline (Saxon close or Two Languages), the objection-handling section (chiasmus), the final CTA button (Saxon punch). Usually two or three devices total — a landing page has less plain runway than a VSL, so the machinery shows faster. Epistrophe works across section subheads if they're already near each other. |
| **Email** | Tightest format — often *one* device per email. Pick the single conversion turn this email exists to make: objection-killer email → chiasmus; belief-building email → epistrophe; the close email → Saxon CTA. Stacking devices in a 150-word email is the fastest way to sound like a rhetorical robot. One device, buried, in service of one job. |
| **Social hook into copy** (LinkedIn/X → DM/link) | The hook's last line is a Saxon punch (stop the scroll), or a chiasmus reframe that *feels* wise enough to earn the click. One device only — at hook length the machinery shows in a heartbeat. The body copy then does its own (separate) tuning pass. |
| **Tagline / brand line on the page** | Wants the chiasmus closed-loop or a punch-only Saxon line — the "wisdom illusion" that makes it feel inevitable rather than written. Short, hard words. This is the one place a near-pure device is correct, because the line *is* the moment. |
| **Sales-page subhead / section opener** | Two Languages: name the section's promise conceptually (so the skimmer's logical brain knows what's coming), then the gut version (so they keep reading). Keeps the skeptic and the dreamer both moving down the page. |

## Output Format

Deliver exactly this:

```
COPY ASSET: __________ (VSL / landing / email)
CORE BELIEF (one word): __________   ·   #1 OBJECTION: __________
HONESTY SPINE: all four tuned lines say true, substantiated things [confirmed]

CONVERSION-TURN MAP (devices land ONLY here):
  • OBJECTION moment      → CHIASMUS
  • CORE-BELIEF moment    → EPISTROPHE
  • LOGIC+GUT moment      → TWO LANGUAGES
  • CTA / OFFER moment    → SAXON CLOSE
  (Everything else stays plain — that's the floor that makes these audible.)

— 1 · CHIASMUS on the objection —
  BEFORE: "[the line as it stands — usually argues]"
  AFTER (direct):     "[A] doesn't dictate [B]; [B] dictates [A]."
  AFTER (conceptual): "[the conversational, non-repeating version]"
  Why it converts: shuts the objection — arguing against it feels like arguing against gravity.

— 2 · EPISTROPHE on the core belief —
  BEFORE: "[the spine, stated once, forgettable]"
  AFTER:  "… ____ [word].  … ____ [word].  … ____ [word]."  (three beats, lands on "____")
  Why it converts: the belief is still ringing at the CTA.

— 3 · TWO LANGUAGES on the logic+gut beat —
  BEFORE: "[one register only — logic OR gut]"
  AFTER:  "[Latinate justification.] [Saxon gut-punch.]"
  Why it converts: logic signs off, then the gut buys — in that order.

— 4 · SAXON CLOSE on the CTA —
  BEFORE: "[soft Latinate button — 'transform/unlock/discover']"
  AFTER:  "[brief conceptual value-run]— [Saxon punch]."   Button: "[the punch]"
  Why it converts: the do-it word lands like a slammed door and rings after they stop reading.

WEAVE CHECK: tuned lines spread across the copy, plain prose between, one device each [confirmed]
RECOMMENDED PRIORITY: install #__ first — because __________
```

### Worked example A — VSL for a productivity system

**Core belief (one word):** *work*. · **#1 objection:** "I've bought systems before and nothing stuck."

**1 · Chiasmus on the objection**
> BEFORE: "Other systems failed you because they were too complicated, but ours is different because it's simple and we have testimonials proving it works."
> AFTER (conceptual): "The system was never the thing that failed you. You don't need a better system. You need to do the work the system keeps you from doing."
> *Why it converts:* it doesn't argue with the past failures — it inverts the frame so the reader's own objection ("systems don't stick") becomes the proof of *your* point ("the system was never the lever — the work was"). Closed loop; nothing to push back on.

**2 · Epistrophe on the core belief**
> AFTER: "You can plan the work. You can optimize around the work. But at some point, somebody has to do the work."
> *Why it converts:* three clauses, each landing on *work*; by the CTA the reader has internalized that the system is not the point — the work is — which is the exact belief that makes them stop shopping for the next tool.

**4 · Saxon close on the CTA**
> BEFORE: "Click below to start transforming your productivity today."
> AFTER: "You've seen the whole architecture of how this runs. Now—stop planning. Work." Button: **Start the work**
> *Why it converts:* the Latinate *architecture* lulls; *stop planning. Work.* drops the floor out on two stony syllables. *Work* — the same word the epistrophe has been ringing all page — is the last thing in the reader's ear before the button. Spine and punch agree.

### Worked example B — Email close for a premium coaching offer

**#1 objection:** "It's too expensive." · **Logic+gut beat:** why the price *is* the value.

**1 · Chiasmus on the objection**
> BEFORE: "I know it seems pricey, but when you consider the ROI and everything you get, it's actually a great deal."
> AFTER (direct): "The price isn't high because the work is good. The work is good because we refuse to make it cheap."
> *Why it converts:* the reader walks in thinking *price reflects value* and the line crosses it to *standards reflect price* — arguing against it means arguing for cheap work, which no serious buyer wants to be caught doing.

**3 · Two Languages on the logic+gut beat**
> AFTER: "You're not paying for the hours. You're paying for the years it took me to know which hour matters—so you stop wasting yours."
> *Why it converts:* the conceptual half ("paying for the years of expertise") gives the logical brain its justification; the gut half ("so you stop wasting yours") lands the desire — *time back* — in short, picture-able words. Agree, then ache.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Rhetorical Pattern Precision**, **Wisdom Resonance**, **Dual Appeal (Two Languages)**, **Economy of Impact**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Devices on payload only (AN-2):** every device sits on a *conversion turn* — an objection, the core belief, the logic/gut pivot, the CTA. None on connective filler. Remove any device and that line must get *worse* at converting; if it doesn't, it was decoration — cut it.
- **One device per moment (AN-1):** no line is doing two devices at once. The chiasmus line isn't also the epistrophe anchor and the Saxon close. If a reader would *notice* the technique, it failed — they should feel certain, not impressed.
- **Devices spread, not stacked:** the tuned lines are distributed across the copy with plain prose between them. Four eloquent lines in a row reads as a bad political speech. The announcer gets loud near the goal, not the whole game.
- **Chiasmus closes the loop (Pattern 3):** the objection-handler *inverts the frame* rather than *arguing* with counter-evidence. Arguing against the finished line should feel like arguing against gravity. Conceptual (non-archaic) version preferred for modern copy.
- **Epistrophe is three, not five (AN-6 / Pattern 7):** the must-linger belief lands on the same word across two-to-three clauses, no more. Past three, switch one to the front (anaphora) and snap back, or trim.
- **Two Languages is two *registers* (Pattern 4):** the logic+gut beat states the idea conceptually *then* viscerally — not twice in the same register. Logic signs off, then the gut buys; the reader should *agree, then ache*.
- **CTA lands Saxon (Pattern 1, AN-4):** the close ends on a short, hard, picture-able Germanic word, built backwards from that word, with a brief conceptual run to drop *from*. No soft Latinate button ("transform/unlock/elevate"). The value-run resolves into the punch or it's pomp.
- **Clarity before music (AN-3):** every tuned line parses on first read for a skeptic in a hurry. A confused buyer doesn't buy. Plain first, music second.
- **Honesty spine intact:** every tuned line says something *true and substantiated*. No device is the reason an empty or unproven claim *sounds* airtight. Ward tunes the ear; he never manufactures the belief — that's the copywriter's claim and the proof's job.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Carpet-bombing the page with devices (AN-2 / AN-1).** Treating every sentence as a candidate for eloquence, so the whole page reads as over-engineered and the *actual* conversion lines get lost in the noise — and the reader's emphasis-detector burns out before the CTA. **Recovery:** re-run Step 1. Mark at most one moment per device (often fewer for short formats). Everything else stays deliberately plain; the plain prose is the silence that makes the tuned lines audible.
- **Arguing the objection instead of inverting it.** Stacking counter-evidence at the objection ("but actually, here's why it's worth it, and here are five testimonials") invites the reader to keep score and find the hole. **Recovery:** use chiasmus (Step 2) — don't beat the objection, *cross the frame* so the objection becomes the proof of your point. A closed loop admits no scorekeeping.
- **The CTA floats off on a Latinate word (AN-4).** "Start transforming your potential today" — the most important line on the page, ending on the softest possible word. **Recovery:** Step 5, build backwards. Decide the Saxon punch word first (*work / start / yours / build*), run the value conceptually just before it, drop the close on the stony word. The button text *is* the punch.
- **A device making an unproven claim sound true (honesty-spine breach).** A flawless chiasmus that closes the loop on a benefit the offer can't actually deliver — the *most* dangerous failure, because the better the rhetoric the more convincing the lie. **Recovery:** before tuning any line, confirm the claim under it is substantiated; if it isn't, that's a copy-engine / luke-iha problem (get a real proof point or a real mechanism), not a Ward problem. Devices ring true lines; they must never gild empty ones.
- **The four tuned lines clump together (the "demo reel" failure).** Stacking the chiasmus, epistrophe, Two Languages, and Saxon close in the same paragraph because that's where the "important part" is — so the prose suddenly announces itself and reads as trying too hard. **Recovery:** Step 6 weave-check. Spread the turns across the asset, plain prose between. The reader should hit one well-said line, settle back into plain copy, then hit the next — not four eloquent lines in a fistful.
