# The Apprenticeship Loop (Ward Farnsworth)
Every other workflow in this skill produces a line. This one produces the *ear* that makes those lines findable. Farnsworth is explicit that the instinct cannot be installed by rules — "Lincoln learned how to write like Lincoln by reading the Bible, by reading Shakespeare... thousands of hours of immersion" — and equally explicit about the classroom method that accelerates it: deliberate imitation. "I have students rewrite modern Supreme Court opinions as Oliver Wendell Holmes would have written them... If you want to imitate somebody's writing, you've really got to understand it. You've got to listen." Then the crucial final move: "eventually your imitations get better — and then you throw away the imitations." You keep the learning, never the costume, because "anybody who imitates anybody sounds like a fool." This is influence built on purpose: soak, transcribe, x-ray, imitate, discard.

## Input Required
- **The writer's domain and native voice** — what they write (essays, copy, legal, LinkedIn, scripts) and a sample of their current prose (sets the finishing check: the loop must end in *their* voice, improved)
- **A master candidate, if they have one** — or none; Step 1 selects. The criterion is Farnsworth's: someone "worth apprenticing yourself to," whose lines have survived the tournament of time
- **Time budget** — minutes per day they will actually spend (the loop scales from 10 minutes/day to a full study practice; honesty here beats ambition)
- **One modern text from their own world** — a memo, a post, an opinion, an article in their field; this becomes the imitation exercise in Step 4

## Workflow

### Step 1: Choose the master (the tournament test)
Pick one writer — not five — worth apprenticing to. Farnsworth's named bench: **Lincoln** (plainness with total structural sophistication — "nobody thinks of Lincoln as pompous... but look at all the patterns and artfulness"), **Holmes** ("the best writer American law has ever had"), **Churchill**, the **King James Bible** translators. The selection test is durability: "time is kind of a tournament," and a line still quoted a century on has proven it works on the naked ear. Match master to the writer's lane:

| Writer's lane | Master that maps | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Persuasion, argument, law, ops memos | Holmes | Latinate build → Saxon verdict; authority without pomp |
| Plain-style essays, posts, public writing | Lincoln | The proof that plain and sophisticated are not opposites |
| Speeches, manifestos, rallying copy | Churchill | The full repetition toolkit in its native habitat |
| Gravity, vows, moral weight | King James Bible | The sustained Saxon run; profundity in humble words |

One master at a time. Two masters at once trains neither ear.

### Step 2: Set the counter-immersion diet
Immersion is already happening — the feed is "a campus where rhetoric is taught by bad example." The diet replaces default soak with chosen soak. Prescribe a concrete, small regimen the time budget survives: a fixed short session (even 10 minutes) reading the master — Farnsworth built his books as "browsable reference": "you can spend 10 minutes with it and you'll be 10 minutes better off." No note-taking required at this stage; the goal is exposure — "if you read good stuff, it can gently influence what your ear thinks is an attractive way to say something."

### Step 3: Transcribe and x-ray (the daily rep)
From each session, pick **one line that struck you** and:
1. **Write it out by hand — never copy-paste.** "As you write out the quote, you begin to see what's going on in the writing in a way that you can't just by reading."
2. **X-ray it**: name what is under the surface — the device(s), the register run (where Latinate, where Saxon), the last word and why it is last. Use the Pattern 1–11 catalog as the vocabulary.
3. **Say the why in one sentence.** "Anybody can look at good writing and say 'that's impressive.' The question is: can you understand *why*?"

| Line (transcribed by hand) | Device(s) under it | Register run | Last word — why it's last |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

One line per day, fully x-rayed, beats ten lines admired.

### Step 4: The imitation exercise (walk up to AN-5 on purpose)
Weekly, not daily. Take the modern text from the writer's own world and **rewrite it as the master would have written it** — the Farnsworth classroom move. This is not pastiche for shipping; it is forced listening: to imitate Holmes you must decide, sentence by sentence, what Holmes would cut, where he would place the verdict word, when he would go Saxon. The exercise makes the master's decisions *conscious*. Grade the imitation only on fidelity: would a reader who knows the master recognize the signatures? (Here — and only here — sounding like the master is the success condition.)

### Step 5: Throw the imitation away — return in native voice
The discard is the step that separates influence from costume. Take the same modern text and rewrite it once more, **in the writer's own voice**, keeping only the *decisions* the imitation surfaced — the earlier end-weight, the cut qualifiers, the one chord change — and none of the master's cadence. "Writing sounds best when you're basically speaking the words onto the page... if you force your writing to follow somebody else's style, it's never going to sound right." Compare against the writer's original sample: the delta should be instinct, not accent.

### Step 6: Cycle and rotate
Repeat Steps 2–5 as the standing practice. Rotate masters only after the current one's signatures show up uninvited in native-voice drafts (that is the graduation signal, usually months, not days). Log one x-rayed line per session; the log *is* the curriculum.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Writer type | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Copywriter** | Imitation exercises on real control copy: rewrite a winning ad as Lincoln (plain + structured). The x-ray habit doubles as swipe-file study — but x-ray *structure*, not hooks. |
| **Ghostwriter** | Run the loop on the *client's* voice as a second track: transcribe and x-ray the client's best lines with the same rigor as the master's. The devices must eventually read as theirs (see genius.md Cross-Domain: Ghostwriting). |
| **Exec / leadership comms** | Holmes and Lincoln over Churchill — verdict sentences, not rally cries. Imitation text: their own last all-hands memo. |
| **Social writer** | Shortest loop: one transcribed line per day from the master, one native-voice post per week that applies a single surfaced decision. The feed is the counter-immersion problem at its worst; the diet matters more than the exercise. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` — AN-5 is the entire failure surface of this workflow.
- **The imitation was discarded.** Step 5 produced a native-voice pass; nothing shipped or kept sounds like the master. Influence, never imitation — "anybody who imitates anybody sounds like a fool."
- **Transcription was by hand.** Copy-paste x-rays do not count; the seeing happens in the writing-out.
- **The x-ray names mechanics, not vibes.** Every logged line has device, register run, and last-word reasoning — "that was well said" is the reader's job, not the apprentice's.
- **One master at a time.** No composite curriculum; the ear trains on a single voice per cycle.
- **The regimen fits the stated time budget.** A 10-minute plan actually followed beats an hour-a-day plan abandoned — the loop compounds only if it runs.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Keeping the imitation because it sounds impressive.** The Holmes-flavored rewrite often reads "better" than the native draft — grander, more cadenced. Shipping it is AN-5 wearing a medal. *Recovery*: the imitation is scaffolding; run Step 5 and keep only decisions. If the native pass is weaker, the surfaced decisions weren't transferred — redo the discard pass, don't reinstate the costume.
- **Collecting quotes instead of x-raying them.** A swipe file of admired lines with no named mechanics is a museum, not training. *Recovery*: enforce the three x-ray fields per line; if you can't fill them, that line is tomorrow's rep, not today's trophy.
- **Rotating masters too fast.** A week of Lincoln then a week of Churchill trains nothing — immersion works by accumulated hours in one voice. *Recovery*: hold one master until their instincts appear uninvited in your drafts; that signal, not boredom, triggers rotation.
- **Treating the loop as a deliverable engine.** Someone asks for a hook and gets assigned a reading diet. *Recovery*: the Pre-Flight Gate — chronic flatness gets the loop; today's draft gets `/ward-rhetorical-engine`.
