# The Cadence Audit (Ward Farnsworth)
A draft can be clear, correct, and still tire the ear — every sentence the same length, every clause the same weight, the whole page one even shade of gray. "If everything is loud, nothing is loud." This is a diagnostic pass over prose already written: it walks the draft sentence by sentence to find *where* the music goes flat, names *why* (no chord change, no short sentence to land, the strong word buried mid-line), and then re-tunes only those places — installing the contrast that lets a line punch and the short Saxon finish that makes it ring. You diagnose the rhythm before you touch it. The chord, not the change, is what you fix.

AN-3 is the guard rail: lesson one (concise, clear, an allergy to wasted words) comes *before* lesson two (memorable). If the draft is not yet clear — if a cold reader has to re-read a sentence to parse it — **do not tune rhythm yet**. A line that sounds great and confuses has failed lesson one and cannot be saved by lesson two. Fix clarity first, then audit the music.

## Input Required
- The draft in full — essay, post, page, email, speech, script (the whole thing, not an excerpt; rhythm is a property of the *run* of sentences, not any one)
- The format and channel it ships on (sets the tolerance — a tagline lives or dies on one chord change; an essay can breathe across paragraphs — see Adaptations)
- Read-aloud permission: this audit is performed **out loud** (or sub-vocally). Rhythm is heard, not seen. A line that scans fine on the page can drone in the ear.
- The one or two sentences that carry the thesis, if known — those are where a chord change must land, and where flatness costs the most
- Any line you already love and would fight to keep (it gets the same scan as the rest; "I like it" is not a rhythm)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Read it aloud and mark the sentence-length map
Read the draft out loud at the reader's pace. Break it into sentences and, for each, record only two cheap measurements first: **word count** and a one-glyph **rhythm mark**. You are drawing the shape of the prose before you judge any single line.

| # | Sentence (first 4–5 words …) | Words | Rhythm mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | L / M / S |
| 2 | | | |
| 3 | | | |
| … | | | |

- **L** (long) = ~25+ words, or multi-clause. **M** (medium) = ~12–24. **S** (short) = ≤11, often a single stony beat.
- Then read the marks as a *column*: `L L L L L` or `M M M M M` is the danger sign — an even shade with no short sentence to land against. The ear wants a long build *broken* by a short punch: `L L M S`. Churchill builds across the conceptual stretch, then drops to "so few." The variety is the rhythm; the monotony is the flaw.
- Flag any run of **3+ sentences at the same length** as a SUSPECT STRETCH. That is where the page goes gray. Most flat drafts are flat because every sentence came out the same size.

### Step 2: Score the Saxon/Latinate ratio per sentence (find the color)
Now go sentence by sentence and judge its *color* — is this line built of short, hard, picture-able **Saxon** words (get, hit, fall, fear, hate, few), or long, conceptual, multi-syllable **Latinate** words (acquire, accumulate, demonstrate, conflict, determination)? Mark each sentence's dominant register. This is the diagnostic underneath the chord change: you cannot install a chord change in a draft that is **all one color**.

| # | Dominant register | Heaviest Latinate words in it | Could the final word be Saxon? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | SAXON / LATINATE / MIXED | | YES / NO |
| 2 | | | |
| … | | | |

Read the register column the way you read the length column:
- **All LATINATE** = pomp with no payoff (**AN-4**). Fancy, even, insecure — "acquire" where "get" would do. There is no Saxon to punch *toward*, so nothing lands. The fix is not more Latinate; it is a short, stony word at the end of the key sentence.
- **All SAXON** = the copywriting-guru flatline. Blunt, short, monotonous — and the genius.md warning applies in reverse: with no Latinate to build tension, the short words have nothing to *shatter*. A page of all-Saxon is as gray as a page of all-Latinate. You need the build to earn the punch.
- **MIXED, well-ordered** = the chord change is available. Latinate front, Saxon back. This is what you are tuning *toward*.

### Step 3: End-weight check — is the strongest word last?
For every sentence that *matters* (thesis lines, section closers, the last line of the piece), run the end-weight test from Pattern 8: **the end of a sentence is the most powerful real estate in English** — is the word you most want the reader to carry actually sitting there, or is it buried in the middle with weak words trailing after it?

| # (key lines only) | Last word as written | The word that *should* land last | Trailing dead weight to cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |
| | | | |

- If the strongest word is mid-sentence and the line dribbles out on a preposition, a qualifier, or an "in order to do so," the punch is wasted. "Freedom for the thought that we hate" works because *hate* is built to land last; move it and the line dies.
- The fix is structural, not additive: **start at the end, work backward.** Decide the final word, then build the sentence so that word arrives last and lands clean — usually short, hard, Saxon.
- Watch for the **trailing tail**: long sentences that reach their real point and then keep going for another clause. That extra clause is the dead weight after the punch. Cut it; let the strong word ring into silence.

### Step 4: Locate the flat stretches and prescribe the chord change
Cross the three maps — length, register, end-weight — and the flat zones declare themselves. A SUSPECT STRETCH from Step 1 that is *also* all-one-color from Step 2 is a confirmed dead patch: no variety in size, no variety in shade, nothing for the ear to catch on. This is "all one color." Prescribe the smallest tuning move that restores contrast — and prescribe by *cause*, the way the audit-shaped engines work:

| Flat-stretch cause | What it looks like | Re-tuning move |
|---|---|---|
| **No short sentence to land** (all L/M) | A run of long builds with nothing to break it | Find the most important point in the run; cut it down to one short Saxon sentence and place it *after* the longest build. The build earns the punch. |
| **All one color — no chord change** (all Latinate) | Conceptual stretch that never resolves into stony words (AN-4) | Keep the Latinate *build* (it sets the tension); rewrite only the final clause in one-syllable, hard-consonant words so the line shatters at the end. |
| **All one color — no build** (all Saxon) | Blunt, even, monotone short sentences | Don't add a punch — there's nothing to punch toward. *Combine* two or three short sentences into one longer build so a single later short sentence can land against it. |
| **Buried end-weight** | Strong word mid-line, dead tail trailing | Rebuild the sentence backward from its strongest word (Step 3). Cut the trailing clause. |
| **Two Languages missing** (thesis line lands once, in one register) | A pivotal idea stated only conceptually *or* only viscerally | State it twice: once Latinate (for the head), then immediately again in short Saxon words (for the gut) — Lincoln's "dissolved … divided." Use sparingly, on the thesis, not everywhere. |

The discipline of the audit: **prefer the smallest tuning that restores the contrast.** You are not rewriting the draft. You are finding the three or four places where the ear goes flat and installing a chord change there — and leaving every clean line alone. One well-placed short sentence does more than ten rewritten ones.

### Step 5: Re-tune the flagged lines, then re-read aloud cold
Write the tuned version of *only* the flagged stretches. Then read the whole draft aloud once more, cold, and confirm three things:
1. The length column now has **variety** — the long builds break to short punches; no surviving run of 3+ same-length sentences in the high-stakes zones.
2. The key sentences now **land on their strongest word**, and it is usually short and hard.
3. **No chord change was over-installed.** This is the failure mode of rhythm work: if *every* sentence now ends on a Saxon punch, you have a new monotony — the punch itself becomes the even shade (AN-6, in spirit). The announcer gets loud *near the goal*, not for the whole game. Some sentences must simply move cleanly so the tuned ones ring.

If a tuned line now sounds *constructed* — if a cold reader would notice the technique rather than just feel "that was well said" — it has failed (AN-1). Bury the device deeper or revert the line to plain. Clarity (AN-3) is never sacrificed for sound: a line that reads beautifully and confuses is a regression, not a fix.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Social hook** (LinkedIn / X) | The chord change is the whole game and you get one. Audit the *first* and *last* line above all. Build the hook on a slightly longer, conceptual line, then drop the last line to one short Saxon punch. At this length a single flat line is a third of the post — and a single buried end-weight kills the scroll-stop. One chord change per post; more and the machinery shows. |
| **Headline** | Almost pure end-weight. The last word *is* the headline. Run Step 3 only: is the strongest, shortest, most concrete word last? "Acquire customers" → "win customers" → land on the Saxon. No room for a build, so the whole headline must point at its final word. |
| **Converting copy** (VSL / landing / email) | Audit the rhythm of the *run*, not the single line — copy fatigues when every claim is the same medium-length sentence (`M M M M M`, the most common copy flatline). Break claim-runs with short punches; reserve the chord change for the CTA line so it rings after they stop reading. The Two Languages move earns its keep here: state the offer conceptually, then again in gut-Saxon. |
| **Tagline / brand line** | One sentence, maximum end-weight and maximum chord change. Build it backward from the last word; that word should be short, hard, and quotable. The contrast (one conceptual word setting up one stony word) is what lends it the "hue of wisdom." If it's all Latinate it sounds corporate; if it's all Saxon it sounds like everyone else's. |
| **Essay line / Substack** | Rhythm operates across *paragraphs*, not just sentences. Flatness clusters mid-paragraph and after the best line (the reader coasts). Audit the **last sentence of each paragraph** for a chord change and the **opening of each section** for length variety. An essay can sustain longer Latinate builds than copy can — but each must break to a short Saxon line before the reader's ear glazes. |
| **Speech / manifesto** | The native habitat of the chord change — read *entirely* aloud at speaking pace, where every flat stretch is audible. Audit for the build-then-break: the long anaphoric run of conceptual sentences that *must* land on the short Saxon climax ("…so much owed by so many to so few"). The flaw to hunt is the build with no payoff — pomp that never drops to the stone. |

```
DRAFT: __________  |  FORMAT/CHANNEL: __________
THESIS LINE(S) (where a chord change must land): __________

  1. [L/M/S] (__ words) — "first words…"
  2. …
  SUSPECT STRETCHES (3+ same length): sentences __–__  [L L L L], sentences __–__ [M M M]

REGISTER MAP (the color):
  1. [SAXON / LATINATE / MIXED] — heaviest Latinate: "____"
  2. …
  ALL-ONE-COLOR ZONES: sentences __–__ (all LATINATE — AN-4 pomp, no payoff)

END-WEIGHT CHECK (key lines only):
  • #__ : ends on "____" → should land on "____"  | cut trailing: "____"

FLAT-STRETCH DIAGNOSIS (length × register × end-weight):
  • Sentences __–__ — cause: [no short to land / all one color / buried end-weight / Two Languages missing]
  • …

RE-TUNED PASS (flagged lines only — rest left alone):
  • #__  BEFORE: "____"
         AFTER:  "____"   (move: chord change / short Saxon finish / rebuilt end-weight)
  • …

COLD RE-READ CONFIRMATION:
  • Length column now varies (no surviving 3+ same-length run in high-stakes zones): [yes / no]
  • Key lines land on their strongest word: [yes / no — which still buried]
  • Chord change NOT over-installed (not every line ends on a punch): [confirmed / over-tuned at __]
  • Clarity preserved (AN-3 — no line traded sense for sound): [confirmed / fix __]
```

**Worked example 1 — the all-Latinate flatline (no chord change):**
> BEFORE: *"In order to accumulate a sustainable competitive advantage, organizations must continually demonstrate their commitment to differentiated value propositions."*
> Diagnosis: 19 words, all-LATINATE, register column dead even — pomp with no payoff (AN-4). The strong idea ("win, and keep winning") is nowhere; nothing lands.
> AFTER: *"You don't accumulate an edge by talking about value. You earn it. Then you keep it."*
> The first sentence holds the Latinate build ("accumulate," "value"); the line then breaks to two short Saxon punches. "Earn it" and "keep it" are one syllable each and land last. The chord changes; the ear catches.

**Worked example 2 — buried end-weight (the trailing tail):**
> BEFORE: *"What we are really defending, when you strip away the slogans and the noise of the moment, is the freedom to think the thoughts that the people in power happen to hate the most, in the end."*
> Diagnosis: the strongest word — *hate* — is buried, and the sentence dribbles out on "the most, in the end." The punch is wasted; dead weight trails after it.
> AFTER: *"Strip away the slogans. What we defend is freedom — freedom for the thought that we hate."*
> Rebuilt backward from *hate*. The trailing tail is cut. A short opener ("Strip away the slogans") breaks the length, the epistrophe on *freedom* sets the build, and the line lands on the stony Saxon word and rings into silence.

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Ward Would Never… (Anti-Patterns)` + `§ Expert-Specific Quality Rubric` (esp. *Impact Velocity*, *Economy of Impact*, *Structural Priming & Flow*). Flag and fix before delivering.
- **The audit was performed aloud.** Rhythm is heard, not seen — a silent scan does not count. If the length and register columns weren't read as columns, the flat stretches weren't found.
- **Clarity was checked before music (AN-3).** No tuned line traded sense for sound. A line that sounds great and forces a re-read is a regression; lesson one before lesson two, always.
- **The fix is the chord change, not the chord.** Diagnosis names *contrast missing* (length monotony, one-color register, buried end-weight) — not vague "make it punchier." Each re-tune installs the specific contrast the stretch lacks.
- **No new monotony was created.** The draft does not now end *every* sentence on a Saxon punch. Over-installed punches are a new even shade — if everything is loud, nothing is loud. Some lines must simply move cleanly.
- **No Latinate left with no payoff (AN-4).** Every conceptual build that was flagged now resolves into a short, stony, picture-able word — or was simplified entirely. No tension built without a release.
- **The tuning is surgical.** Clean lines were left alone. This is a re-tune of named flat points, not a rewrite — the draft's argument, meaning, and voice are preserved.
- **The machinery does not show (AN-1).** No tuned line reads as constructed; nowhere does the output label its own moves ("here I added a chord change"). The reader should feel "that was well said," not see the scaffold.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Auditing with the eyes, not the ear.** A sentence can scan fine on the page and drone aloud; you cannot see monotony, you can only hear it. *Recovery*: read the whole draft out loud at the reader's pace — the flat stretches announce themselves the moment you stop scanning and start listening.
- **"Fixing" flatness by adding more Latinate.** The instinct on a gray, important paragraph is to reach for bigger, more impressive words — which doubles the pomp and the flatness (AN-4). *Recovery*: the cure for all-one-color is *contrast*, and contrast usually means a shorter, plainer, harder word — not a fancier one. When you really know what you mean, you can land it in Saxon.
- **Over-tuning until every sentence punches.** Installing a chord change in every line just relocates the monotony: a page where every sentence ends on a stony Saxon beat is as even as one where none do. *Recovery*: tune only the high-stakes stretches; let the connective sentences move cleanly so the tuned ones ring. The announcer is loud near the goal, not the whole game.
- **Tuning rhythm on a draft that isn't yet clear.** Polishing the music of a sentence the reader has to re-read to understand is fixing lesson two while lesson one is broken (AN-3). *Recovery*: stop the audit, rebuild the unclear lines plainly first, confirm a cold reader parses them on the first pass, *then* return and tune the cadence.
