# Metaphor-as-Argument — Metaphor That Persuades, Not Decorates (Ward Farnsworth)

Lincoln did not say "the Union is in a state of internal contradiction the founders intended to resolve over time." He said *a house divided against itself cannot stand* — and the whole argument arrived in a picture you can see before you can dispute it. That is the move: a metaphor is the Saxon, visceral *restatement* of an abstract claim — you metabolize Saxon words instantly, you *feel* them, and a true metaphor does the same to a whole idea. The reader doesn't decode it; their gut closes the case before their head opens it. This workflow takes one intellectually-clear-but-emotionally-inert claim and finds the single concrete image that carries it — then ranks 2-3 candidates by how much of the argument each one actually loads, never by which sounds prettiest.

## Pre-Flight

- **Pattern 4 — The "Two Languages" Translation** — the engine: state the idea conceptually, then restate it visceral and street-level. Metaphor is the *most compressed* version of the second register — one picture instead of a paraphrase.
- **Pattern 1 — The Saxon Punch (The "Chord Change")** — *why* the picture lands: short, hard, picture-able words hit; conceptual abstraction floats. The metaphor must resolve into things the body can see and feel.
- **How to Use This Skill (Opus Calibration)** — restraint over saturation; the device must feel native, not assembled; do not label the metaphor by name.
- **Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-4 (the picture must pay off the argument, not ornament)**, **AN-2 (rhetoric on a trivial line)**, **AN-3 (breaking clarity for sound)**.
- **Decision Framework** — the gate below.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before executing. Specifically confirm **Q1** (this is the *thesis* claim, not ornament — a metaphor wrapped around a throwaway line spends attention on nothing), **Q2** (the soil is *translation* — you have an abstraction the reader understands but does not yet *feel*; this is the Two-Languages occasion, not chiasmus or epistrophe), and **Q4** (there is a real chord change — the metaphor exists to drop the reader out of the conceptual register into the visceral one). If the copy is unclear rather than inert, stop — a metaphor cannot rescue a claim the writer hasn't thought through. Fix the thinking first; a fuzzy idea makes a fuzzy picture.

## Input Required

- **The abstract claim** — the single conceptual point that is intellectually clear but emotionally flat. One plain sentence ("our onboarding loses users at every handoff," "discipline compounds," "the market is irrational short-term"). This is the *Latinate half* already in hand.
- **What the reader must FEEL, not just know** — the gut verdict the picture should deliver: dread, relief, urgency, contempt, inevitability, scale. One word. This is the persuasive load the metaphor has to carry.
- **The reader's domain / lived experience** — what they touch, fear, and recognize day-to-day. The strongest metaphor borrows from *their* world, not yours (a surgeon and a sailor need different pictures for the same idea).
- **Format** — social hook, headline, converting-copy line, tagline, essay line, speech. (Drives length and how much the image can unfold.)
- **Anything you're already in love with** (optional) — a metaphor you've drafted, so we can pressure-test it against the load instead of defending it.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Pin the abstract claim and the felt verdict (the Latinate half + the target)

Write the claim in its plainest conceptual form — the version a smart consultant would say and a reader would nod at and forget. Then name, in one word, the feeling the reader currently is *not* having. The gap between those two is exactly what the metaphor must close.

```
ABSTRACT CLAIM (the Latinate half, already clear): __________
WHAT THE READER MUST FEEL (and currently doesn't): __________
```

This is the diagnosis from Pattern 4: the idea is *known* but not *felt*. A metaphor is the only Two-Languages move compressed enough to move the whole idea from head to gut in one image — not a paraphrase, a *picture*.

### Step 2 — Extract the load-bearing relationship (what the metaphor must actually carry)

A metaphor decorates if it copies the *subject*; it argues if it copies the *structure*. Before reaching for any image, name the abstract relationship inside the claim — the thing that must survive translation. The picture has to reproduce *that*, not just gesture at the topic.

| In the abstract claim, what is the load-bearing… | Name it plainly |
|---|---|
| **Relationship** (what acts on what — erodes, compounds, leaks, blocks, severs, devours) | |
| **Hidden cost or consequence** the reader underestimates | |
| **The thing the reader should DO or fear** as a result | |

Whatever sits in these rows is the *argument*. A candidate metaphor that does not carry all three is pretty, not persuasive — it will get cut in Step 4. (Lincoln's "house divided" carries the relationship *self-opposition*, the cost *collapse*, and the imperative *resolve it now* — all three, in four words.)

### Step 3 — Generate 3-5 candidate pictures from the reader's world (the Saxon half)

Now restate the claim as a concrete image — and write several, fast, ugly ones included. The first metaphor is almost always the dead one (the journey, the battle, the iceberg). The true one is usually the third or fourth, and it is usually borrowed from something the *reader* already touches.

Pull images from these wells, ranked by how visceral they tend to run:

| Image well | Why it lands | Example domain |
|---|---|---|
| **The body** (bleed, choke, scar, ache, starve) | The reader feels it before they read it — the most Saxon well there is | "the funnel bleeds users at every step" |
| **Physical labor & tools** (saw, anchor, rope fraying, load-bearing wall) | Concrete, weighty, picture-able; nothing abstract survives here | "you're sanding a beam that's already cracked" |
| **The reader's own trade** (surgeon, sailor, builder, coder, parent) | Borrowed authority — it sounds like *their* truth, not your cleverness | match to the input domain |
| **Money & survival** (debt, interest, a leak below the waterline) | Hits the loss-aversion gut directly | "compounding is interest the lazy pay" |
| **Time & decay** (rust, rot, erosion, a fuse) | Makes a slow abstract cost feel urgent and physical | "the skill rusts faster than you think" |

Write each candidate as a *single* picture — one image, fully load-bearing. Resist the urge to extend it into a paragraph; one true picture beats five half-pictures (see the restraint note in How to Use This Skill).

```
CANDIDATE PICTURES (Saxon / visceral restatements of the same claim):
  1. __________
  2. __________
  3. __________
  4. __________
  5. __________
```

### Step 4 — Rank by persuasive load, NOT prettiness (the whole point of this workflow)

This is the step everyone skips and the reason most metaphors decorate. Score each candidate against the load you named in Step 2 — does the picture *carry the argument*, or does it just sound nice sitting near it? The prettiest candidate is rarely the heaviest. Choose the heaviest.

| Candidate | Carries the **relationship**? (Step 2) | Delivers the **felt verdict**? (Step 1) | Borrowed from the **reader's world**? | One picture, picture-able? (no abstraction smuggled in) | **Load score /10** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. | | | | | |
| 2. | | | | | |
| 3. | | | | | |

The "carries the relationship" column is the veto column — a candidate that fails it scores low no matter how lovely it reads (**AN-4**: the metaphor must pay off the argument, not ornament). The test, in Farnsworth's terms: if you deleted the abstract claim entirely, would the picture *alone* still make the argument? A load-bearing metaphor does. A decorative one collapses without the sentence it was leaning on.

### Step 5 — Set the metaphor as the Two-Languages drop, then read it cold (AN-3 guard)

The metaphor rarely stands fully alone in copy; it is the *second* half of a chord change. Place the abstract claim (Latinate, conceptual) first if context needs it, then drop into the picture (Saxon, visceral) — or lead with the picture and let the claim land after. Then read the whole thing aloud, cold, as a first-time reader.

- **The metabolize test:** does the reader *get* it before they analyze it? A true metaphor is understood instantly — "you metabolize Saxon words instantly, you feel them." If the reader has to work out the comparison, it's a puzzle, not a picture; rebuild it more concrete.
- **The chord-change test (Pattern 1):** is there an audible drop from the conceptual register into the picture? If the claim and the metaphor are the same temperature, there's no translation happening — make the claim more conceptual or the picture more physical.
- **The clarity test (AN-3):** lesson one (clear) before lesson two (memorable). If the metaphor is *clever* but a first-time reader misreads what it means, sound beat clarity — cut to the plainer picture. A metaphor that confuses has already failed; no amount of beauty rescues it.
- **The restraint test:** one picture, not five. If you find yourself extending the metaphor across three sentences (the "house" becomes the foundation becomes the roof becomes the mortgage), you are decorating. Cut back to the single image that carries the load.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social hook (LinkedIn / X)** | The picture IS the hook — lead with it, let the abstract claim land underneath. You have one image's worth of runway; pick the highest-load candidate and drop the rest. At this length a second metaphor reads as flailing. |
| **Headline** | Compress to the picture alone — the metaphor *is* the headline, the claim is the article. Lincoln's "house divided" is a headline that contains a thesis. Borrow from the reader's world so it reads as recognition, not wordplay. |
| **Converting copy (VSL / landing / email)** | Set it as a true Two-Languages drop: state the mechanism conceptually so the logical brain signs off, then restate it as the picture so the gut buys. The metaphor often carries the *problem* (the leak, the bleed, the rusted lock) so the offer can be the fix. One controlling picture per section. |
| **Tagline / brand line** | The picture-only version, hardened — a metaphor compressed until it has the "hue of wisdom" and feels inevitable. It must carry the brand's actual claim, not a generic uplift image. If it could sit on any brand, it carries nothing. |
| **Essay / Substack line** | The most runway — you can state the abstraction, then drop into the picture as the line readers quote back. Still one image: resist the literary temptation to develop the metaphor over a paragraph. The single picture that lands once beats the extended conceit that announces itself. |
| **Speech / keynote** | Native habitat for the controlling image — one metaphor the whole talk returns to (Lincoln's house, MLK's "promissory note marked insufficient funds"). Land it early in concrete words, let the room *see* it, then refer back without re-explaining. The picture does the arguing; you don't gloss it. |

## Output Format

Deliver exactly this:

```
ABSTRACT CLAIM (the Latinate half): __________
FELT VERDICT TARGET (head → gut): __________
LOAD-BEARING RELATIONSHIP the metaphor must carry: __________

CANDIDATE PICTURES (ranked by persuasive load, not prettiness):
  1. "[picture]" — load __/10 · carries: [relationship ✓/✗] · feels: [verdict ✓/✗]
  2. "[picture]" — load __/10 · ...
  3. "[picture]" — load __/10 · ...

WINNER: "__________"
  • It carries the argument because: [if you deleted the claim, the picture alone still makes it]
  • Set as the Two-Languages drop: [claim, conceptual] → [picture, visceral]

CUT (and why the prettier ones lost): "______" — sounds good, carries no load.
```

### Worked example A — Converting copy (a leaky onboarding funnel)

**Abstract claim:** *our multi-step onboarding loses a percentage of users at every handoff between screens.* · **Felt verdict target:** *alarm — this is bleeding money right now.* · **Load-bearing relationship:** *steady, compounding loss at each junction the reader can't see.*

> **Two Languages:** *sequential attrition across onboarding handoffs* → **the funnel bleeds.**

**Candidates, ranked:**
1. "Your onboarding is a funnel with a hole at every joint — it bleeds users at every handoff." — **load 9/10.** Carries the *loss-at-each-junction* relationship exactly (every joint = every handoff), delivers alarm (*bleeds*), borrowed from the body. Picture-able, single image.
2. "Onboarding is a journey, and some users don't finish the trip." — **load 2/10.** *Journey* is the dead first metaphor — it carries no loss, no junction, no urgency. Pretty-adjacent, argues nothing. **Cut.**
3. "Onboarding is a bucket with the tap left running." — **load 6/10.** Decent loss image, but a bucket loses from the bottom, not at *joints* — it misses the per-handoff structure that is the actual argument.

**Winner:** *"Your onboarding bleeds users at every handoff."*
*Why it carries:* delete the analytics claim and the line still argues — *bleeds* names the loss, *every handoff* names where, the body-well delivers the alarm. The reader sees the wound before they ask for the data.

### Worked example B — Essay line (on neglected skill)

**Abstract claim:** *a high-level skill degrades surprisingly fast when it isn't practiced.* · **Felt verdict target:** *quiet dread — it's happening to me already.* · **Load-bearing relationship:** *invisible, passive decay that's faster than the owner assumes.*

> **Two Languages:** *unpracticed competence undergoes accelerated degradation* → **the skill rusts.**

**Winner:** *"A skill you don't use doesn't wait for you. It rusts — and rust is always faster than you think."*
*Why it carries:* *rust* is concrete, Saxon, picture-able, and reproduces the exact relationship — passive, invisible, faster-than-assumed decay — that the abstract claim only described. One picture, no extension. Delete the first clause and "it rusts, faster than you think" still makes the whole argument.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Dual Appeal (Two Languages)**, **Wisdom Resonance (Structure)**, **Economy of Impact**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **The metaphor pays off the argument (AN-4):** delete the abstract claim — does the picture *alone* still make the case? If it collapses without the sentence it leans on, it's ornament. Rebuild from the load-bearing relationship (Step 2), not the topic.
- **It carries the named relationship, not just the subject:** the winning picture reproduces the *structure* of the claim (the erosion, the bleed, the compounding), not merely its theme. A metaphor that copies the subject decorates; one that copies the relationship argues.
- **It's one concrete picture, metabolized instantly (Pattern 4 / Saxon):** the reader *sees* and *feels* it on first read without decoding. Picture-able, Saxon, single image — not a paraphrase, not a puzzle.
- **Chord change is real (Pattern 1):** there's an audible drop from the conceptual claim into the visceral picture. If both halves are the same temperature, no translation happened — sharpen the contrast.
- **Clarity before cleverness (AN-3):** read cold, a first-time reader takes the intended meaning on the first pass. A clever metaphor that misreads has failed; cut to the plainer picture.
- **One picture, not five (restraint):** the metaphor is not extended into a strained conceit across multiple sentences. If the image keeps spawning sub-images, it's announcing itself — cut to the single load-bearing one.
- **Borrowed from the reader's world where possible:** the strongest candidate reads as *their* recognition, not the writer's display. Generic library metaphors (journey, battle, iceberg) score low by default — they carry no specific load.
- **Ranked by load, not prettiness:** the delivered winner is the heaviest candidate, and the cut candidates are named with *why* they lost. A ranking that crowned the prettiest line skipped Step 4.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Decorating instead of arguing.** Reaching for an image that *matches the topic* (a "growth" piece gets a seedling, a "speed" piece gets a rocket) without checking whether it carries the claim's actual relationship. **Recovery:** run Step 2 first — name the load-bearing relationship, then find the picture that reproduces *that*. If you can delete the claim and the picture still argues, it's load-bearing; if it collapses, it was wallpaper.
- **Crowning the prettiest candidate.** The most elegant-sounding metaphor is rarely the heaviest; mistaking music for load is exactly how copy ends up "intellectually clear but emotionally inert." **Recovery:** re-score on the Step 4 table with the *carries-the-relationship* column as a veto. Choose the heaviest even when a prettier one is right there.
- **The dead first metaphor.** Stopping on the journey / battle / iceberg / tip-of-the-spear image — the one every writer reaches for, which the reader has gone numb to. **Recovery:** keep generating (Step 3) to candidate four or five, and pull from the reader's specific trade or the body. The live one is usually borrowed, concrete, and slightly unexpected.
- **Extending the metaphor into a conceit.** One good picture becomes a paragraph — the house gets a foundation, a roof, a mortgage, a leaky basement — until the machinery is showing and the reader is admiring the cleverness instead of feeling the point (AN-1 / restraint). **Recovery:** cut back to the single controlling image. One picture, fully load-bearing, beats five half-pictures every time.
