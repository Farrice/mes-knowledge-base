# Aphorism Forge (Ward Farnsworth)
"Freedom for the thought that we hate" is eight words because every other version was longer and weaker — Holmes built it backwards from *hate*, the one word he wanted left hanging in the air, and let the sentence assemble in front of it. Compression is not trimming; it is deciding the last word first and dismantling everything that does not earn its place before it. This workflow takes a claim drowning in qualifiers and finds the line that survives the talk — then it does the thing most "make it punchy" tools never do: it tells you honestly whether the line is *true*, or merely *beautifully said*. The balance lends a sentence "the hue of wisdom" whether or not the idea inside it is sound; that is the sword and the shield, and you do not hand someone the sword without the shield.

## Input Required
- **The claim, raw** — the worthy idea exactly as it currently exists: the bloated sentence, the paragraph that buries it, the talk section you want to leave with one line. Paste the too-many-words version verbatim; do not pre-trim it.
- **What you actually mean by it** — one plain-English gloss. The compression must keep *this*, not whatever the original words drifted into.
- **Where the line will live** — pull-quote, social hook, headline, tagline, maxim, the closing line of a talk. (Drives Content Type Adaptations and the length ceiling.)
- **Is it a truth or a stance?** — your honest read on whether the claim is *demonstrably true*, *probably true*, or *a rhetorical position you are taking*. This seeds the shield note; the forge will pressure-test it, but start honest.
- **Any word you will fight to keep** — a proper noun, a brand term, a load-bearing concept word. (Constrains the candidates; flagged if it fights the compression.)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Find the payload — what is the one claim, stripped of qualifiers?
Before you compress *words*, isolate the *idea*. A quotable line carries exactly one claim; the reason the original is too long is almost always that it is carrying two or three. Strike every hedge ("often," "I think," "in many cases"), every dependent clause, every example, until one assertion is left standing naked. If two assertions survive, you have two lines, not one — pick the one that carries the thesis (Decision Framework Q1) and set the other aside.

> Run Q1 here: if removing all the rhetoric leaves a sentence that did its job fine, the claim was never worth a maxim — it was working copy. Maxims are reserved for the line that carries the weight.

### Step 2: Read the soil — which device wants to grow here?
This is Pattern 10, and it is the front door. Do not admire devices in the abstract and reach for whichever sounds grand. Diagnose the *intent* of the bare claim, then pick the device the occasion grows. Most claims grow exactly one device cleanly; if you find yourself wanting two, the line is doing two jobs (return to Step 1).

| If the bare claim is doing this... | The soil grows... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Saying *they have it backwards* / "and vice versa" / give-and-take | **Chiasmus (ABBA)** | The crisscross reversal feels closed-loop and admits no objection; it is the shape of "you have this inverted." |
| Drawing a hard line between two things — *this, not that* | **Antithesis** | Two ideas set against each other in parallel; the contrast is the meaning. |
| Landing a single thesis as a punch, must ring after they stop reading | **Saxon close** (end-weight) | Build with conceptual words, end on the short, hard, picture-able Saxon one. The chord change *is* the impact. |
| A series, a list, a sweep that wants completeness | **Rule of three** | Three parallel beats land as "rhetorically perfect" — balanced, nothing to add or cut. |
| The claim is true but flat and abstract | **Two Languages** | State it once conceptual, once visceral — logic then gut, in one breath. |

Name the device and the *why* in one sentence ("this is a backwards-thinking claim → chiasmus"). If you cannot name *why this device here*, you are decorating, not deploying — go back to the table.

### Step 3: Decide the last word, then build backwards
Pattern 8 is the engine of the whole forge. The end of an English sentence is its most powerful real estate; the last word is the one the reader carries away. So decide it *before* you write anything else. "Always start at the end. Work backwards from the end of the sentence."

1. **Pick the last word.** Usually short, hard, Saxon, picture-able — *hate, few, end, gone, win, dirt*. It should be the word you would want quoted back to you. (For chiasmus/antithesis the "last word" is the second half's pivot term — the thing the loop snaps shut on.)
2. **Build the sentence in front of it.** Assemble the rest so that word arrives last and lands clean. Move every other candidate word *off* the end; if the strongest word is not last, the sentence is wired wrong.
3. **Set the chord change.** If the device is a Saxon close, the front of the line should carry the conceptual / Latinate weight that the final Saxon word shatters. "If everything is loud, nothing is loud" — a blunt line with no setup has no punch to land. The Latinate builds the tension; the Saxon breaks it.

### Step 4: Forge three candidates — one device, sparingly
Write **three** distinct compressions of the same bare claim. Vary the *approach*, not by stacking more machinery, but by trying the line at different lengths or with the device tuned harder or softer. The rule that governs all three: **one device per line.** A line doing chiasmus *and* epistrophe *and* a Saxon punch at once has its machinery showing — "if they notice the technique, it's bad technique" (AN-1). If a candidate needs two devices to work, it is not compressed enough yet.

Pressure each candidate against three tests as you write it:
- **Clarity first (AN-3).** Read it cold. If a first-time reader has to re-read to parse it, you chose the prettier loop over the clearer statement — Farnsworth's lesson one (clear) comes *before* lesson two (memorable). Rebuild plainly, then add the music back.
- **Earn the device (AN-2).** Remove the device mentally. If the sentence still did its job, the device was decoration — either the line is too trivial for rhetoric or the device is in the wrong place.
- **Conceptual is fine, perfect is not required (Pattern 9).** If a literal ABBA reads archaic, get the *feeling* of the closed loop by inverting the ideas, not the exact words. The line should "ring" with balance even if no two words are identical.

Tune length to the venue: a tagline wants ≤7 words, a social closer ≤12, a maxim or essay line can run to a tight 15. Past that you have a sentence, not a line people repeat.

### Step 5: Run the Wisdom-Illusion Shield — true, or just well-said?
This is the step that separates the forge from every "make it punchy" tool, and it is non-optional. The balanced form you just built *grants the hue of wisdom whether or not the idea is sound* — "when a sentence maps to the math of the universe, the brain assumes the idea inside it is also an undeniable truth." That is the shield Farnsworth teaches: the same structure that makes a true thing land harder makes a false thing land *just as hard*. So for each finalist, label honestly what the rhetoric is doing for the reader.

For each of the three candidates, fill the shield:

| Candidate | The claim it asserts | TRUE / LIKELY / STANCE | What the rhetoric is doing | Shield note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | | | (does the balance *prove* the claim, or just *dress* it?) | |
| B | | | | |
| C | | | | |

- **TRUE** — the claim is demonstrably so; the rhetoric earns the wisdom-hue honestly. Ship without a caveat.
- **LIKELY** — defensible but not proven; the form is doing some persuading the evidence has not earned. Note it.
- **STANCE** — a rhetorical position, not a fact. The line is *beautifully said*, and that beauty is doing work the truth-value cannot back. **This is exactly the line to flag**, not to suppress — Farnsworth's inoculation is to *know* when a sentence is closing the reader's critical defenses by its shape alone. Deliver it with the note attached so the user wields it knowingly.

The shield note never blocks delivery. It labels. The whole point of the sword-and-shield ethic is that you hand over the sharpened line *with* the honest tag, never one without the other.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Social hook / closer** (LinkedIn / X) | One device, ≤12 words, the Saxon punch or the chiasmus reframe — at this length the machinery shows in a heartbeat. The chiasmus "you have it backwards" stops the scroll because it *feels* wise enough to argue with. Put the decided last word at the literal end of the post. |
| **Headline** | End the headline on the short, hard, concrete word (end-weight). Or run Two Languages — the concept once for the curiosity-click reader, once Saxon for the gut. Never two devices; a headline is too short to hide a second one. |
| **Converting copy** (pull-quote / CTA line) | The CTA wants the Saxon close so it rings after they stop reading; the objection-handling pull-quote wants chiasmus ("arguing against gravity"). Run the shield hard here — a STANCE dressed as TRUTH in sales copy is exactly the line that converts *and* the one that will be quoted back at you. Flag it so the user ships it knowingly. |
| **Tagline / brand line** | The native habitat of the wisdom illusion. Chiasmus or a clean Saxon close, ≤7 words, closed-loop, admits no objection. The structure lends the line the hue of inevitability — but a tagline is a STANCE almost by definition (it is a promise, not a proven fact), so the shield note will usually read STANCE; that is correct, not a failure. |
| **Essay line / maxim** | Up to ~15 words; the line the reader underlines and the essay is remembered for. Rule of three earns its place here — the tricolon that closes a section. The maxim must be *true* (TRUE/LIKELY), because a reader who later finds the underlined line was merely well-said distrusts the whole essay. |
| **Speech closer** | The line that survives the talk — the one quoted in the hallway after. Saxon close on the decided last word, built backwards, sparingly. The announcer gets loud near the goal, not the whole game: this is the single loud line, set up by plain ones before it. |

**Worked example 1 — a thesis claim, Saxon close (end-weight)**

> **Raw (too many words):** "The most important thing to understand is that the people who end up writing your story for you are precisely the people whose voices you spent your whole life trying not to upset or disappoint."
>
> **Bare claim:** the voices you appease are the ones that end up authoring your life.
> **Soil → device:** a single thesis that must land like a punch → **Saxon close**.
> **Decided last word:** *yours* → no; *win* → no; the picture-word is *pen*. Build backwards from "pen."
>
> - **A.** "The voices you never wanted to disappoint are the ones holding the pen." *(STANCE-leaning LIKELY — the chord change from conceptual "disappoint" to the hard image "pen" does the persuading; defensible, not proven.)*
> - **B.** "Appease every voice, and one of them writes your life." *(Saxon close on "life"; tighter, blunter.)*
> - **C.** "You spend years not upsetting them. They spend it authoring you." *(Two-beat antithesis — but note "authoring" is Latinate at the end, weakening the punch; A and B land harder.)*
>
> **Why the example works:** A decides the last word *first* (the concrete, monosyllabic *pen*) and assembles the conceptual setup ("never wanted to disappoint") in front of it — the Latinate builds the tension the Saxon image breaks. Eight-words-or-so, one device, clarity intact. The shield is honest: the line *feels* like proven wisdom, but it is a stance about agency, so it ships LIKELY with the note attached.

**Worked example 2 — a backwards-thinking claim, chiasmus**

> **Raw (too many words):** "Coaches tend to assume that if they could just get an athlete strong enough, the discipline would naturally follow — but in our experience it actually works the other way around, where the discipline is what produces the strength in the first place."
>
> **Bare claim:** discipline produces strength, not the reverse — *and vice versa* is in the air, so the soil grows a chiasmus (Pattern 10's tell).
> **Soil → device:** "they have it backwards / and vice versa" → **chiasmus (ABBA)**.
> **Decided pivot word:** the loop snaps shut on *strong*.
>
> - **A.** "Strength does not build discipline. Discipline builds strength." *(Clean ABBA — strength/discipline, discipline/strength. The closed loop grants the hue of wisdom.)*
> - **B.** "You don't get disciplined by getting strong. You get strong by getting disciplined." *(Conceptual chiasmus — Pattern 9 — the same loop in plainer, more conversational words; rings without literal symmetry.)*
> - **C.** "Discipline is not the reward for strength. It is the price of it." *(Antithesis instead — "not X, it is Y" — a different soil; offered as the runner-up if the venue wants a line less obviously balanced.)*
>
> **Why the example works:** A is a literal A-B-B-A whose balance circumvents the reader's critical defense — it *feels* true because it *sounds* closed. The shield catches exactly this: the claim is LIKELY (defensible in S&C, not a law of physics), and the perfect form is doing some of the persuading the evidence has not. It ships with that note, never as bare TRUTH.

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review all three candidates against `genius.md § Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` + § Decision Framework + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.

- **One device per line (AN-1).** Count the devices firing in each finalist. More than one → the machinery is showing; cut to the strongest. A line is not *more* quotable for stacking chiasmus on a Saxon punch — it is less.
- **The line carries a thesis (AN-2).** If removing the device leaves a sentence that did its job fine, the claim was too trivial for a maxim. Rhetoric on a throwaway line trains the reader to ignore your emphasis.
- **Clarity survived the compression (AN-3).** Each candidate read cold parses on the first pass. A line that sounds great and confuses fails lesson one and cannot be rescued by lesson two — rebuild it plainly, then re-add the music.
- **The chord change lands (AN-4).** Saxon-close candidates resolve a conceptual setup into a short, hard, picture-able final word. Latinate-with-no-payoff ("authoring you") is tension with no release — either land the punch or simplify the whole line.
- **Influence, not imitation (AN-5).** No candidate reads as costume Churchill / King James pastiche. It sounds like *this* claim in *this* voice, said more memorably — not a speech impersonation.
- **Built backwards (Pattern 8).** The decided last word is genuinely last and genuinely the strongest; moving any other word to the end would weaken the line. If the punch word is buried mid-sentence, the line is wired wrong.
- **The shield is filled and honest (Hidden Knowledge — Wisdom Illusion).** Every finalist has a TRUE / LIKELY / STANCE label and a note on what the rhetoric is doing. A STANCE labeled TRUE is the one failure this workflow exists to prevent — the sword without the shield.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Trimming words instead of deciding the last one.** Hacking adjectives off the bloated original shortens it but never makes it *ring* — you get a smaller flat sentence, not a maxim. **Recovery:** stop editing the original. Decide the last word cold (Pattern 8) and build a fresh line backwards from it; compression is reconstruction, not deletion.
- **Stacking devices to seem more impressive.** Cramming chiasmus + epistrophe + Saxon punch into one line so it sounds maximally "rhetorical" — which is the exact tell of trying too hard, "a staple of bad political speeches." **Recovery:** AN-1. Identify the one device that does the most work for *this* soil (Step 2) and cut the rest; the buried single pattern is the quotable one.
- **Shipping a STANCE as a TRUTH.** The line is so beautifully balanced it *feels* undeniable, so it goes out with no caveat — and the wisdom illusion has just closed the reader's critical defenses on a claim the evidence does not back. **Recovery:** Step 5 is mandatory, not optional. Label honestly; deliver the sword *with* the shield. A flagged STANCE is not a weaker deliverable — it is an honest one.
- **Choosing the prettier loop over the clearer claim.** Twisting the line into a perfect ABBA until a first-time reader has to re-read it to understand. **Recovery:** AN-3 — lesson one (clear) before lesson two (memorable). Restate the bare claim in the plainest possible words, confirm it parses cold, *then* add the device back without sacrificing the parse. If the device and clarity genuinely fight, clarity wins.
