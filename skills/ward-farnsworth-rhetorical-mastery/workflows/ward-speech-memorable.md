# Speech-Memorable — Talks, Manifestos & Keynotes That Stick (Ward Farnsworth)

Churchill did not say "we shall fight" four times because he loved the phrase. He said it so the ear would lock onto the rhythm — *beaches, landing grounds, fields and streets, hills* — and then he broke it: "we shall never surrender." The line everyone remembers is the one that *snapped the pattern* the repetition had built. That is what a speech is, mechanically: a drumbeat that primes the ear for the goal, a switch that keeps the drumbeat from becoming a tic, and a stony Saxon word dropped on the crucial line so it rings for decades. This is rhetoric's native genre — the one the devices were built for. Which is exactly why restraint is load-bearing here: the announcer gets loud *near the goal*, not for the whole game. Relentless repetition is the staple of bad political speeches. Deployed sparingly, it is the staple of immortal ones.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise and the restraint guard is the whole game):
- **Pattern 5 — The Anaphoric Drumbeat (Anaphora)** — the crescendo engine: repeat the front-words, let the climax land on whatever *breaks* the pattern.
- **Pattern 6 — The Switch (Anaphora ↔ Epistrophe)** — relief and return: run repetition in one position, shift it to the opposite, snap back. The switch is what keeps the device from becoming a gimmick.
- **Pattern 7 — The Rule of Three** — three beats is "rhetorically perfect"; two feels incomplete, four+ drags and announces itself.
- **Pattern 8 — Start at the End (End-Weight Engineering)** — decide the last word of every crucial line first; build backwards to it.
- **Cross-Domain Applications** — the "Speeches / manifestos / keynotes" row: the native habitat, full toolkit, still sparingly.
- **How to Use This Skill (Opus Calibration)** — restraint over saturation; influence not imitation; never label by device.
- **Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-6 (relentless repetition)** and **AN-1 (device-stacking)**, the two that kill speeches first.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before executing. Specifically confirm Q1 (the lines you architect carry the *thesis/stakes*, not ornament — you are not drumbeating filler), Q3 (you have decided the last word of the climax and the close before writing them), Q5 (you are not stacking three devices on one line), and Q6 (would Farnsworth *notice the technique* — or just say "that was well said"?). If the speech has no single goal the crescendo is driving toward, do not architect rhetoric yet — find the one thing this talk is *for*. A speech with no goal line has nothing for the announcer to get loud about.

## Input Required

- **The one goal of the talk** — the single line the whole speech exists to land. If you cannot name it in one sentence, the speech is not ready to architect; it is still a topic. ("We shall never surrender" is a goal; "thoughts on the war" is a topic.)
- **The stakes / the enemy** — what is at risk, or what we are fighting. The drumbeat needs something to beat *against*.
- **The audience and the occasion** — who is in the room, why now. (Rally? Eulogy? Product keynote? Founder manifesto? The occasion sets how hot the announcer is allowed to get.)
- **The native voice** — whose voice is this in? (Yours, a client's, a founder's.) The eloquence must sound like *this* speaker, never like a Churchill costume. Paste 2–3 real lines of theirs if ghostwriting.
- **Existing draft** (optional) — if sharpening rather than writing from scratch, the current text. We will find the crescendo it is reaching for and cut the repetition it is over-using.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Name the goal line and decide its last word FIRST (Pattern 8)

Before any architecture, find the *one* line the speech is driving toward — the goal the announcer gets loud about. Then decide the final word that line ends on, because in a speech the last word of the climax is the word the room carries out the door.

Strip the goal to its hardest, most picture-able final word and audit it. A speech climax that ends on a long, soft, Latinate word has no punch to land — the crowd has nothing to chant.

| The goal line's last word should be… | Why it matters in a *room* | Pass / Fail |
|---|---|---|
| **One syllable, hard-consonant, Saxon** | The room can *say it back*; stony words echo, soft words evaporate | *surrender, free, dead, win, fight* — not *capitulate, liberty, deceased, prevail* |
| **The true end of the meaning** | End-weight wasted on a filler word kills the climax | Move any other word to the end — is it weaker? It must be |
| **Chant-able / repeatable** | A great speech line gets *repeated* after; design for the echo | Could a stranger quote it tomorrow from one hearing? |

```
THE GOAL LINE (rough): __________
FINAL WORD the room carries out: __________
Felt verdict it should leave: __________ (defiance / resolve / grief / wonder / hope)
```

### Step 2 — Build the anaphora drumbeat that primes the goal (Pattern 5 + Pattern 7)

Now build the *runway* to the goal. Pick the phrase that carries the stakes and open consecutive units with it, verbatim, varying only what follows. This is the announcer getting louder as the player nears the line. Default to **three** beats (Pattern 7) — three is "rhetorically perfect"; the fourth is where AN-6 begins.

The repeated front-words become the rhythm the ear locks onto. The climax does *not* repeat the phrase — it **breaks** it. That break is where the goal lands hardest, because the pattern set it up.

| Beat | Repeated opening (verbatim) | What varies after it (rising specificity) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | (e.g. *We shall fight…*) | (…on the beaches) |
| 2 | (e.g. *We shall fight…*) | (…on the landing grounds) |
| 3 | (e.g. *We shall fight…*) | (…in the fields and in the streets) |
| **BREAK** | — *the phrase changes* — | **the goal line from Step 1** (e.g. *we shall never surrender*) |

Rules of the drumbeat:
- **Three beats, then break.** If you wrote four or five, you are no longer priming — you are nagging. Cut to three (AN-6).
- **The break is the goal line**, ending on the Step-1 word. The reader has internalized the rhythm; snapping it is the percussion.
- **Rising stakes across the beats.** Each beat should be *bigger* than the last (beaches → landing grounds → streets → hills), so the climb is felt, not just heard.

### Step 3 — Add the switch so the device never becomes a tic (Pattern 6)

One anaphora run is a drumbeat. A *second* run in the same position, later in the speech, is a tic — the ear starts to feel "lay off" (AN-6). The fix is the **switch**: when you need repetition again, move it to the *opposite* position (start → end, i.e. anaphora → epistrophe), then let it snap back. The ear "got used to it, you stopped, you came back — and that creates satisfaction."

| Where in the speech | Repetition position | Example pattern |
|---|---|---|
| The build (Step 2) | **Start** (anaphora) | *We shall fight… / We shall fight… / We shall fight…* |
| The kicker / takedown | **End** (epistrophe) | *…giving all, daring all, enduring all* · *…government of the people, by the people, for the people* |
| The return | **Snap back** | a final line that brings the front-word home, or lands one Saxon close |

Use the switch specifically for **the kicker** — the memorable insult, the takedown, the rhetorically perfect closing turn. ("I knew Jack Kennedy. Jack Kennedy was a friend of mine. Senator, you're no Jack Kennedy" — *end, end, beginning, end*: the position keeps moving, which is why it survived.) Epistrophe is also the right tool for the **one belief that must linger** after the room empties: end three clauses on the single word you want chanted in the parking lot.

### Step 4 — Architect the arc: where the announcer is quiet, and where it is loud

A speech is not loud throughout — it is *mostly quiet*, with the rhetoric concentrated at the goals. Map the arc so the devices cluster only where the stakes peak. Everywhere else is plain, clear prose (lesson one before lesson two): the flat ground that makes the crescendo feel like a climb.

| Section | Announcer volume | What lives here |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Quiet | A plain, concrete first line. No device yet — earn the right to get loud. Possibly one Saxon close to set the stakes. |
| **Body / argument** | Quiet, mostly | The case, stated clearly. Maybe one Two-Languages translation (concept, then gut) on the pivot. Resist drumbeats here. |
| **The build** | Rising | The anaphora run from Step 2 — three beats, rising stakes. |
| **The climax** | Loudest | The broken pattern from Step 2, ending on the Step-1 Saxon word. The goal. |
| **The kicker / close** | One last hit, then quiet | The switch (Step 3) for the kicker, OR a single Saxon close. Then *stop*. Do not keep drumming after the goal is scored. |

The discipline: **no more than one device cluster per section, and most sections have none.** If every paragraph is doing rhetoric, the speech has no quiet ground for the crescendo to rise from — "if everything is loud, nothing is loud," and the whole talk reads as a bad political speech trying too hard to sound impressive.

### Step 5 — Saxon-close the crucial lines only (Pattern 8 + the chord change)

Walk the crucial lines — the goal, the kicker, the last line of each major section — and engineer the chord change on *those lines only*: a Latinate / conceptual run that sets up a short, hard, Saxon finish. Leave the connective prose alone; a speech where every sentence punches has no punch.

| Crucial line | Latinate setup (the conceptual runway) | Saxon drop (the finish) |
|---|---|---|
| The climax | (e.g. *Never in the field of human conflict…*) | (…*so much owed by so many to so few*) |
| The kicker | | |
| Section closer | | |

Reserve the Saxon punch for the lines that carry the thesis (Q1). On a throwaway transition it is AN-2 — it trains the room to ignore your emphasis when it finally matters.

### Step 6 — Strip to influence, not imitation; then read it ALOUD

Two final passes, both load-bearing for this genre:

1. **De-costume it (AN-5).** Read for any line that sounds like it is *trying to be* Churchill, Lincoln, or the King James Bible. Lincoln read Shakespeare and the Bible for years and never once *sounded* like he was imitating them. If a reader would think "this person is doing a Churchill impression," cut the pastiche and say it in *this* speaker's real voice. Eloquence in someone else's cadence "is never going to sound right."
2. **Read it aloud — the only test that counts.** A speech is heard, not read. Say the whole thing out loud, on its feet. Mark every place the repetition makes you wince ("lay off" — that is AN-6; switch or cut it). Mark every climax that *drifts* instead of *lands* (the last word is wrong — back to Step 1). Mark every line where you can *hear the machinery* (AN-1 — stacking; cut to the one device that does the most). The ear catches what the eye forgives.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Keynote / conference talk** | One goal line, one crescendo, one kicker — the whole talk drives at a single moment. The build (Step 2) lands ~80% through; everything before is the quiet climb. Open plain and concrete; do not deploy a device in the first 30 seconds — you have not earned the room's ear yet. |
| **Manifesto (written to be read aloud / chanted)** | The most device-dense format, but still governed by the rule of three and the switch. Anaphora for the "we believe / we refuse / we will" sections; epistrophe for the line meant to be *repeated* (the rallying word at the end of three clauses). Stacks UNDER `/manifesto` — that workflow owns the belief architecture; this one tunes its rhetoric so the lines stick. |
| **Rallying cry / call to action (short)** | Pure goal line. Often *just* the break — the Saxon close that would have ended a crescendo, deployed standalone. One device. ("We shall never surrender" works without the four "we shall fight"s when the room already feels the stakes.) |
| **Eulogy / occasion speech** | The announcer stays *low*. Grief is quiet. One epistrophe on the name or the virtue you want to linger; one Saxon close. Drumbeats read as theatrical in a room that is grieving — restraint is not optional, it is respect. |
| **Founder / product keynote** | The "enemy" is the status quo; the goal is the new world. Chiasmus for the "they have it backwards" reframe (route to `/ward-chiasmus`), then one anaphora build toward the vision, broken by the product as the goal line. Resist the temptation to drumbeat every feature — three beats, one break. |
| **Social-clip pull from a speech** | The line that gets clipped is almost always *the break* — the broken pattern or the Saxon close, lifted out. Design the climax so it *survives without its runway*: it must land cold, with no setup, because that is how it will travel. (Stacks with `/lulu-cheng` for the clip framing.) |

## Output Format

Deliver the architected speech (or the sharpened draft) PLUS a short build map so the speaker knows *where the announcer gets loud* — never label devices inside the speech text itself (the room must feel the wisdom, not see the scaffold).

```
SPEECH: __________ (occasion / audience)
THE ONE GOAL: "__________" — the line the whole talk drives at.
GOAL LINE'S LAST WORD: ______ (Saxon, chant-able, carries the felt verdict)

THE SPEECH (clean — no device labels in the body):
__________
__________
__________

—— BUILD MAP (for the speaker, not the audience) ——
• OPEN — quiet, plain, concrete. No device.
• BODY — clear case. One Two-Languages pivot at: "______"
• THE BUILD — anaphora, 3 beats, rising: "______ / ______ / ______"
• THE CLIMAX (goal) — pattern BREAKS here → "______" (lands on Saxon word: ___)
• THE KICKER — the switch (anaphora→epistrophe) at: "______"
• CLOSE — one Saxon punch, then STOP: "______"
RESTRAINT CHECK: device clusters = ___ (most sections have none) · longest repetition run = ___ beats (≤3)
```

**Worked example A — the build → break (anaphora climax):**

> *Before (a flat draft of a founder keynote close):*
> "We've fought hard to get here, against investors who doubted us, against a market that wasn't ready, against our own fear, and we're not going to stop now because the work matters and we believe in it."
>
> *After (the drumbeat primes, the break lands the goal):*
> "We fought when the investors doubted us. We fought when the market said *not yet*. We fought our own fear in the dark. **We will never go back.**"
>
> *Why it works (build map, not shown to the room):* three anaphora beats — *We fought… / We fought… / We fought…* — rising in stakes (outside doubt → market → the self in the dark). The pattern primes the ear. The climax **breaks** the repetition — "We will *never go back*" — and lands on *back*, one syllable, Saxon, chant-able. The single "we shall never surrender" move. Three beats, one break. No fourth. No second device stacked on it (AN-1, AN-6 both clear).

**Worked example B — the switch (anaphora → epistrophe kicker):**

> *Before (a manifesto line, all front-loaded repetition — relentless):*
> "We refuse the busywork. We refuse the vanity metrics. We refuse the endless meetings. We refuse the theater. We refuse to pretend it's working."
>
> *After (run the anaphora, then SWITCH the repetition to the end):*
> "We refuse the busywork. We refuse the vanity metrics. We refuse the theater. What we will not do is *pretend*. What we build, we *ship*. What we say, we *mean*."
>
> *Why it works:* the first three "We refuse" beats are the drumbeat (rule of three — the fourth and fifth in the *Before* were AN-6, "lay off"). Then the **switch**: repetition moves to the *end* — *pretend / ship / mean*, three epistrophe beats ending on hard Saxon words. The ear got the front pattern, lost it, and got it back in a new position — "you stopped, you came back, and that creates satisfaction." The kicker survives because the position *moved*, not because the phrase repeated.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review the speech against `genius.md § Ward Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering. For this genre, AN-6 and AN-1 are the first to break — check them hardest.

- **AN-6 (relentless repetition) — THE load-bearing check here.** No anaphora or epistrophe run exceeds **three** beats without a switch or break. Read aloud: any place the ear says "lay off" gets trimmed or switched. A speech that drumbeats relentlessly is "a staple of bad political speeches."
- **AN-1 (device-stacking).** No single line does anaphora *and* epistrophe *and* a Saxon punch at once. Count devices per line — more than one, cut to the strongest. The machinery must not show.
- **AN-2 (rhetoric on a trivial line).** The crescendo drives at the *goal*, not at filler. If you removed a device and the line still did its job, the device was decoration — cut it. Most sections have **no** device (Step 4 arc).
- **AN-4 (Latinate with no Saxon payoff).** Every chord-change line resolves into short, hard, picture-able words. No pomp that never lands. The climax and the close end on a Saxon word.
- **AN-5 (imitation not influence).** No line sounds like a Churchill/Lincoln/KJV impression. It is in *this speaker's* native voice. If a reader would smell costume, it is rewritten plain.
- **AN-3 (clarity broken for sound).** Read every line cold, once. A first-time *listener* (not reader) cannot rewind — if a line needs a second pass to parse, clarity lost to sound. Plain first, then music.
- **The goal line is the *break*, not a repeat** — the climax snaps the pattern the anaphora built; it does not continue it.
- **The arc breathes.** There is genuine quiet ground (plain prose) for the crescendo to rise from. If every paragraph is loud, the speech has no climax — only noise.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Drumbeating the whole speech.** The single most common failure in this genre: anaphora in every section, repetition that never lets up, the announcer screaming for the whole game. The room goes numb and the goal line lands on deaf ears. **Recovery:** strip rhetoric back to *one* cluster per crescendo; make 70%+ of the speech plain, clear prose. The drumbeat only works against silence (Step 4 arc; AN-6).
- **The climax repeats the pattern instead of breaking it.** Writing a fourth "we shall fight" as the finale — the ear was primed for a *snap*, and got more of the same, so nothing lands. **Recovery:** the goal line must *break* the anaphora and end on the Step-1 Saxon word. Three beats, then a different sentence (Step 2).
- **Costume eloquence (sounds like a Churchill cover band).** The speech reaches for grandeur by borrowing a dead statesman's cadence; it reads as pastiche, and the room feels the speaker performing rather than meaning it. **Recovery:** rewrite the purple lines in the speaker's own plain voice, then add *one* device where the stakes peak. Influence, not imitation (Step 6.1; AN-5).
- **The crucial line drifts instead of lands.** The climax ends on a soft, abstract, Latinate word (*…a sustainable future*), so there is nothing for the room to carry out or chant. **Recovery:** redo Step 1 — decide the hard, one-syllable Saxon final word first, then build the line backwards to it. If you cannot find a chant-able last word, the goal itself may be too abstract to be a speech climax.
