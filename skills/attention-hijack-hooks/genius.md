## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb them, then build originally. If the output labels a hook "Punchy + Context format" or narrates "here's the curiosity gap," you have failed — Escobar's own complaint is that most hooks read as "soft, vague, throat-clearing sentences" (00:01:03) precisely because the writer is explaining instead of hooking.

Specifically:
- Do NOT enumerate which format (Dense/Punchy+Context/Single-Line Bomb/Stacked/Hybrid) you used unless asked. Ship the hook; the operator can see the shape.
- Do NOT summarize the anchor ("Here's a post about X trending") — Escobar's whole thesis is that "the format is just packaging. The gap is the engine" (00:09:42). Summarizing the anchor produces packaging with no gap, which fails even a perfect format.
- Her texture is operator-blunt, not academic: "the part that kills me" (00:00:52), "here's the technical part we get wrong" (00:03:32), "here's why this is dangerous" (00:06:49). Match that directness — flag the risk, don't soften it into a framework slide.
- Polish is the tell-class failure here specifically: a hook that is too smooth to have an actual gap ("would this survive someone predicting the body from the first line?") is the single most common way this skill's output reads as AI rather than as an operator's judgment call.
- Would Diandra Escobar recognize this as a hook built the way she teaches it — signal locked, gap opened, format chosen last, judgment named? Or would she recognize it as someone reciting hook-format vocabulary without the underlying gap? If it's the second, rebuild before shipping.

## Core Genius

This system combines Diandra Escobar's attention-redirection philosophy with her 2026 hook-format study — 131 hooks pulled from 21 working creators "across SaaS, agency, e-com, B2B, content, AI" (02:36), explicitly excluding hooks from "2022" or even "2024" because, in her words, "what earns the click today looks different than what earned it even 12 months ago" (00:02:21). The useful leap is that brandjacking, newsjacking, namejacking, trendjacking, and draft rehooking are all the same underlying operation:

1. Find the signal that already has attention.
2. Attach it to a real payload.
3. Open a gap between expectation and claim.
4. Package that gap inside the first platform-visible window — for LinkedIn, Escobar cites "your first 40 to 50 words before it decides whether to push your post to anyone" (00:00:36).
5. Use human judgment to choose the hook that deserves amplification.

## Source-Backed Patterns

### Pattern 1: Dual Audience Hooking

Every hook serves two audiences: the human deciding whether to keep reading and the platform model deciding whether the post deserves distribution. Escobar names the LinkedIn model directly: "the new LinkedIn model called 360 Brew... specifically tracks whether people stop and read" (00:01:41). A strong hook cannot only be emotional; it also needs semantic signal, because "when your hook fails, two things die at the same time. Human curiosity and algorithmic reach" (00:01:54).

### Pattern 2: Attention Anchor First

Before generating hooks, identify the attention anchor: brand, person, news, trend, claim, number, belief, artifact, or body insight. If there is no anchor, the hook has to create all recognition from scratch. Escobar's own opening anchor is credential-based: "I've been writing LinkedIn hooks for 3 years... for clients, for founders paying us thousands a month to get this right" (00:00:00) — the anchor is the stakes, not a generic claim.

### Pattern 3: Gap Before Format

Dense, Punchy plus Context, Single-Line Bomb, Stacked, and Hybrid are packaging options. The engine is the gap between what the reader expects and what the post will claim: "Every one of these formats does the same psychological thing. It opens a gap between what the reader expects and what you are claiming. That gap is called curiosity. Curiosity is the click. The format is just packaging. The gap is the engine. If your hook doesn't open a gap, no format will save it" (00:09:25-00:09:50).

### Pattern 4: Pixel/Fold Reality

On LinkedIn, the practical first-screen budget is visual, not only character-based. Escobar is explicit: "LinkedIn doesn't render by characters, it renders by pixels" (00:03:38), and "the letter W takes up four times the visual space of the letter I" (00:03:45), which means "a 150 character limit doesn't actually exist. What exists is a pixel width budget, around 110 width units per line on mobile before the line wraps" (00:03:59-00:04:06). A Dense hook "fits a roughly three mobile lines, give or take" (00:04:17).

### Pattern 5: Context Creates Better Hooks

Do not ask for hooks from a thin topic label. Give the system the draft, source, offer, audience, and payload so it can identify hookable elements instead of inventing disconnected bait — the same discipline behind Escobar's own workflow of studying "131 hooks, five formats" pulled from real working creators (02:50) rather than inventing generic templates.

### Pattern 6: Judgment Over Automation

AI can generate stronger starting points, but the operator must choose, combine, cut, or rewrite based on audience, voice, and taste. Escobar's own framing of her Claude skill: "it won't replace your judgment. You know your audience. You know your voice. AI thinks inside the box. Your job is to think outside it. But for 80% of posts, it gets you a stronger starting point than a blank page ever will" (00:10:37-00:10:51).

## Hidden Knowledge

- A brand or news item is not the idea. It is the doorway into the idea — Escobar's framing is that a genuinely useful post has "real insights, real experience, and clear value" (00:00:56) but still fails if "their first line is always soft, vague, throat-clearing sentences that don't really do anything" (00:01:03).
- A hook that can be used by any creator is usually not specific enough for one creator.
- Punchy plus Context is the default workhorse — Escobar calls it "the format we see most and the one with the highest hit rate" (00:04:36) — but overuse turns it into wallpaper, and "the mistake we see constantly with this format is that the punchy line tries to do everything. It explains, summarizes, hedges. The punchy line should provoke. The context line earns the click" (00:05:24-00:05:34).
- Dense hooks work when context itself creates tension; they run roughly "three mobile lines" (00:04:17).
- Single-Line Bomb hooks should be rare: Escobar calls it "the riskiest format" (00:06:31) and states plainly, "we don't recommend this format unless you know in your gut that one line is an actual bomb" (00:06:59) — but "when it lands, it lands harder than any other format. It's the highest variance hook" (00:07:56-00:08:03).
- Stacked hooks fail when the lines are not part of the same series — "where stacked hooks die is when the lines are kind of random" (00:09:07).
- The safest universal workflow is body first, hook second, format third, platform check fourth — mirrored in Escobar's own sequencing advice for Hybrid: "master the four first, then start breaking the rules" (00:10:11).

## Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Score 4 | Score 7 | Score 10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signal Anchor | A recognizable source is named | The anchor has audience overlap and a clear reason to care | The anchor creates recognition, timeliness, and boomerang or debate potential |
| Payload Fit | The hook points to a general topic | The hook leads into a real argument or useful lesson | The hook makes the payload feel inevitable and worth reading now |
| Curiosity Gap | Mild intrigue | Clear expectation-versus-claim tension | Reader needs the next line because the gap hits a private belief |
| Format Choice | One format is used | Format matches the type of tension | Format, spacing, and rhythm make the hook feel native to the platform |
| Platform Fit | Roughly short enough | Survives first-screen constraints | Uses line breaks, width (~110 width units/line on mobile, 00:04:02), first-40-to-50-word signal, and mobile view deliberately |
| Judgment | Many options, no decision | Top choice with reason | Chosen hook plus rejected hooks teaches the operator how to improve future hooks |

## Anti-Patterns

1. **Summary bait**: naming a brand or event without a point of view. Escobar's own diagnosis of failed hooks: "their first line is always soft, vague, throat-clearing sentences that don't really do anything" (transcript 00:01:03-00:01:08, `Zc4E_K48v48`, published 2026-05-28).
2. **Generic hook formulas**: "Most people do X" without specific stakes — the opposite of Escobar's rule that the hook's one job with humans is "Get the click, not summarize, not explain, not set up the post" (transcript 00:01:26-00:01:32).
3. **Format cosplay**: using line breaks or punchy spacing while the idea stays weak. Directly contradicts the core thesis: "If your hook doesn't open a gap, no format will save it" (transcript 00:09:44-00:09:50).
4. **Disconnected bait**: hook promises a fight the body cannot pay off. Escobar's Punchy+Context warning names the mechanism: "The punchy line tries to do everything. It explains, summarizes, hedges. The punchy line should provoke. The context line earns the click" (transcript 00:05:27-00:05:34).
5. **Random stacking**: treating Stacked hooks as unrelated short lines instead of one organizing axis — "each line has to be a part of a series. If a reader can't predict the rhythm, the structure fails" (transcript 00:09:11-00:09:17).
6. **AI option dump**: many hooks with no scoring, no winner, and no taste call — the opposite of Escobar's own framing of the free Claude skill: "it won't replace your judgment... AI thinks inside the box. Your job is to think outside it" (transcript 00:10:37-00:10:43).
7. **Platform denial**: ignoring mobile fold, line breaks, first-50 signal, or channel norms — specifically the character-count myth Escobar corrects directly: "This is why most hook advice is wrong. They tell you a character count. The real answer is pixel width" (transcript 00:04:12-00:04:17).
