# Connelly Ghostwrite Economy — Dialogue & Voice Economy for a Client's Voice (Michael Connelly)

A ghostwritten draft in someone else's voice fails in a predictable way: it says too much. Every claim gets spelled out, every credential gets named, every feeling gets explained, and the whole thing settles into the smooth, generic register of "a professional being professional." It reads competent and it reads like no one. Connelly's 14 years of "give me six inches" taught him that the most over-explained copy is also the lowest-trust copy — and that *how much a person says, and what they leave to a nod, is itself characterization.* This pass takes a flabby draft in a client's voice and does four things, in order: cuts the dialogue/voice roughly in half while preserving every load-bearing fact (an information-preservation discipline, not a word massacre), defines the gap between what the client *has access to* and *why they feel they don't belong* — the tension that IS the voice — installs the nod-over-yes economy so the client's silences characterize them, and runs a two-layer subtext check on the handful of lines that have to persuade without a visible sell. Connelly supplies what to LEAVE OUT and the economy that makes a voice distinct; nicolas-cole fills the frame with sentence rhythm and structure, Connelly *implies* it. The non-negotiable: the finished cut must read as **the client's** voice, never the ghostwriter's. If it sounds like a beautiful piece of writing, it's wrong. It should sound like *them*, only sharper.

## Input Required

- **The draft as it stands** — the actual ghostwritten piece (LinkedIn post, email, About section, founder note, newsletter). Whole thing, not a summary. We are cutting specific lines, so we need the real lines.
- **The format** — LinkedIn post / email / About-page / founder note / newsletter (drives how aggressive the cut is and how much the voice can carry).
- **Whose voice it is** — the client. Not just their name: their actual credential/access, and the real reason they feel like an outsider in their own world (origin, temperament, a values conflict). If you don't have this, the access/belonging step has nothing true to build on — go get it before proceeding.
- **The information inventory** — the load-bearing facts/claims the piece exists to deliver. (This is the preservation checklist: the cut may delete *words and explanations*, never these.)
- **The persuade-without-selling lines** — the 1–3 lines where the piece has to move the reader toward a belief or action without sounding like a pitch. (These get the subtext check.)
- **Voice samples (if any)** — how the client actually talks: a voice note, a Slack message, a reply they wrote themselves. The cut should move the draft *toward* this, never toward "good writing."
- **Honesty status** — confirm the claims in the draft are already true/substantiated. We are tightening the voice, not manufacturing belief.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Build the information-preservation checklist BEFORE you cut a single word

The cardinal failure of this pass is cutting *information* when you meant to cut *words*. So you fix the target before you fire. Read the draft and extract the load-bearing payload — the facts, claims, and one or two true telling-details the piece exists to carry. Everything not on this list is fair game for the half-cut; everything on it must survive in some form (a fact can survive as a four-word fragment, but it must survive).

| Payload type | What it is | Preservation rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Load-bearing fact** | a real result, number, credential, event the piece needs | survives — may be compressed, never deleted; **honesty spine: must be true** |
| **The one telling detail** | a single concrete true particular that reveals the client (the thing they actually do, the specific moment) | survives, and gets *more* room — this is what the cut clears space for |
| **The persuasive beat** | the line(s) that move the reader toward a belief/action | survives, rewritten for subtext (Step 4) |
| **Explanation / setup / hedge** | the words spent *explaining* the fact, easing into it, qualifying it | **the half-cut's primary target** — this is the flab |
| **Throat-clearing / generic-professional** | "I'm excited to share…", "As someone deeply passionate about…", "In today's fast-paced world…" | delete on sight — zero payload, pure filler |

> **The preservation rule, stated hard:** at the end of this pass you will compare the information inventory before and after. Every load-bearing fact must still be present. If the cut lost a fact, you didn't compress — you amputated. That's Pitfall #2, the second-most-common failure of this workflow, and it's why we build the checklist *first*.

### Step 2 — The cut-in-half pass: halve the words, keep the payload (the "six inches" move)

Now cut. Connelly's rule, transferred straight from the newsroom and his own drafting habit: *as soon as you write it, go back and cut it in half.* Not trim — halve. The over-explained draft is carrying twice the words its payload needs, because the ghostwriter (reasonably) wanted to be clear and ended up being verbose. Run each sentence through three blades:

1. **Delete the explanation, keep the claim.** A claim followed by three sentences explaining the claim usually needs the claim and one. The reader infers the rest — and trusts what they infer more than what they're told.
2. **Collapse the setup.** "I want to talk about something that's been on my mind lately, which is…" → the something. Get in late, get out early. Start the sentence at the first word that carries weight.
3. **Cut the hedges and intensifiers.** "I genuinely, truly believe that this is really important" → "This matters." Every *genuinely / really / truly / actually / I think* is a hand on the reader's sleeve. Drop them.

Target: **~50% word reduction with 100% payload retention.** If you've cut less than a third, you haven't cut enough; the draft is still explaining itself. If you've lost a fact from the Step-1 list, you cut too deep in the wrong place — restore the fact in compressed form. Track the ratio; you'll report it.

> The economy is the point, not a side effect. A voice that says half as much, carrying the same payload, reads as *more confident* — the client who doesn't over-explain is the client who knows they're right. That confidence is itself characterization. (And it's the floor nicolas-cole then builds rhythm on: Connelly clears the half; Cole shapes what's left.)

### Step 3 — Define the access/belonging gap: the engine of the voice (Principle 4)

A halved draft that's merely tighter is still generic — clean, fast, and no one. What makes a voice *distinct* is the gap between what the client has access to and why they feel they don't belong there. This is Connelly's outsider engine applied to a real person: Bosch had a badge and a gun "but I wanted him to feel like an outsider." Your client has credentials and a seat at the table — and somewhere, a reason they feel one step outside it. That tension generates a point of view nobody else has, because nobody else has *their* gap.

Fill the worksheet from the client's *actual* life (honesty spine — invented gaps read as posture and collapse on contact):

| | Prompt | Example A — LinkedIn founder | Example B — coaching client (email) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ACCESS** (what they have) | credential, experience, inside knowledge, the seat they hold | 12 years building ops teams inside three scale-ups; runs a $4M agency | 15 years as an ER nurse; now coaches other nurses on burnout |
| **BELONGING GAP** (why they feel outside it) | origin, temperament, a values conflict, a thing they can't unsee | came up on the operator side, watches "founder" culture perform hustle it doesn't feel; distrusts the theater | left clinical work because the system grinds the people who care most; can't sit comfortably inside "wellness" language |
| **THE TENSION STATEMENT** | "Has [access] but [gap]." This is the voice parameter every line now answers to. | "Runs an agency but refuses to perform the founder hustle she watches everyone fake." | "Knows burnout from the inside but won't sell it as a mindset problem." |
| **3 signature observations** (things only this gap would notice) | what a comfortable insider would never say | • the busiest founders post the most about freedom • "we're a family" means no overtime pay • most ops dashboards exist to be screenshotted, not read | • the hospital's wellness webinar was scheduled during a double shift • "self-care" is what they tell you instead of staffing the floor • the nurses who burn out are the ones who still believe |

The tension statement is now the filter. Every surviving line should sound like it could only come from a person standing in *that* gap. If a line could have been written by any competent professional in the field — it hasn't been routed through the gap yet. Rewrite it from the outsider seat. (Optional: route this worksheet through `outsider-lens-filter` to sharpen the gap into a full voice profile before continuing.)

> **Calibration (from the outsider-lens craft):** sardonic, not bitter. The client *works the system while seeing through it* — competent participation plus clear-eyed skepticism, never just anger at the field. Too much access = boring expert. Too much gap = angry outsider. The sweet spot is "I know this world, I'm good in it, and I see exactly how absurd it is."

### Step 4 — The nod-over-yes economy: let silence characterize (Principle 3 + dialogue economy)

Connelly on Bosch: *"He nods a lot. He's a real economy-of-words guy."* The nod isn't shorthand for "yes" — it's a *character event*. A man who nods instead of saying yes is telling you he's uncomfortable with institutional language, that he holds something back, that he's an outsider even when he agrees. Transfer this to the client's voice: **what the client refuses to spell out characterizes them as much as what they say.** The over-explained draft says everything; the distinct voice trusts a silence to do work.

Run the nod-vs-yes substitution table on the draft. Find the places where the voice over-confirms, over-qualifies, or over-states, and replace the spoken "yes" with the nod — the implication the reader completes:

| The draft "says yes" (over-explains) | The "nod" (what the client leaves unsaid) | What the silence characterizes |
|---|---|---|
| "And yes, this absolutely works — I've seen it work dozens of times across many different clients." | "It works. I've watched it." | confidence that doesn't need to argue |
| "I'm not saying everyone else is wrong, but in my honest opinion, I think there might be a better way." | "There's a better way." | conviction; the hedges were apology, and they're gone |
| "I learned this lesson the hard way, through a lot of pain and difficult experiences that taught me…" | "I learned this at 2 a.m., re-running payroll for the third time." | the specific does the emotional work the explanation was begging for |
| "I would absolutely love to help you if you think you might be a good fit for this." | "If this is you, you know where to find me." | self-respect; the client who doesn't beg is the client worth hiring |
| "What I've come to deeply believe over my many years in this industry is that…" | "After 15 years: " | the credential is stated once, flat, and trusted |

The rule: a line that *announces* a feeling or a conviction (AN-3 — "I'm deeply passionate," "I genuinely believe," "I'm so excited") is the spoken "yes." Replace it with the nod — a specific, a flat statement, or a deliberate silence — and let the reader infer the conviction. **A voice that under-states reads as more certain than a voice that insists.** This is where the cut stops being mere brevity and becomes *characterization through economy*.

### Step 5 — The subtext check on the persuade-without-selling lines (Principle 5, Two-Layer Test)

The lines that have to move the reader — toward booking, replying, believing — are exactly the lines most likely to tip into a visible pitch and trigger the reader's sales-radar. Connelly's subtext doctrine fixes this: *"conversations that on face are about this, but they're really about that."* A line that on the surface is genuinely helpful, while the undertow does the persuading, sells without ever sounding like selling — because the reader feels *served*, not *worked*.

Take the 1–3 persuasive lines from your input and run each through the Two-Layer Test:

| | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **What is this line literally about?** | the surface content (a tip, an observation, a story) |
| **What is this line ACTUALLY about?** | the belief/action it's moving the reader toward (book the call, trust me, you have this problem) |
| **Gap check** | If the two answers are *identical*, the line is a naked pitch — there's no undertow, and the reader sees the sell. Create separation: keep the surface genuinely useful, let the undertow carry the persuasion. |

Worked transformation:

> **Literal = actual (naked pitch):** "If you're struggling with team burnout, you should book a call with me — I help leaders fix exactly this."
> **Surface ≠ undertow (subtext):** "The leaders who fix burnout never start with the team. They start with the one meeting they're afraid to cancel."
> *Why it persuades without selling:* on the surface it's a free, useful insight (the reader gets value just by reading it). Underneath, it positions the client as someone who sees the real lever, makes the reader suspect they have the exact problem named, and leaves the next step *implied*. The reader moves toward the client because they feel understood, not pitched. The CTA, if any, can now be a nod — "If that meeting just came to mind, you know what to do" — not a sell.

> **Honesty spine on the subtext lines (the most dangerous moment in this pass):** subtext makes a *true* insight land harder; it must never be the reason an empty claim *feels* earned. If the persuasive line's undertow promises a result the client can't actually deliver, the subtext is gilding a lie — and the cleaner the line, the more convincing the lie. Confirm the belief you're moving the reader toward is one the client's real track record supports. If it isn't, that's a `ghostwrite` / proof problem (get a real proof point), not a subtext problem.

### Step 6 — The final read-aloud: is this the CLIENT'S voice, or yours? (the #1 gate)

Now read the whole cut *aloud, cold,* against the client's voice samples. This is the step that separates "a ghostwritten piece that's tighter" from "the client's own voice, sharpened." It catches the #1 failure of all ghostwriting: **the ghostwriter's voice bleeding through.**

- **The voice-match test (the #1 gate).** Read the cut beside the client's actual voice note / Slack message. Does the cut sound like *them on their best day* — or like a polished writer wearing their name? If a phrase is *better written* than the client would ever write, it's yours, not theirs — and it has to go, however good it is. The tell: lines you're proud of as a writer. Connelly's economy serves the client's distinctness, never the ghostwriter's showpiece. Cut your darlings *first*, because they're the most likely to be in your voice, not theirs.
- **The gap test.** Does every surviving line sound like it came from someone standing in the Step-3 access/belonging gap? Any line a generic competent professional could have written hasn't been routed through the gap — rewrite it from the outsider seat or cut it.
- **The information re-check (preservation).** Walk the Step-1 inventory. Every load-bearing fact still present? If one's missing, restore it in compressed form. *Economy never costs information.*
- **The "didn't sound sold-to" test.** Read the persuasive lines as a skeptical reader. Do they feel like help, or like a pitch? If the reader would feel worked, the subtext failed — bury it deeper or pull the surface back toward genuine usefulness.
- **The honesty re-check.** Every surviving claim and telling detail still true and substantiated? No clean line is making an unproven claim sound effortless. A device rings a true line; it never gilds an empty one.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social** (LinkedIn / X post in a client's voice) | The most over-explained format — the cut is most aggressive here (often well past 50%). The access/belonging gap *is* the hook: open from the outsider seat (a signature observation from Step 3), not from a claim. Nod-economy on every confirmation line; social punishes over-explaining hardest. One persuasive line max, full subtext — a post that pitches reads as an ad and dies in the feed. The whole piece should sound like the client *talking*, not writing. |
| **Marketing** (About page / bio / brand-voice copy) | The access/belonging gap carries the entire page — a bio that lists credentials is "comfortable insider" and has no voice (Principle 4: rebuild). Cut the credential-recitation in half; let the one telling detail (a specific the client actually does) do the work the list was attempting. Nod-economy on the "what I believe" section: show the conviction through a specific, never announce it (AN-3). |
| **Copy** (sales email / landing line in a client's voice) | Subtext is load-bearing — every persuasive line gets the Two-Layer Test. The half-cut kills the benefit-list (one telling detail *implies* the benefits, which the reader trusts more than claims). Honesty spine is strictest here: the cleaner the cut, the more an unsubstantiated claim can sound effortless — confirm proof first. CTA as a nod, not a sell. |
| **Ghostwriting** (founder note / executive ghostwrite / newsletter) | The home format. All four moves run. The voice-match test (Step 6) is the deciding gate — an exec ghostwrite that sounds like *good writing* has failed; it must sound like the exec, only sharper. The access/belonging gap is what makes an exec sound like a person and not a press release. Preservation discipline matters most here: execs are accountable for every fact, so the cut may never lose or distort one. |
| **Content** (long-form article / thought-leadership in a client's voice) | Distribute the cut across sections — read the whole piece at speed and halve the *explanatory* paragraphs while preserving the argument's facts. The access/belonging gap is the through-line that keeps a long piece sounding like one person. Subtext on the persuasive close only; the body stays genuinely useful (the single specific anecdote out-persuades the principle stated five ways). |

### Worked example A — LinkedIn post in a founder's voice

**Client:** runs a $4M ops agency; came up operator-side. · **Gap:** "Has an agency but refuses to perform the founder hustle she watches everyone fake." · **Persuasive job:** make ops leaders trust her enough to DM, without pitching.

**INFORMATION-PRESERVATION CHECKLIST:** must survive → (1) she runs an ops agency, (2) she came up operator-side, (3) the claim that dashboards are mostly theater. Telling detail (gets more room): the dashboard nobody reads.

**BEFORE (108 words):**
> I wanted to share something that's been on my mind lately about the world of operations and dashboards. As someone who has spent over a decade building operations teams, I've genuinely come to believe that most of the dashboards companies build are actually not that useful. I think a lot of them exist mostly to look impressive in meetings rather than to actually help anyone make decisions. In my honest opinion, if you're a founder reading this, you should really take a hard look at whether your dashboards are truly serving your team or just serving your need to look on top of things. I'd love to chat if this resonates!

**AFTER (41 words):**
> Most ops dashboards exist to be screenshotted, not read.
>
> I came up operator-side. The busiest dashboard I ever built got opened twice — once to demo it, once to kill it.
>
> If yours is mostly for the meeting, you already know.

> *ECONOMY RATIO: 108 → 41 (62% reduction, 0 facts lost).* The half-cut deleted every hedge ("genuinely," "I think," "in my honest opinion," "really") and the setup ("I wanted to share…"). The access/belonging gap drives the open — *"exist to be screenshotted, not read"* is a signature observation only the operator-who-distrusts-theater would make. The telling detail (opened twice — to demo, to kill) does the work the explanation was begging for, and it's true. The close is a nod, not a pitch: *"you already know"* persuades by understanding, not by asking. The "I'd love to chat if this resonates" — the ghostwriter's voice, the begging CTA — is gone; the DM now comes because the reader feels seen. Voice-match: reads like *her*, flat and certain, not like a written post.

### Worked example B — Email in a coaching client's voice

**Client:** 15 years as an ER nurse, now coaches nurses on burnout. · **Gap:** "Knows burnout from the inside but won't sell it as a mindset problem." · **Persuasive job:** move a burned-out nurse to reply, without selling coaching.

**INFORMATION-PRESERVATION CHECKLIST:** must survive → (1) 15 years ER, (2) she coaches nurses, (3) the claim that the system, not the nurse, is the cause. Telling detail: the wellness webinar scheduled during a double shift.

**BEFORE (96 words):**
> Hi there, I hope this email finds you well! I wanted to reach out because I'm someone who is deeply passionate about helping nurses who are struggling with burnout. After spending fifteen long years working in the ER, I truly understand how hard it can be, and I've learned so much about how to overcome these challenges through mindset and self-care practices. If you're feeling burned out right now, I would absolutely love to help you on your journey to recovery. Please don't hesitate to reach out to me if you think you might be a good fit!

**AFTER (44 words):**
> Fifteen years in the ER taught me one thing about burnout: it's not your mindset.
>
> They scheduled our "wellness webinar" during a double shift. Nobody came. Everybody understood.
>
> The nurses who burn out are the ones who still believe. If that's you — reply.

> *ECONOMY RATIO: 96 → 44 (54% reduction, 0 facts lost).* The half-cut deleted "I hope this finds you well," "deeply passionate," "truly understand," "absolutely love," and the begging close. The access/belonging gap *is* the message: *"it's not your mindset"* is the line only the insider-who-won't-sell-mindset would write — and it refuses the entire category her competitors sell, which is the whole voice. The telling detail (webinar during a double shift; nobody came) is a true specific that does the persuading the explanation couldn't. Subtext on the close: literal = "you might be burned out"; actual = "I see you and I'm safe to reply to" — the surface names the reader's reality, the undertow earns the reply without pitching coaching. The CTA is a nod: *"If that's you — reply."* No "good fit," no journey, no begging. Voice-match: blunt, clinical, unsentimental — a nurse's voice, not a marketer's.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Connelly Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` (especially **AN-3 Characters Explaining Their Feelings**, **AN-1 Decorative Prose**, **AN-5 Exposition Dumps**) and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Economy of Expression**, **Outsider Lens Clarity**, **Subtext Density**, **Character-Driven Revelation**, **Prose Transparency**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **The cut is real and the payload is whole:** ~50% word reduction (at minimum a third) *and* every load-bearing fact from the Step-1 checklist still present. Economy that lost a fact is amputation, not compression — restore the fact.
- **The access/belonging gap is defined and true:** the tension statement has *both* poles (access AND a real reason they feel outside it). Both poles "comfortable insider" = no voice; rebuild (Principle 4). The gap is drawn from the client's actual life, not invented.
- **Every surviving line serves character (Q2):** no line survives the cut that reveals nothing about who the client IS. A shorter generic line is still generic. Route it through the gap or cut it.
- **Nod-over-yes is installed (AN-3):** the over-explained confirmations and announced feelings ("I genuinely believe," "I'm so passionate") are replaced by nods — specifics, flat statements, or deliberate silences. Under-statement reads as more certain than insistence.
- **The persuasive lines have an undertow (Q4 / Principle 5):** every persuade-without-selling line passes the Two-Layer Test — surface genuinely useful, undertow doing the persuading. A line where literal = actual is a naked pitch; rewrite for subtext. The reader should feel served, not sold.
- **It's the CLIENT'S voice (the #1 gate):** read aloud against the voice samples, the cut sounds like the client sharpened — not like a polished writer wearing their name. Any line "better written" than the client would write is the ghostwriter bleeding through; cut it.
- **Prose is transparent (AN-1):** no line is asking to be admired as writing. The reader sees through to the meaning, not at the craft. The economy is invisible; it reads as *the client being confident*, not as *a tight piece of copy*.
- **Honesty spine intact (Q7):** every surviving fact and telling detail is true and substantiated; no clean cut or subtext line is the reason an unproven claim *sounds* effortless. The craft sharpens a true voice; it never manufactures one.

## Common Pitfalls

- **#1 — The ghostwriter's voice bleeding through.** The most common and most fatal failure: the cut is genuinely well-written, the lines are sharp and quotable — and they're *yours*, not the client's. The tell is pride: the lines you're most pleased with as a writer are the ones least likely to be in the client's voice. A client who would never write "exist to be screenshotted, not read" now "sounds like" that — and it reads false to everyone who knows them. **Recovery:** Step 6 voice-match test. Read the cut beside the client's real voice note or Slack message; cut your darlings *first*, because they're the most likely to be your register. The goal is the client on their best day, never the ghostwriter's showpiece. Connelly's economy serves the character's distinctness, not the writer's ego (genius § Cross-Domain — Ghostwriting row; § How to Use This Skill).
- **#2 — Cutting information, not just words.** Halving the draft by deleting *facts* instead of *the words spent explaining facts* — the piece gets shorter and also gets dumber, losing the proof or the credential it existed to carry. **Recovery:** the Step-1 preservation checklist exists precisely to prevent this — build it *before* you cut, and walk it again at Step 6. A load-bearing fact can survive as a four-word fragment; it can never be deleted. Economy never costs information; if it did, you compressed the wrong thing (genius § Principle 1; § Decision Framework Q7).
- **#3 — A halved draft that's tighter but still generic.** The cut works, the words are gone, and the voice is now clean, fast, and indistinguishable from any competent professional — because the access/belonging gap was skipped.
