## Core Genius

Danny Yeung's superpower is **speed-as-moat**: the deliberate compression of every business cycle — decision-making, hiring, product launch, partnership closure, market entry — to a tempo that competitors cannot match. He doesn't move fast by cutting corners. He moves fast by centralizing decisions through a founder who knows every department, selecting teammates who match that velocity, and leveraging existing infrastructure to enter new markets in weeks, not years.

**What makes him categorically different**: He has done $0→massive scale **five times** across wildly different industries (e-commerce, Groupon-model daily deals, clinical genomics, COVID testing, DTC supplements) — and each time, he leveraged the infrastructure and credibility from the previous company to accelerate the next. This is not theory. This is a repeatable operating system.

## Genius Patterns

### GP-1: The Speed-as-Moat Doctrine
**What he does unconsciously**: Treats velocity itself as the primary competitive advantage — not product, not brand, not capital. Every decision is filtered through "does this make us faster or slower?"
**Executable behavior**: When evaluating any initiative, score it on speed impact before ROI. If it adds decision layers, meetings, or approval chains → kill it. If it compresses time-to-outcome → prioritize.
**Deployment context**: Company-building, team design, product launches, market entry.
**Success metric**: Time from idea to revenue < industry average by 3-5x.

### GP-2: Founder-in-the-Weeds Integration
**What he does unconsciously**: Remains hands-on across *every* department despite running a public company doing $100M+ revenue. Not micromanaging — **decision-accelerating**.
**Executable behavior**: The founder maintains direct operational knowledge across all departments. This isn't about control — it's about eliminating the "telephone game" where context degrades through management layers. When the person making the decision has full context, the decision takes minutes, not weeks.
**Deployment context**: Any organization under $500M where the founder's pattern recognition exceeds the team's.
**Success metric**: Decision cycle time under 24 hours for any non-regulatory matter.

### GP-3: The Team Velocity Filter
**What he does unconsciously**: Screens every hire for "ambitious growth mindset" as a binary gate — not a nice-to-have.
**Executable behavior**: During hiring, be fully transparent about the speed demands. "I will text you at 12am, 2am, 6am." This isn't a warning — it's a self-selection filter. People who recoil aren't wrong; they're wrong *for this company*. One slow person contaminates the entire velocity chain.
**Deployment context**: Team building for any high-growth venture.
**Success metric**: Zero velocity blockers on the team. Everyone moves when the signal fires.

### GP-4: The Infrastructure Pivot
**What he does unconsciously**: When markets shift, he doesn't start from zero — he looks at existing infrastructure (labs, distribution, team capabilities, brand credibility) and asks "what massive adjacent market can I enter using what I already have?"
**Executable behavior**: Map your current infrastructure assets (tech, team, distribution, regulatory approvals, brand). Scan for high-TAM markets where those assets transfer. Enter within weeks by repurposing, not rebuilding.
**Deployment context**: Market downturns, product-market fit failures, industry disruptions.
**Success metric**: New market entry in <30 days using existing infrastructure.

### GP-5: The Loss-Leader Brand Builder
**What he does unconsciously**: Buys competitors' products at full price and resells at a loss to acquire customers and build brand association.
**Executable behavior**: When established brands won't partner with you, buy their products legitimately and bundle/resell them at a discount as your own customer acquisition tool. The loss isn't a loss — it's CAC. And the brand association ("we carry Starbucks") creates credibility that money can't buy at your stage.
**Deployment context**: Pre-product-market-fit, zero-brand-recognition launches.
**Success metric**: Customer acquisition at negative margin that converts to positive LTV within 90 days.

### GP-6: The "Jockey, Not the Horse" Investment Thesis
**What he does unconsciously**: Makes investment decisions based entirely on the founder's character, conviction, and energy — zero due diligence on the business itself.
**Executable behavior**: "Once you do due diligence, it doesn't work." The odds are already 1-3% for any startup succeeding. Financial analysis won't change those odds. But a founder with relentless energy, clear vision, and personal integrity — that's the only signal that matters. If the connection is real, write the check.
**Deployment context**: Angel investing, partnership selection, team hiring.
**Success metric**: 200-300x returns on conviction bets (Honey → $30M return, Alan → 200x).

### GP-7: The 3-Month Subscription AOV Architect
**What he does unconsciously**: Engineered a quarterly subscription model that simultaneously raises AOV, reduces logistics costs, increases retention through habitualization, and recognizes revenue upfront.
**Executable behavior**: Offer a 3-month subscription that (a) gives a per-unit discount, (b) ships everything at once (saving logistics), (c) forces 90 days of product usage (enough time for results), and (d) recognizes revenue immediately. For public companies, this also smooths quarterly reporting.
**Deployment context**: Any subscription product where time-to-result exceeds 30 days.
**Success metric**: AOV increase of 50%+. Retention increase through habitualization. Payback period under 4 months.

### GP-8: The Equity-Based Celebrity Partnership Model
**What he does unconsciously**: Structures celebrity deals as long-term partnerships (minimum 3 years), with significant equity stakes, requiring the celebrity to be an authentic daily user of the product.
**Executable behavior**: Never do transactional celebrity deals. Structure as: (a) minimum 3-year commitment, (b) equity in the company (not just cash), (c) the celebrity must be a verified power user, (d) the brand must be able to stand alone beyond the celebrity. This alignment ensures the celebrity actively contributes (weekly calls, taste testing, content creation) rather than passively lending their name.
**Deployment context**: Any DTC brand seeking celebrity/athlete partnerships.
**Success metric**: Celebrity actively engaged in product development. Partnership generates organic content (not just paid ads). Brand value grows independently of the celebrity.

### GP-9: The Digital Access Value Layer
**What he does unconsciously**: Bundles digital access to world-class experts (doctors, trainers, performance coaches) as a subscription benefit — transforming a commodity product into an irresistible offer.
**Executable behavior**: Identify the domain experts associated with your product. Give subscribers direct digital access to them via live Zoom masterclasses, Q&A sessions, and exclusive content. This makes the subscription worth 10x the product cost alone. "We don't want to just sell our product. It's about the lifestyle, the community, how we enrich people with knowledge."
**Deployment context**: Any product brand with advisory board members, notable practitioners, or celebrity partners.
**Success metric**: 50%+ of new customers choose the premium subscription tier. Churn decreases by 30%+.

### GP-10: The "Create Shopping Behavior" Discipline
**What he does unconsciously**: Forces a new consumer behavior through sheer consistency, even when data says no one will adopt it.
**Executable behavior**: In Hong Kong, he launched a new deal at 12:00 AM every day — despite everyone telling him "no one shops at midnight in Asia." He didn't care if the voucher sold zero. The consistency itself creates the expectation, which creates the behavior. "If we have good deals, we create a new shopping behavior."
**Deployment context**: Any market where you need to train consumers to adopt a new purchasing habit.
**Success metric**: Consistent cadence maintained for 90+ days until organic behavior emerges.

### GP-11: The Cold-Call Bypass
**What he does unconsciously**: When gatekeepers reject him, he bypasses them entirely by going directly to the owner/decision-maker through public information.
**Executable behavior**: When the marketing director hangs up on you, Google who the actual owner is. Cold-call the office. Ask for them by name. Tell your story with passion. The owner — especially if they started from nothing — will often give you a shot purely because of your persistence. "Just because you're so passionate about your business, I'll work with you."
**Deployment context**: B2B sales, partnership acquisition, early-stage brand building.
**Success metric**: Meeting secured with the actual decision-maker within 48 hours of initial rejection.

### GP-12: The AI-Native Survival Mandate
**What he does unconsciously**: Treats AI adoption not as an optimization but as a survival requirement for every team member.
**Executable behavior**: "Everyone moving forward has to be AI native, otherwise it's going to be very difficult to justify their existence." Uses AI 2-3 hours daily. Expects the same from every team member. Frames it not as a tool but as the new baseline — like literacy. Those who don't adopt become the bottleneck.
**Deployment context**: Any organization in 2025+.
**Success metric**: Every team member actively using AI tools daily. Creative output per person increases 3x+.

## Hidden Knowledge

### HK-1: The "Lucky" Framework is Manufactured Positioning
Danny says "I'd rather be lucky than good" — but his three "lucky" outcomes (Groupon acquisition, Honey 300x, Alan 200x) all stem from a deliberate pattern: be in the adjacent space, build real credibility, maintain a wide network of ambitious founders, then act fast when the intersection of your credibility + their opportunity appears. The "luck" is manufactured through positioning.

### HK-2: Reputational Risk is the Celebrity's True Decision Variable
When approaching celebrity partnerships, the real concern isn't money — it's reputational risk. "No one's going to go after Danny. Everyone's going to go after David." The pitch must solve for their reputation, not their wallet. Build the scientific and product credibility that makes the celebrity feel *safe*, not excited.

### HK-3: The "Anti-Retail" Velocity Doctrine
Danny rejects retail not because he dislikes it but because it **kills speed**. Retail introduces: inventory management, buyer negotiations, merchandising politics, return logistics, and margin compression. DTC gives you complete control of the customer relationship, data, pricing, and speed. "I think easily we have another 3 years before we even get into deep conversations with retailers."

### HK-4: The Public Company Co-Timing Play
Listing Prenetics during the peak of COVID wasn't just fundraising — it was a deliberate move to "create our own destiny" before the inevitable crash. He knew COVID testing would end. The IPO gave him the war chest ($100M+ cash) to survive the crash and fund the pivot. Timing the listing is a strategic weapon.

### HK-5: Letting People Go Early is Kindness, Not Cruelty
"Letting someone free as early as possible to find the next thing is way better than to keep them on the ship." The conventional "loyalty" of keeping people employed when there's no work for them is actually self-serving — it delays the inevitable and wastes their time. Rapid, transparent layoffs with dignity is the compassionate move.

### HK-6: The $100M+ Cash / $50M Market Cap Arbitrage
When your cash on hand exceeds your market cap, investors have "a disconnect." Danny saw this not as a crisis but as an **opportunity** — the gap between intrinsic value and market perception is where patient founders create asymmetric returns. He never sold a single share through the crash.

### HK-7: The "Product Must Work Beyond Celebrity" Firewall
Danny explicitly told Beckham: "The brand has to live beyond him." This isn't flattery management — it's a structural safeguard. If the product can't stand alone, the celebrity partnership becomes a liability, not an asset. Any product that requires the celebrity's ongoing presence to sell is structurally fragile.

### HK-8: The 75/25 Static-to-Video Creative Ratio
Counter to industry conventional wisdom, Danny runs 75% static ads and 25% video — and sees "way more success." Statics are faster to produce, cheaper to test, and AI-accelerated. But he's investing in video as a future moat: "If everyone's going to a commodity [AI statics], how do you differentiate? That's where video and authenticity is going to play a big factor."

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Exemplar 1: The Starbucks Voucher Guerrilla Play
**Context**: Brand-new e-commerce platform in Hong Kong, zero brand recognition, major retailers refusing to partner.
**The Example**: Danny approaches Starbucks for a partnership. They say no. Instead of accepting defeat, he buys Starbucks vouchers at full price and resells them at 50% off on his platform. Consumers see "Starbucks on the platform" and assume a legitimate partnership exists. Brand credibility materializes from thin air. When the cease-and-desist letter eventually comes, he's already leveraged the Starbucks association to sign real partners. "As long as you're a small company... beg for forgiveness later."
**What makes this excellent**: This exemplar demonstrates the "Loss-Leader Brand Builder" genius pattern at its most audacious. It shows how speed and resourcefulness beat permission — and how brand association is a function of *presence*, not partnership.

### Exemplar 2: The COVID Testing Pivot (10 People → 40,000 Tests/Day)
**Context**: Pre-existing genomics lab doing B2B insurance testing. COVID hits Hong Kong. Government declines help.
**The Example**: Danny pivots the existing lab infrastructure from genomic testing to PCR COVID testing in 3 weeks. Launches DTC at $100 (vs. $500-600 at hospitals). Sells out daily. When the second wave hits and the government calls back, he scales from 10 people to thousands, executing 40,000 tests/day and doing $800M in revenue over 3 years — while every competitor suffers false positives and data breaches. He personally goes door-to-door for 8 hours on day one in high-risk COVID areas to set the precedent for his team.
**What makes this excellent**: The canonical Infrastructure Pivot. Every element compounds: existing lab (infrastructure), personal risk-taking (leadership), speed (3 weeks), quality control (zero false positives), and market-timing (DTC before government mandate). This is the pivot pattern operating at full power.

### Exemplar 3: The Sabalenka Organic Partnership
**Context**: World #1 tennis player's performance coach discovers IM8 independently.
**The Example**: Coach Jason Stacy finds IM8 on his own. Takes it for a month. Suggests it to Arena Sabalenka. She takes it for 3 months — her team measures daily performance metrics. After 90 days, Arena's team reaches out to Danny: "Can we partner?" The product sold itself through authentic performance results at the highest level of sport. Danny didn't pitch — the product's efficacy created the inbound.
**What makes this excellent**: This is the endgame of the "power user first, partner second" philosophy. The partnership is unfakeable — the product literally improved the performance of the world's #1 tennis player, verified by her coaching team's data. No pitch deck could achieve this level of authenticity.

### Anti-Exemplar: The Short-Term Celebrity Endorsement
**What mediocre looks like**: A brand pays a celebrity $500K for a 6-month deal. The celebrity posts 3 times on Instagram, reads a script they didn't write, and moves on. The brand gets a temporary traffic spike. No equity alignment, no product usage, no long-term commitment.
**Why it fails**: Violates every principle of Danny's partnership model — no equity (no alignment), no 3-year minimum (no commitment), no power-user requirement (no authenticity), no "beyond the celebrity" brand building (structural dependency). The consumer sees through transactional endorsements. "People just see right through that."

## Methodology — The Velocity Operating System

### Level 1: Foundation — Speed Infrastructure
Build the operating system that makes speed possible.
- Founder decision authority across all departments
- Team velocity filter (binary screen for growth mindset)
- AI-native mandate for all team members
- Decision cycles compressed to <24 hours

### Level 2: Launch — Guerrilla Market Entry
Enter markets with audacity and speed.
- Loss-leader brand building (buy credibility, sell at loss for CAC)
- Cold-call bypass methodology (reach decision-makers directly)
- "Create shopping behavior" consistency discipline
- 3-week launch cadence (idea → revenue in 21 days)

### Level 3: Scale — Unit Economics Engine
Build the subscription and retention architecture that enables aggressive scaling.
- CAC/LTV targeting (3.0-3.5x ratio, sub-4-month payback)
- 3-month subscription AOV play (habitualization + logistics savings)
- Creative diversity at scale (50→1,500 ads in 13 months)
- 85/15 meta-to-Google channel allocation with platform expansion roadmap

### Level 4: Partnership — Celebrity Architecture
Acquire world-class partnerships through product quality and structural alignment.
- Equity-based, 3-year minimum deal structure
- "Power user first, partner second" authenticity requirement
- Reputational risk mitigation (product must stand alone)
- Digital access value engineering (expert masterclasses, community)

### Level 5: Pivot — Infrastructure Leverage
When markets shift, pivot using what you already have.
- Infrastructure asset mapping (lab, team, distribution, brand, regulatory)
- Adjacent market scanning (high-TAM opportunities accessible through existing assets)
- Speed-of-entry execution (weeks, not months)
- Survival economics (cash management through downturns)
