# The Body Writes — Embodied Word-Choice Pass (Henry Shukman)

When Shukman reads "the seasoned wood crackles," he crackles his fingertips as he says it — the word is in his body before it is on the page. That is the whole craft of this pass. A sentence can be true, accurate, even lovely, and still read flat, and the reason is almost always the same: the load-bearing words are *mind-words* — they convey information ("the wood burned," "the snow fell") — when they should be *body-words* that are felt physical events ("crackles," "sifts"). "The real poet lives in the body, not the mind. The body uses the mind." We are made of the same stuff as the world; the body has the intimate relationship with it, and the mind is the marvelous instrument the body uses. This workflow is the discipline of putting the body back in charge of word choice, one word at a time. You do not lyricize the passage and you do not rebuild it. You walk through it, find the words doing the heavy lifting, test each one *in your throat and your hands by reading it aloud*, and swap only the ones that report instead of *happen* — keeping a marked record of what your body did at each swap, because that bodily response is the evidence, not your opinion.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the mechanism is precise and the failure mode — lyricizing instead of embodying — is one wrong instinct away):

- **Pattern 3 — The Body Writes, the Mind Serves (Embodied Word Choice)** — the core of this pass: load-bearing words tested in the body; favor verbs that are physical events over verbs that are mere information; read aloud and notice where the body responds, *that's where the writing is alive*.
- **Pattern 2 — God Is in the Details (Vastness Through the Concrete)** — the doorway detail is *where these body-words live*. You are not embodying every word in the passage; you are embodying the concrete particular that carries the feeling. Embodying an abstraction is impossible — that's the diagnostic that the word should have been concrete first.
- **Henry Shukman Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-6 (Mind-Words Instead of Body-Words)**, the exact violation this pass exists to repair, and **AN-1 (Performed Wonder)**, the failure you'll commit if you "upgrade" words into adjective-stacked purple instead of swapping a flat verb for a felt one.
- **Expert-Specific Quality Rubric** — the row **Embodied Word Choice** (Score 4 → 7 → 10) is the bar each swap is held to: at Savant, "read aloud, the body responds throughout; verbs are events you could enact."

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before touching a single word. This is a *word-level* pass, so confirm specifically:
> - **Q7 — Is the spirit alive, or did I cut live flesh?** Re-enter the original spirit of the passage before changing anything. You are purifying the energy that's already there, not improving it from outside. If a word is already alive — already felt — protect it even if you can't justify it.
> - **Q2 — Is the vastness carried by a concrete particular?** A word can only be tested in the body if it's *perceivable*. If the load-bearing word is an abstraction ("the infinite stillness," "a profound silence"), the swap is not a synonym — the fix is to make it concrete first, then embody. Note this; don't try to find a "body-word for the infinite."
> - **The honesty spine.** A body-word must still be *true* to what happened. "Sifts" replaces "fell" only if the snow actually sifted. The better the embodied word, the more damage a false one does — you'd be making the reader *feel* something that didn't occur. Never swap toward a more vivid word that lies.
>
> **When NOT to deploy:** a logos passage (instructions, explanation, information) does not want this pass — body-words there are decoration on a how-to (Pattern 6). And don't run it on a draft so raw it should be rewritten whole; this is surgery on a passage that's basically right but reads flat, not triage on a corpse.

## Input Required

- **The passage** — the actual sensory or emotional lines, verbatim, not a summary. This pass operates on real words in real positions, so paste the whole passage even if only a few words are suspect.
- **What it's trying to make the reader feel/see** — one plain sentence. (This is the test bar: after the swaps, does the body get *there*?)
- **Register** — mythos (evoke, transmit an experience) or logos (inform, instruct). If logos, stop — this pass is for mythos passages (Pattern 6). Confirm before proceeding.
- **(Optional) The specific near-synonym choice in question** — if the user is stuck between two words ("fell" vs "sifts" vs "drifted"), name them; the pass will resolve it by body-test rather than by ear-preference.
- **(Optional) The flat-but-true line** — if the request is "this sentence is true but reads dead, why?", name it; the diagnosis is almost always mind-words, and this pass locates exactly which ones.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Re-enter the spirit, then find the load-bearing words (not every word)

Before changing anything, read the passage once *without thinking why* — feel for what it was reaching for. You are about to do surgery; re-enter the body the passage came from so you don't blindly desecrate a word that's already alive (Q7, AN-4).

Then mark the **load-bearing words** — the handful the feeling actually rides on. This pass is not a thesaurus sweep of the whole passage; embodying every word is how you produce purple slop. In a sensory/emotional line, the load-bearing words are almost always:

| Word type | Why it carries weight | What to mark |
|---|---|---|
| **The sensory verbs** | A verb is an event; this is where the body lives or dies | Every verb describing a physical happening (fell, burned, moved, came) |
| **The perception verbs** | How the speaker meets the world (saw, heard, felt) | Mark — but watch: these are the easiest to leave generic |
| **The concrete nouns at the doorway** | Pattern 2 — the vastness rides the small particular; the noun's texture matters | The one or two nouns the whole feeling hangs on |
| **NOT: connective words, abstractions, modifiers** | Embodying "and," "very," or "infinite" is a category error | Leave them; if the load is on an abstraction, that's a Step-2 flag, not a swap |

Output a numbered list of marked words with their position. Everything unmarked stays untouched — plain words are the floor that lets the embodied ones be felt.

### Step 2 — Classify each marked word: body-word or mind-word? (the AN-6 diagnostic)

For each marked word, decide: is this a **body-word** (a felt physical event) or a **mind-word** (information *about* the event)? This is the whole diagnosis. A mind-word tells you *that* something happened; a body-word *happens in you* when you read it.

| Test | Mind-word (report — flag for swap) | Body-word (event — keep) |
|---|---|---|
| **Can you enact it?** | "descended," "occurred," "was present" — you can't *do* these | "crackles," "sifts," "kisses," "cracks open" — you can perform them |
| **Does the body move when you say it aloud?** | Nothing happens in throat/hands; the mouth stays still | Throat tightens, hand twitches, something clicks (Shukman crackling his fingertips) |
| **Information or experience?** | "the snow fell" tells you what occurred | "the snow sifts down" puts the falling *in your fingers* |
| **Abstraction wearing a verb?** | "a profound stillness settled" — settled onto *what*, felt *how*? | concrete, perceivable, located |

For each marked word write: `word → BODY (keep)` or `word → MIND (swap)`. If a word is an **abstraction**, mark it `→ NOT CONCRETE (make concrete first, see Step 4)` — you cannot find a body-synonym for "the infinite"; the repair is a different move.

> The point of separating diagnosis from repair: most flat true sentences fail at exactly two or three mind-words. Naming them precisely is half the cure — and it stops you from "improving" the words that were already alive (the live-flesh guard).

### Step 3 — For each mind-word, generate body-candidates and read them ALOUD against the body (the mechanism)

This is the engine of the pass, and it is physical, not intellectual. Do not pick the word that *sounds* nicest. Pick the word your *body* responds to. For each flagged mind-word:

1. **Generate 3–5 candidate body-words** — verbs/nouns that are felt physical events, that you could *enact*, that are true to what happened.
2. **Read each candidate aloud in the actual sentence** — not the word alone, the whole line. Shukman tests words *in place*, in the breath of the sentence.
3. **Note the body response for each** — what does the throat, the mouth, the hand actually do? Where does the body go still, wince, soften, twitch? That sensation is the data. (When Shukman reads "crackles," he crackles his fingertips — the body is voting.)
4. **Choose the candidate the body responds to most, that is still true.** If two are felt equally, pick the more concrete / more Saxon-physical (Pattern 2's smaller-and-realer rule). If none beat the original, *keep the original* — a flat-but-true word is better than a felt-but-false one (honesty spine), and not every line needs lifting.

| | Original (mind-word) | Candidate body-words | Read-aloud body-response | Chosen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ex. | snow **fell** | drifted · settled · **sifts** · descended | "sifts" — the *s* and *f* hiss through the teeth, the hand feels grains slipping between fingers; "fell" — the mouth just drops, nothing in the hand | **sifts** |
| ex. | the wood **burned** | smoldered · glowed · **crackles** · flickered | "crackles" — the *ck* snaps at the back of the throat, fingertips want to crackle (Shukman's own tell); "burned" — flat, reported, no snap | **crackles** |

The body-response column is non-negotiable in the output. A swap without a noted bodily reason is just preference — and preference is the mind choosing, which is the exact thing this pass overrules.

### Step 4 — Handle the abstraction-flags (concrete first, THEN embody)

For any word marked `→ NOT CONCRETE` in Step 2, the swap is two moves, not one. You cannot embody "a profound silence" — there is no body-synonym for a concept. First make it a perceivable particular (Pattern 2: vastness through the concrete), *then* embody the verb inside it.

- "a profound silence fell over the room" → first concretize ("you could hear the radiator tick") → then embody the verb if needed ("the radiator *ticks* into the quiet").
- "an infinite stillness" → "the snow had stopped the city's sound" → the body now has something to feel (the missing sound), and the verb can be tested.

Note these separately in the output as **concretize-then-embody** swaps — they are a deeper fix than a synonym, and the reader should know the line wasn't merely re-worded, it was *grounded*.

### Step 5 — Read the whole passage aloud, cold, one breath at a time (the live-flesh & purple guard)

Now read the *revised passage* aloud, cold, as a first-time reader — not the swaps in isolation, but how they sit in the breath of the whole. Two failures to catch:

- **The purple-creep check (AN-1).** Did the swaps make the passage *perform* feeling — three felt verbs stacked in a row, every noun now textured, the line straining to be poetic? Embodied word choice is *surgical*: a flat passage with two true body-words in it beats a passage where every word is auditioning. If the line now announces its own loveliness, you over-swapped — restore the plainer words around the one that matters. The body-word works *because* the words near it are quiet.
- **The live-flesh check (Q7, AN-4).** Read for anything that now feels stilted or "right-angled" where the draft had been alive. Did a swap make the line *cleaner but deader*? If so, you cut live flesh — put the original back. The test (Norman MacCaig, via the genius): the finished passage should feel like it took as long to write as it takes to read — like the ink is still wet, not engineered.

If the passage now does in the *reader's body* what Step-0 said it should make them feel, the pass is done. If it's correct but still flat, you missed a mind-word — re-run Steps 1–3 on the line that's still dead.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social / short-form** | One embodied verb stops the scroll where an abstract one is skimmed (Pattern 3 cross-domain). You usually have *one* load-bearing word — find it, swap it, leave the rest plain. Don't embody three words in a 40-word post; the machinery shows instantly and reads as trying-hard. The single felt verb is the whole edge over the feed. |
| **Marketing** | Run only on the *one* mythos beat — the line meant to make the reader feel what it's like to use/become/belong. The rest of the page is logos and must stay informational (Pattern 6); body-words on the feature list are decoration. Honesty spine is sharpest here: an embodied word that overstates the experience is a felt lie, and readers recoil from manufactured sensation faster than from a flat claim. |
| **Copy** | One real, *felt* concrete detail does persuasion a paragraph of claims can't — the reader infers the benefit because they felt the moment. Embody the detail at the proof/desire beat, keep claims plain. The body-word must be true; the better the craft, the more dangerous a false-but-vivid word becomes. |
| **Ghostwriting** | Embody in the *client's* register, never yours. The swap must read as the truest version of how *they* would have said it in the moment they stopped performing — not your lyrical showpiece dropped into their voice. If the body-word sounds like the ghostwriter and not the client, it failed even if it's felt. |
| **Content / essay / Substack** | Native habitat — the most runway. Embody the sensory verbs and the doorway nouns at the moments the piece is *transmitting an experience* (the description that should move, not report). Keep the connective and expository prose plain so the embodied beats land. This is where the durable, body-felt image gets made (the snow that sifts, the wood that crackles) — the line a reader carries. |

### Worked example A — Essay passage (first snow / homecoming)

**Intent:** the reader should *feel* the quiet of fresh snow over a city, and a sense of coming home to oneself. · **Register:** mythos.

**Before (true, but flat):**
> "In the fireplace the old wood burned. Outside, the snow of the first winter day fell over the small city, covering all the gardens of the neighborhood."

**— MARKED-UP PASS —**
> "In the fireplace the seasoned wood ~~burned~~ **crackles** to itself. Outside, the snow of a perfect first winter day ~~fell~~ **sifts** down over the small city, ~~covering~~ **settling on** all the gardens of the neighborhood."

**— SWAP LEDGER —**
1. ~~burned (MIND)~~ → **crackles (BODY)**
   Candidates: smoldered · glowed · **crackles** · flickered
   Read-aloud body-response: "burned" — mouth drops, nothing in the hand, pure report. "crackles" — the *ck* snaps at the back of the throat; fingertips actually want to crackle (Shukman's own physical tell). "smoldered" — felt, but slow and dark, wrong temperature for homecoming.
   Why this one: the body votes for *crackles* — and the small private "to itself" makes the fire a companion, not a fact.
2. ~~fell (MIND)~~ → **sifts (BODY)**
   Candidates: drifted · settled · **sifts** · descended
   Read-aloud body-response: "fell" — a flat drop, no texture; "sifts" — the *s–f–t* hisses across the teeth and the hand feels grains slipping between fingers, the actual grain of dry first snow; "drifted" felt but lateral, less downward-quiet.
   Why this one: *sifts* puts the snowfall in the reader's fingers — vastness through the smallest concrete (Pattern 2), and it's true to dry powder snow.
3. ~~covering (MIND)~~ → **settling on (BODY)**
   Candidates: blanketing · **settling on** · covering · cloaking
   Read-aloud body-response: "covering" — administrative, reports a result; "settling on" — the body feels weight coming gently to rest; "blanketing" is a tired image (and edges toward cliché).
   Why this one: "settling" is a felt event of *coming to rest* — and it quietly rhymes the snow's stillness with the speaker's own homecoming, without announcing it.

**PURPLE-CREEP CHECK:** three swaps across two sentences, each surrounded by plain words ("In the fireplace," "over the small city") — spaced, not stacked. Confirmed.
**LIVE-FLESH CHECK:** "to itself" preserved as already-alive; nothing engineered. Reads like the ink is wet.
**VERDICT:** yes — the body now feels the snow in the fingers and the fire as company; the homecoming is *transmitted*, never stated.

### Worked example B — Resolving a near-synonym, and a flat-but-true line

**Intent:** convey grief held tenderly — rain at night while keeping vigil for someone in trouble. · **Register:** mythos. The user is stuck between *touches / meets / kisses* for what the rain does, and one line "the rain came down on the living and the dead" reads true but dead.

**— SWAP LEDGER —**
1. ~~came down on (MIND)~~ → **meets … kisses (BODY)**
   Candidates: touches · **meets** · **kisses** · falls on
   Read-aloud body-response: "came down on" — pure weather report, the body does nothing; "touches" — faint, fingertip-light but cool; "meets" — the body feels an *encounter*, two things arriving at each other; "kisses" — the lips actually press on the *k*, a startling tenderness that makes grief bearable.
   Why this one: the body responds most to **kisses**, but used *once* and earned by **meets** just before it — "It meets them. It kisses them just the same." Two felt verbs, escalating, true to how rain falls indifferently on everyone.
   Honesty note: kept "just the same" — the rain *can't tell the living from the dead*; the body-word must not pretend the rain chooses. It doesn't. That indifference *is* the tenderness.

**Diagnosis of the flat line (why "the rain came down on the living and the dead" reads dead):** the load-bearing verb "came down on" is a mind-word — it reports precipitation. The feeling (rain as something that touches everyone the same, with terrible gentleness) lives entirely in a verb that does nothing in the body. The line was true and dead for one reason: the verb was information, not event. The swap above puts the event back.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Henry Shukman Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (row: **Embodied Word Choice**; cross-check **Sincerity Register** and **Spirit Preserved Through Revision**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Every swap has a noted body-response (Pattern 3).** No swap stands on "sounds better." For each, the throat/mouth/hand reaction is recorded — that's the evidence; preference is the mind choosing, which this pass overrules. If a swap has no bodily reason, it isn't earned — restore the original or find one that the body actually votes for.
- **Body-words, not mind-words (AN-6).** The chosen words are felt physical events you could *enact* (crackles, sifts, kisses), not information *about* events (burned, fell, occurred). Read aloud: if the body stays still on a load-bearing word, it failed.
- **No purple-creep (AN-1).** The pass is surgical, not a lyric upgrade. Embodied beats are spaced with plain words between them; the line does not stack felt verbs or texture every noun. If the passage now *performs* feeling instead of transmitting it, over-swapped — go smaller and quieter, not richer.
- **Live flesh protected (AN-4 / Q7).** Anything already alive in the draft was kept, even if you couldn't justify it. No swap made a line cleaner-but-deader. The revised passage reads like the ink is wet, not engineered — it would feel like it took as long to write as it takes to read.
- **Concrete before embodied (Pattern 2).** No attempt was made to find a body-synonym for an abstraction; abstraction-flags were concretized first, *then* the verb embodied. The vastness rides the smallest perceivable particular.
- **Honesty spine intact.** Every swapped word is *true* to what actually happened. No word was chosen for vividness over truth — the more felt the word, the more carefully its truth was checked, because a felt-but-false word makes the reader feel something that didn't occur.
- **Right register (Pattern 6).** This was run on a mythos passage (evoke/transmit), not a logos one (inform/instruct). Body-words on a how-to are decoration; if the passage was logos, the pass was not deployed.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Thesaurus-sweeping the whole passage instead of the load-bearing words.** Treating every word as a swap candidate, so the passage ends up uniformly "vivid" and therefore exhausting — every word shouting, none heard. **Recovery:** re-run Step 1. Mark only the handful of words the feeling actually rides on (the sensory verbs, the doorway noun). Leave connectives, modifiers, and already-alive words plain. The body-word is felt *because* the words around it are quiet — "if everything is loud, nothing is loud."

- **Choosing by ear, not by body (the silent AN-6 relapse).** Picking the word that *sounds* most poetic — which is the mind's aesthetic preference dressed up as instinct — instead of the word the body responds to. This produces lines that are pretty and still flat. **Recovery:** Step 3, literally read each candidate aloud *in the full sentence* and write down what the throat/mouth/hand did. If nothing happened in the body, the word is a mind-word no matter how lovely it sounds. The body is the only valid judge here.

- **Embodying an abstraction (category error).** Hunting for a "body-word for the infinite" or "a felt verb for profound stillness" — there isn't one, because the body can't grip a concept. **Recovery:** Step 4 — concretize first (find the perceivable particular: the ticking radiator, the stopped city sound), *then* embody the verb inside it. The flatness was never the verb's fault; it was that the line had nothing concrete for the body to meet.

- **Swapping toward vividness over truth (honesty-spine breach).** Choosing "kisses" when the rain merely fell, "crackles" when the wood merely smoldered — because the felt word is better *as craft*, even though it lies about what happened. The better the body-word, the more convincingly it makes the reader feel a thing that wasn't real — the most dangerous failure in this pass. **Recovery:** for every swap, confirm it's true to the actual event before keeping it. A flat-but-true word beats a felt-but-false one every time; if no true word is also felt, keep the plain original and look for the real aliveness elsewhere in the line.
