## Cards on the Table  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Isolates all the major objections.
**Structure**: `Other than {stated objection}, is there any reason we couldn't {desired action}?`

> ****

#### Other than {stated objection}, is there any reason we couldn't {desired action}?

##### Examples:
- Other than the investment, is there any reason we couldn't move forward today?
- Besides timing, is there anything else standing in the way?
- Aside from wanting to think it over, is there any reason this wouldn't make sense?
- Outside of budget considerations, do you see any reason not to do this?
- Other than getting internal approval, is there anything preventing us from getting started?

### Why It Works
Most salespeople make the mistake of treating the first objection as the real objection.
Prospect:
> "It's too expensive."
Salesperson:
> Handles price.
Prospect:
> "I also need to talk to my partner."
Salesperson:
> Handles partner.
Prospect:
> "I'm also concerned about implementation."
Salesperson:
> Handles implementation.
Now the conversation becomes a game.
The prospect produces objections.
The salesperson tries to eliminate them.
The prospect produces another.
The salesperson eliminates another.
This creates a negative rhythm where the prospect's job is to generate resistance and the salesperson's job is to react to it.
Every objection that gets handled creates room for another objection to appear.
This persuasion pattern fixes that. 
---

#### It Changes The Objective Of The Conversation
Most objection handling asks:
> "How do I overcome this objection?"
This pattern asks:
> "Is this actually the objection?"
Before you try to solve a problem, you first determine whether it's the only problem.
This prevents solving the wrong thing.

#### It Stops The Whac-A-Mole Cycle
Once all objections are visible, the conversation changes.
Instead of:
Objection → Response → New Objection → Response
You get:
Objection A
Objection B
Objection C
Then:
> "If we solved A, B, and C, would there be any reason not to move forward?"
Now you're solving a complete set of constraints rather than chasing an endless series of them.

#### Commitment Before The Objections Are Solved
After identifying all objections, you can ask:
> "If we were able to address those concerns, could you make a yes decision?"
The prospect now commits to the logic of the decision before the objections are resolved.
This creates consistency later.
If the objections are genuinely addressed, the prospect has already agreed that buying would make sense. 
---

#### Are the Objections Genuine?
Many prospects don't intentionally hide objections.
They simply haven't thought through them.
Which is why they still have the problem and need your help with solving it.
By asking a question like: 
> Other than the investment, is there any reason we couldn't move forward today?
You can proactively figure out all the potential things that could stop a person from buying. 
---

#### Evaluate the Whole, not the Parts
Normally, prospects evaluate one concern at a time.
This pattern causes them to step back and evaluate the opportunity as a whole.
For example:
> "Other than timing, is there any reason this wouldn't make sense?"
Now they're no longer focused exclusively on one aspect of the investment. 
They're evaluating the entire decision.

#### Preempts the "Think It Over" Objection
Because you've uncovered and addressed the prospect's concerns throughout the conversation, there's far less chance of reaching the end only to hear:
> "I need to think it over."
By the time a decision is requested, the major objections have already been identified, discussed, and resolved. 
Instead of discovering new concerns at the finish line, both parties have a clear understanding of what's standing in the way before going for the close. 

#### Provides Clarity
A prospect can only be in one of three states:

##### State 1: One Objection
> "No, that's the only thing."
Excellent.
Now you know exactly what needs to be addressed.
---

##### State 2: Multiple Objections
> "Actually there are a few other things..."
Excellent.
Now you can uncover them all before attempting to solve them.
---

##### State 3: Endless Objections
Also valuable.
Because now you're discovering that the issue is lack of fit. 
You don’t want these customers. Don’t close them. Refer them to your competitors instead!

## Emotional Labeling  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Names an unspoken issue in a way to gain more information. 
**Structure**: `It seems/sounds/feels like you're {emotion}.`
> ****

##### It seems/sounds/feels like you're {emotion}.

##### Examples
- It sounds like your {emotion}.
- You’re {emotion}. 
- It seems like you’re {emotion}
- It feels like you’re {emotion}
Can be framed positively or negatively. 

##### Negative Labels
You're upset.
---
It sounds like you're disappointed.
---
It seems like you feel like nobody is listening.

##### Positive Labels
It sounds like this is very important to you.
---
It seems like this solution fits the way you naturally like to operate.
---
It feels like you're more confident than when we started this conversation.

#### Why It Works

##### Helps People Feel Understood
One of the deepest human desires is to feel understood.
Not agreed with.
Not fixed.
Understood.
When someone feels that you've accurately identified what they're experiencing, rapport increases dramatically.
They feel seen.
They feel heard.
They feel less alone in the experience.

##### If You're Right, You're Really Right
When you accurately label an emotion, something interesting happens.
The other person often expands.
> Exactly!
Or:
> That's it!
Then they elaborate.
They provide more information.
They become more open.
The label acts as proof that you're paying attention.

##### If You’re wrong, It Invites Correction
Human beings have a powerful urge to correct inaccurate descriptions of themselves.
That's why emotional labeling works even when it misses.
The label creates a response.
Either:
> Yes, that's exactly it.
Or:
> No, it's actually this.
Both outcomes move the conversation forward.

##### Reduces Defensiveness
Questions can sometimes feel interrogative.
Advice can feel judgmental.
Labels feel observational.
Instead of:
> Why are you angry?
We get:
> It sounds like you're angry.
The second version feels far less confrontational.
The person doesn't feel examined.
They feel as if you’re really trying to understand them.

##### Slows the Conversation Down
People often speak from emotion before they've fully processed it.
Labeling slows the interaction.
The conversation moves from reaction to reflection.
Instead of immediately defending their position, they begin examining it.

##### Earns the Right to Be Heard
When someone feels misunderstood, they automatically discount your advice. 
It doesn't matter how intelligent, logical, or accurate your solution is. 
Emotional labeling closes that gap.
Every accurate label communicates:
> I get it.
> I see what's really going on here.
> I understand you. 
When someone feels deeply understood, your words carry more weight because they trust that your recommendation is being made from an accurate understanding of their situation rather than from assumptions.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates rapport.
- Increases emotional awareness.
- Encourages elaboration.
- Helps people feel understood.
- Surfaces hidden concerns.
- Reduces defensiveness.
- Creates clarification.
- Slows emotional reactions.
- Builds the deepest level of trust.

## How Important?   [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Increases percieved significance of problem, solution or capability.
**Structure**: `If {condition is true}, how important is {solution/value}?`
> ****

##### If {condition is true}, how important is {solution/value}?

##### Examples
- If something breaks at 2 a.m., how important is it to have responsive customer support?
- If leads are expensive to acquire, how important is conversion?
- If attention is scarce, how important is having a compelling message?
- If customer acquisition costs continue rising, how important is referral generation?
- If growth creates operational complexity, how important are systems?
- If implementation is important to you, how important is having a team that helps you implement?
- If things don't go perfectly… and they rarely do… how important is it to have someone in your corner?

#### Why It Works

##### Creates Value Before Presenting the Solution
Most people try to prove the value of their solution after they've presented it.
Better is to get the prospect to acknowledge the value of the ingredients before they're connected to the solution.
Instead of saying:
> "Our support team is valuable."
You ask:
> "If something breaks at 2 a.m., how important is responsive support?"
Now the prospect creates and commits to the value. 

##### Establishes Agreement
This pattern functions as a soft trial close.
Every time someone answers:
> "Very important."
They are making a small commitment.

##### Stacks Reasons to Buy
One of the most powerful uses of this pattern is repetition.
Not of the exact question.
But of the structure.
For example:
> If leads are expensive, how important is conversion?
> If conversion matters, how critical is follow-up?
> If follow-up matters, how valuable is automation?
> If automation matters, how essential is reliability?
By the time the solution is presented, the prospect has already agreed that each component is valuable.
The close becomes less about persuasion and more about connecting the dots.

##### Lets the Prospect Sell Themselves
People trust conclusions they reach themselves more than conclusions imposed upon them.
This pattern works because you aren't declaring value.
You're eliciting it.
Instead of:
> "You need this."
You ask:
> "How important is this?"
The answer comes from them.
And self-generated conclusions tend to be far more persuasive than externally imposed ones.

##### Creates Ownership
Every agreement creates a small amount of psychological ownership.
The prospect is no longer evaluating your opinion.
They're evaluating their own.
When someone says:
> "That's extremely important."
That statement now belongs to them.
Not you.

##### Builds a Logical Chain
The best versions create a sequence of agreements.
Each answer supports the next answer.
Each conclusion makes the next conclusion easier to accept.
Eventually the solution feels like a natural extension of everything they've already agreed is important.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates value before presenting a solution.
- Generates small commitments.
- Functions as a soft trial close.
- Builds agreement incrementally.
- Lets prospects persuade themselves.
- Creates ownership.
- Establishes logical buying criteria.
- Reduces resistance.
- Makes closing feel natural.

## When Did You?  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Establishes a decision, realization, or change as already occured.
**Structure**: `When did you first realize {desired realization}?`
> ****

##### When did you first realize {desired realization}?

##### Examples
- When did you know this needed handled?
- When did you know it was time to find a solution?
- When did you know you were capable to…
- When did you first realize this wasn't going to solve itself?
- When did you know you couldn't keep doing it this way?
- When did you begin actively searching for a better way?
- When did you stop seeing yourself as a beginner?
- When did this start feeling like the right direction?
- When did you decide this was worth investing in?

#### Why It Works

##### Presupposes the Realization Already Happened
We're not asking if they realize something. We're asking when they realized it.
The realization itself is treated as a given. The mind shifts from "did this happen?" to "when did this happen?"
That search produces the belief.

##### Accesses Memory to Build Belief
When someone searches for when they first realized something, they often find supporting memories.
Those memories make the realization feel real and owned rather than suggested.
What was implicit becomes explicit. What was vague becomes concrete.

##### Installs Conclusions Without Argument
Direct statements can be challenged.
"This approach isn't working" invites resistance.
"When did you first realize this approach wasn't working?" installs the same idea without the defensiveness.
The answer creates the belief naturally.

##### Creates a Turning Point
When someone identifies a moment of realization, they mentally mark it as significant.
It becomes a reference point: "This is when things changed."
Reference points anchor new behavior and new commitment.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Installs beliefs through questions rather than assertions
- Presupposes a realization has already occurred
- Creates memory-anchored belief that feels self-discovered
- Establishes a turning point in the listener's narrative
- Reduces resistance by bypassing direct suggestion

## Why Not Less?  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Elicits justification of the listener’s goals and motives.
**Structure**: `What would {desired outcome} look like? Why not {smaller version}?`
> ****

##### What would {desired outcome} look like? Why not {smaller version of that outcome}?

##### Examples
- "You said you want to double the business. Why not just grow 20%?"
- "You said you want to completely replace the hiring process. Why not just improve the onboarding?"
- "You want to work four days a week. Why not just cut one meeting a day?"
- "You want to hit $500k a month. Why not just go for $250k?"
- "You said you want your team to run without you. Why not just hire a better manager?"

#### Why It Works

##### Forces Justification of Goals
Most people state goals but never examine them.
This pattern asks them to defend their ambitions.
When someone explains why they want to double and not just grow 20%, they remind themselves (and tell us)exactly what they're after.
We don't have to motivate them. They motivate themselves.

##### Reveals True Drivers
The answer to "why not less?" exposes what's really being chased.
Sometimes it's money. Sometimes it's status. Sometimes it's freedom. Sometimes it's proving something.
Whatever the answer, it's more real and more motivating than the original goal statement.

##### Amplifies Desire Through Self-Articulation
Asking "why not less?" causes the listener to argue for wanting more.
Every argument they make increases their emotional investment in the bigger outcome.
They're no longer passively hoping for something. They're actively defending why they want it.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Gets the prospect to justify and own their goals
- Reveals true underlying motivation
- Creates a higher bar that makes modest solutions feel insufficient
- Amplifies desire through self-articulation
- Makes the listener advocate for themselves

## Winning Conditions  [Elicitation]
**Summary**: Uncovers the criteria someone needs to move forward.
**Structure**: `What would need to happen for you to {desired commitment/result}?`
> ****

##### What would need to happen for you to {desired commitment / decision / result}?

##### Examples
- "What would need to happen for you to feel ready to move forward?"
- "What's the one thing that, if it were true, would make this an obvious decision for you?"
- "What information are you missing that's keeping you from deciding?"
- "What would you need to see in the first 30 days to know this is working?"
- "What would make you completely confident that this is the right move?"

#### Why It Works

##### Converts Resistance Into a Map
"I'm not sure" is a closed door.
"What would need to happen for you to be sure?" opens it.
The resistance becomes a set of conditions. And conditions can be addressed.

##### Gets the Prospect to Define the Path to Yes
We don't have to guess what "yes" looks like. We ask.
Whatever they tell us becomes the roadmap.
Now we're solving a defined problem rather than pitching against a vague objection.

##### Creates a Conditional Commitment
When someone says "I'd need to see XYZ before I could move forward," they've implicitly agreed: if XYZ is true, they will move forward.
That conditional commitment puts them one step closer to yes before we've done any more convincing.

##### Surfaces Hidden Objections Early
The answer to this question often reveals objections that would have appeared much later.
Better to find them now than at the close.
Every condition they name is an objection we can handle while we still have time.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Turns resistance into a list of addressable conditions
- Creates a clear roadmap to yes
- Produces conditional commitments from the prospect
- Surfaces hidden objections early
- Eliminates guesswork about what the prospect needs
- Creates a much cleaner path to close
