## Either / Or  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Creates the illusion of choice.
**Structure**: `Would you prefer {Option A} or {Option B}?`
> ****

##### Do you prefer {x} or {y}?

##### Examples
- Do you prefer credit card or wire transfer? 
- Would Tuesday at 10am work or is Thursday at 2pm better? 
- Would you prefer the monthly plan or the annual plan?
- Is the challenge primarily strategic, or operational?

##### Variations
Would you rather {option A} or {option B}?
---
Is the issue more {A} or {B}?
---
Would you start with {A} or {B}?

#### Why It Works

##### Replaces Yes/No With Choice
A yes/no question asks:
> Will you do it?
An either/or question asks:
> Which way would you like to do it?
The conversation shifts from deciding whether to participate to deciding how to participate.

##### Reduces Decision Fatigue
Many people struggle with unlimited options.
They do much better when presented with a smaller set of choices.
Instead of:
> What do you want to do?
We ask:
> Would you rather start with lead generation or conversion?
The conversation becomes easier because the search space becomes smaller.

##### Creates Clarity
The strongest use of this pattern is in it's diagnosis.
When someone is overwhelmed, confused, or rambling, narrowing the possibilities often creates immediate clarity.
For example:
> Is the problem that you don't know what to do, or that you're not doing what you already know?
That question can save twenty minutes of conversation.

##### Forces Comparison
Many people evaluate options in isolation.
This pattern forces side-by-side evaluation.
Instead of:
> Should I hire someone?
We get:
> Would you rather hire someone or continue doing it yourself?
Now the decision becomes clearer because both options are visible simultaneously.

##### Reveals Preferences
Sometimes people don't know what they want until they see alternatives.
When presented with two choices, preferences emerge naturally.

##### When It Backfires
This pattern becomes dangerous when the other person feels their choices are being artificially restricted.
For example:
> Would you like to move forward today or tomorrow?
If the prospect is thinking:
> I don't even know if I want to move forward yet.
They'll immediately notice the missing option. Instead of helping the conversation, the pattern creates resistance because it feels like you're trying to steer them somewhere they haven't agreed to go.
The moment someone feels manipulated, they stop evaluating the choices and start evaluating your intentions.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Narrows choices.
- Creates clarity.
- Simplifies decisions.
- Reveals preferences.
- Reduces overwhelm.
- Creates movement.
- Helps diagnose problems.
- Encourages comparison.
- Makes conversations more efficient.

## Embedded Commands  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Hides a command from the conscious mind. 
**Structure**: `You don't have to {desired action} right now…`
> ****

##### You don't have to {desired action} right now…

##### Examples
1. "You don't have to immediately accept this."
1. "I'm not saying you'll become successful overnight."
1. "Whether or not you decide to move forward..."
1. You don’t have to buy right now… 

#### Why It Works
Most commands trigger evaluation.
> Buy now.
> Decide now.
> Take action.
The conscious mind immediately recognizes a command and decides whether to comply.
Embedded commands hide the command inside a larger sentence.
Instead of:
> Decide now.
We get:
> You don't have to decide now.
The conscious mind processes the entire sentence.
The unconscious mind still receives:
> Decide now.
The command is present, but it arrives indirectly.

##### Repetition Creates Familiarity
One of the biggest mistakes people make is treating embedded commands as a one-time technique.
The real power comes from repetition.
If the same command appears repeatedly throughout a conversation, it starts becoming familiar.
For example:
> You don't have to decide right now.
> Whether or not you decide today...
> As you decide what's best for you...
> Before you decide...
> When you decide...
The listener keeps encountering:
> Decide.
> Decide.
> Decide.
> Decide.
The command becomes increasingly familiar and comfortable.

##### The Brain Focuses on the Action
One reason embedded commands are so effective is that attention naturally moves toward the action itself.
Consider:
> Don't think about a pink elephant.
Most people immediately think about a pink elephant.
The mind must first represent the idea before it can process the negation.
Similarly:
> You don't have to buy right now.
The mind still processes:
> Buy right now.
The action receives attention even though it appears inside a negation.

##### Preserves Autonomy
Direct commands often create resistance.
Embedded commands preserve choice.
Instead of:
> Buy now.
We get:
> You don't have to buy right now.
The listener retains freedom.
The suggestion feels less forceful.
People rarely resist freedom.

##### The Word "Now" Carries Weight
Few words do more work than:
> Now.
Now directs attention to the present moment.
Now implies immediacy.
Now creates action orientation.
Now interrupts delay.
Compare:
> You don't have to decide.
Versus:
> You don't have to decide now.
The second version subtly directs attention toward the act of deciding in the present moment.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Introduces commands without resistance.
- Creates familiarity through repetition.
- Directs attention toward desired actions.
- Reduces reactance.
- Makes suggestions feel natural.
- Encourages present-moment action.
- Allows ideas to be revisited repeatedly throughout a conversation.

#### Phonological Marking
A phonological marker occurs when the embedded command is delivered differently than the surrounding language.
For example:
> You don't have to DECIDE NOW...
The words may remain inside the sentence, but the delivery changes.
The speaker might:
- lower their voice
- raise their voice
- slow down
- pause before and after
- change rhythm
- change emphasis
- alter tonality
The command becomes subtly distinct from the surrounding language.
Not enough to consciously stand out. But just enough to be different.
The brain is constantly looking for differences.
If everything is delivered with the same tonality, nothing stands out.
The moment the delivery changes, attention is drawn to it.
A slight pause.
A slower pace.
A softer voice.
A change in emphasis.

## Frames  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Creates a context to make your communication most effective.
**Structure**: `{Situation} is {claim} because {context}. `
A frame is the context that is wrapped around the communication. 
To be as persuasive as possible, you try to put the best context  around what you are communicating. 
Different frames can be effective or disastrous depending upon when you use them. 
But most people don’t even attempt to control a frame at all - and then wonder why they often don’t get what they want when trying to influence a conversation. 
To understand frames, let’s take a casual encounter every child has had. 
Mom pours a glass of milk and gives it to the child and says: “Don’t spill it.”
She’s just framed the communication negatively. 
Instead, she could’ve said “be careful with the milk.”
Which is better?
In this specific circumstance, a positive phrase is better because you are describing the action you want to occur. 
Whereas telling a kid to not spill the milk makes the kids think of spilling the milk. 
Another example - if you tell a patient they have an 80% survival rate on a surgery, they are more likely to opt for it than if it has a 20% fatality rate… even though those two data points mean the same thing. 
It’s only the framing that changes. 
And there are a lot of frames.

## Presuppositions  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Assumes a desired outcome, decision or event as fact
**Structure**: `Ask a question that assumes {desired outcome, decision, action, or future state} has already occurred or will occur.`
> ****

##### Ask a question that assumes {desired outcome, decision, action, or future state} has already occurred or will occur.

##### Examples
- "When would you like to start?"
- "Which of these results are you most excited to get first?"
- "How much of the extra revenue will you reinvest back into growth?"
- "How quickly do you want to see the pipeline numbers shift?"
- "What will you do with the twelve hours a week you get back?"
- "After we fix the follow-up problem, which bottleneck do you want to tackle next?"
- "How are you planning to handle the increased capacity once the close rate improves?"

#### Why It Works

##### Bypasses the Yes/No Decision
Most questions open a debate. The listener decides whether to participate.
Presuppositions skip the debate entirely.
Instead of: "Would you like to start?" we ask: "When would you like to start?"
The decision to act is no longer on the table. Only the timing is.

##### The Mind Answers the Question It's Asked
When we hear a question, the brain automatically searches for an answer.
If we ask "When would you like to start?", the listener's brain starts looking for a time,  not a reason to say no, not a defense, just a time.
The question directs mental energy toward movement, not resistance.

##### Uses Certainty to Create Certainty
Presuppositions communicate confidence.
When we speak as if an outcome is already decided, the listener picks up on that certainty. Uncertainty is contagious. So is confidence.
If we sound unsure, they feel unsure. If we sound certain, the certainty transfers.

##### Creates Forward Momentum
Every presupposition moves the conversation forward. Instead of defending a position, both parties are already discussing what happens next.
The conversation feels natural and progressive rather than a negotiation.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Skips the debate over whether to act
- Directs attention to details rather than decisions
- Communicates confidence and certainty
- Creates conversational momentum
- Makes the desired outcome feel inevitable
- Reduces decision friction

## Tie-down  [Building Block]
**Summary**: Gets listener to commit to the conclusion you want
**Structure**: `{Statement}, isn't it?`
> ****

##### {Statement that both parties know to be true}, {isn't it / wouldn't it / haven't you / doesn't it}?

##### Examples
- "Getting more predictable revenue would take a lot of pressure off, wouldn't it?"
- "You've been sitting with this problem longer than you'd like, haven't you?"
- "Finding a reliable way to fill the pipeline every month would change the game for you, wouldn't it?"
- "Solving this before Q4 would make the whole quarter feel different, wouldn't it?"
- "That kind of certainty is exactly what you've been missing, isn't it?"
Note: you can place it at the beginning or middle of a sentence as well:
- Isn’t it easy to follow along?
- It’s easy to follow along, isn’t it, especially when given clear instructions?

#### Why It Works

##### Creates a Pattern of Agreement
Each "yes" builds a small commitment. Small commitments stack.
The more someone says yes, the more natural it feels to keep saying yes. The pattern creates momentum in the direction you want to go.

##### Invites Confirmation, Not Debate
Most questions open a decision. A tie-down closes one.
Instead of asking whether something is true, we ask them to confirm what both of us already know to be true.
"Do you want more predictable revenue?" invites debate.
"Predictable revenue would make a big difference for you, wouldn't it?" invites agreement.

##### The Question Mark Carries Social Weight
When we add "isn't it?" or "wouldn't it?" to a statement, we signal that we expect agreement.
That expectation carries social pressure. Most people don't push back.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Creates a pattern of micro-agreements that build momentum
- Communicates confidence in the value being discussed
- Makes the listener feel heard and understood
- Creates internal pressure to remain consistent
- Prevents sudden reversal at the end of the conversation
- Frames negative outcomes as undesirable without forcing the point
