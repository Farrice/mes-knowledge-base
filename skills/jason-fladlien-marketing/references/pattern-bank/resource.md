## Decision Primer  [Resource]
**Summary**: Prepares for the decision about to occur. 
**Structure**: `Before you {desired decision}, first {mental prerequisite}.`
> ****

##### Before you {desired decision}, first {easy internal action}.

##### Examples
- Before you make a decision, imagine you've already implemented it and ask yourself what would happen next.
- Before you commit, think about what would need to occur for this to become one of your best business decisions.
- Before you sign up, find the part of yourself that is most excited for the new future you’re about to enroll in.
- Before saying yes today, first congratulate yourself on accepting a new and exciting journey you’re about to go on. 

#### Why It Works

##### Assumes the Decision
The pattern quietly presupposes that the decision is going to happen.
> Before you decide...
Not:
> If you decide...
The mind is subtly moved past the question of whether a decision will occur and toward what should happen before it occurs.

##### Uses One Yes to Create Another
The brilliance of this pattern is in the request of the tiny action that comes before the decision.
For example:
> Before you sign up, find the part of yourself that is most excited for the new future you’re about to enroll in.
The yes of finding the part of yourself that is most excited is an attractive prospect. Easy to say yes to. Fun, even. And when you say yes to that, you also say yes to the bigger decision (signing up). 

##### Links to an Existing Future
This is very similar to telling a child: 
> Turn off the lights after you take the trash out. 
We link a request to an action that is expected to happen anyway.
The trash is already being taken out.
The lights get attached to the behavior.
We treat the future decision as the thing that is already happening.
Then we attach a smaller action to it.
And once the smaller action is accepted, the larger action feels more natural too.

##### Creates a Mental On-Ramp
Many decisions feel large.
Many prerequisite actions feel tiny.
Instead of asking someone to jump directly into the decision, we give them a small mental step first.
Imagine.
Consider.
Reflect.
Picture.
Remember.
Evaluate.
The mind enters through the small door and often ends up arriving at the larger conclusion on its own.

##### Reduces Resistance
A direct request often creates pressure.
A mental prerequisite rarely does.
Very few people resist:
> Before you decide, think about...
The request feels reasonable.
The suggestion within it receives much less resistance.

##### Creates Consistency
Once we perform the prerequisite, we often begin behaving consistently with it.
If we imagine ourselves succeeding, we become more open to any action that can help with that success.
If we imagine ourselves implementing, we become more open to any action that helps with implementation.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Presupposes a future decision.
- Creates momentum toward action.
- Makes large decisions feel smaller.
- Reduces resistance.
- Creates consistency.
- Gets prospects mentally involved.
- Moves attention away from whether and toward how.

## How / When  [Resource]
**Summary**: Reveals the inner process on how decisions are made. 
**Structure**: `How do you know when {internal process}?`
> ****

##### How do you know when {internal process}?

##### Examples
- How will you know when you've found the right solution?
- How do you know when to procrastinate? 
- How do you know when you’re motivated versus excited? 
- How do you know when something is a good decision? 
- How do you know when things aren’t going to plan? 
- How do you know when you have an adequate amount of confidence to move forward? 
- How do you know when to listen to your heart and when to listen to your head? 

#### Why It Works

##### Assumes the Process Exists
This is one of the most elegant aspects of the pattern.
The question does not ask whether a process exists.
It assumes one exists.
For example:
> How do you know when you're motivated?
The question presupposes that somewhere inside the person's experience, there is already a process that distinguishes motivation from not-motivation.
The person may never have consciously examined it.
But the process is there.
The question simply directs attention toward it.

##### Brings the Unconscious Into Awareness
Most decisions are made unconsciously.
People often know they do something without knowing how they do it.
For example:
> How do you know when someone is trustworthy?
Most people can answer.
But very few have consciously studied the criteria they use.
The moment they start answering, they begin uncovering the internal rules they've been following automatically.

##### Reveals Decision Criteria
This pattern is incredibly valuable because it exposes the standards people use to make decisions.
Consider:
> How do you know when something is a good decision?
The answer might reveal:
- certainty
- emotion
- logic
- social proof
- intuition
- risk tolerance
Now you're no longer guessing how they make decisions.
They're telling you.

##### Avoids Defensiveness
Compare:
> Why are you procrastinating?
Versus:
> How do you know when to procrastinate?
The first feels accusatory.
The second feels curious.
The first assumes something is wrong.
The second assumes a process exists.
People naturally become more reflective and less defensive.

##### Moves From Content to Structure
Most conversations stay stuck at the content level.
For example:
> I procrastinate.
That's content.
This pattern investigates the structure underneath the behavior.
> How do you know when to procrastinate?
Now we're exploring:
- triggers
- cues
- criteria
- internal dialogue
- emotional states
That's where change happens.

##### Creates Self-Discovery
Another reason this pattern is so powerful is that people often discover things while answering.
You aren't telling them.
You're helping them observe themselves.
And insights that people discover themselves are usually more persuasive than insights imposed from the outside.

##### Exposes Faulty Criteria
Many limiting behaviors survive because their underlying criteria remain hidden.
For example:
> How do you know when you have enough confidence to move forward?
Someone might answer:
> When I'm 100% certain.
Now the real issue becomes visible.
The problem wasn't confidence.
The problem was an impossible standard.
Once exposed, the conscious mind can evaluate whether that criterion is useful.

##### Creates New Possibilities
Once a process becomes visible, it becomes editable.
What was previously automatic becomes a choice.
People can keep the parts that serve them.
They can modify the parts that don't.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Reveals unconscious decision-making.
- Exposes hidden criteria.
- Avoids judgment.
- Reduces defensiveness.
- Creates self-awareness.
- Uncovers limiting beliefs.
- Surfaces useful distinctions.
- Moves from behavior to process.
- Creates opportunities for change.

#### Why "How" Is Often Better Than "Why"
One of the classic observations in NLP is that "why" questions often produce justifications.
People explain.
Defend.
Rationalize.

## Magic Wand  [Resource]
**Summary**: Reveals ideal outcomes by removing limitations and censorship.
**Structure**: `If you could have everything you wanted with {outcome}, what would it look like?`
> ****

##### If you could have everything you wanted with {outcome}, what would it look like?

##### Examples
- If this generated the exact number of leads you wanted, what would that mean for the business?
- If this solved the problem completely, what would success look like?
- If we could build the ideal solution for you, what would it do?
- If you could design the perfect outcome, what would it include?
- If everything went your way over the next twelve months, what would happen?
- If this turned out better than expected, what would that look like?
- If the company became everything you hoped it would become, what would be true?
- If you could build the ideal business, how would it operate?
- If everything clicked, what would success actually look like?

#### Why It Works

##### Removes the Filter of Practicality
Most of the time, people censor their desires.
They ask "what's realistic?" before they ask "what do I want?"
This pattern bypasses the filter.
"If you could have everything you wanted" signals: don't censor, don't qualify, don't edit.
We get access to the real desire, not the managed version.

##### Reveals the True Target
The censored version of a goal is vague: "I want more revenue."
The uncensored version is specific: "I want to hit $350k a month so I can stop working Saturdays and take my family somewhere for a month in the summer."

##### Creates a Standard for the Solution
Once we know what the full dream looks like, we can position any solution against it.
"Everything you just described is exactly what we've helped other clients build."
Or: "We can't get you all the way there in year one, but here's what we can get you."

##### Generates Emotional Connection to the Outcome
Describing what you want in detail creates emotional attachment to it.
The listener isn't just thinking about the goal abstractly. They're experiencing it.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Removes the self-censorship that produces vague goals
- Reveals genuine underlying desires
- Creates a clear standard the solution can be positioned alongside 
- Generates emotional investment in the desired outcome
- Makes the prospect describe and own their own ideal future
- Produces much richer information than standard goal-oriented questions

#### Variations
- "If you had to describe the perfect version of this, what would you say?"
- "What would you want to be able to say about this time next year if everything went right?"
- "If this exceeded even the most wildest dream you ever had, what would have to happen?”

## Priming  [Resource]
**Summary**: Actives desire identity, mindset, belief or frame before an ask. 
**Structure**: `Do you consider yourself someone who {desired identity trait}?`
> ****

##### Do you consider yourself someone who {desired identity trait}?

##### Examples
Do you consider yourself an adventurous person?
Would you consider yourself truthful?
Are you someone who stands by your commitments?
Do you consider yourself resourceful?
Would you consider yourself a person who keeps commitments to yourself?
Do you consider yourself open-minded?
Are you someone who likes to stay ahead of problems rather than react to them?
Would you say you're someone who holds yourself to a high standard?
Are you someone who finishes what they start?
Do you consider yourself ambitious?

#### Why It Works

##### Identity Questions Activate Self-Concept
When we ask "do you consider yourself someone who X?", we direct attention to self-concept.
The listener evaluates themselves against the trait.
Almost universally, they identify with positive traits.
The activation of that identity then influences the decisions that follow.

##### Pre-Commitment Through Self-Description
When someone says "yes, I consider myself someone who makes decisions based on data," they've made a commitment.
Now if the data supports moving forward, they have to act consistently with the identity they just claimed.
Consistency is one of the most durable drivers of human behavior.

##### Almost Everyone Agrees
The traits we prime with are universally desirable.
Decisive. Action-oriented. Values results. Makes smart investments.
Nobody says "no, I don't consider myself that kind of person."
The agreement is nearly guaranteed, which means the pre-commitment lands every time.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Activates a positive identity before a key moment
- Creates self-consistency pressure
- Produces pre-commitment through identity agreement
- Makes the subsequent ask feel like an expression of who they are
- Builds rapport by acknowledging their positive qualities
- Creates internal alignment between identity and action

## Reawakening    [Resource]
**Summary**: Reconnects to a powerful past state or capability
**Structure**: `Can you remember a time when you felt {resourceful state}…?`
> ****

##### Can you remember a time when you felt {resourceful state}...?

##### Examples
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely certain... where making the decision felt almost effortless?
- Can you remember a time when you felt unstoppable... where everything seemed to be working in your favor?
- Can you remember a time when you felt totally clear about what to do next... where the path forward seemed obvious?
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely energized by your business... where you couldn't wait to get back to work?
- Can you remember a time when you felt totally decisive... where making difficult decisions felt surprisingly simple?
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely confident... where you surprised yourself with what you were capable of?
- Can you remember a time when you felt absolutely certain... where the outcome felt inevitable?
- Can you remember a time when you felt completely absorbed in something... where the outside world seemed to fade away?
- Can you remember a time when you became so interested in something that you lost track of time?
- Can you remember a time when you knew more than you realized you knew?
- Can you remember a time when you discovered you were capable of more than you expected?
- Can you remember a time when you were learning something quickly... and suddenly it all made sense?
- Can you remember a time when you were nervous about something... and then afterward wondered why you worried at all?

#### Why It Works

##### Memory Activates the State It Contains
When we remember a time when we felt confident, we briefly re-experience that confidence.
The brain partially re-creates the neurological experience of what it's remembering.
We don't have to give the listener confidence. We help them access it from within.

##### Resources Are Never Missing, Only Unavailable
Most limiting beliefs are built on the idea that a resource is absent.
"I'm not confident." "I'm not decisive." "I'm not good at this."
The Reawakening pattern challenges this at the root.
If we can find one memory of the state, we prove that the listener is capable of doing something they thought they weren’t. 

##### Sets the Stage for Transfer
Once the state is activated, we can bridge it to the current situation.
The implication is: "You had that feeling before. You can have it here."
The pattern creates the state so we can direct it toward what needs it most.

#### What It Accomplishes
- Activates resourceful states through memory
- Demonstrates that the resource already exists within the listener
- Creates internally generated motivation rather than externally provided inspiration
- Produces a state that can be transferred to the current decision
- Makes limiting beliefs about absent resources feel less certain
- Builds internal confidence and capability before a critical moment

## Speaking as a Child  [Resource]
**Summary**: Puts listener in a child state.
**Structure**: `As a child, wasn’t it amazing how…?`
> ****

#### As a child, wasn’t it amazing how…?

##### Examples:
- As a child, remember how normal it was to try something simply to see what happened?
- {speaking to you} As a child, wasn't it interesting how you could spend hours trying to figure something out without needing to know the answer first?
- As a child, wasn't persistence almost automatic?
- As a child, wasn't it fascinating how easily you could imagine possibilities?
- As a child, do you remember when a cardboard box could become a spaceship?

##### Purpose:
Reconnect someone with a resourceful psychological state they naturally possessed as a child, making that state more available in the present moment.

##### Mechanism:
By recalling childhood experiences, the listener temporarily accesses associated emotions, attitudes, and ways of thinking, often reducing resistance and increasing openness to new ideas.

### Why It Works

#### 1. It Reactivates a Resource State
A child naturally exhibits many states that are useful for influence:
- Curiosity
- Imagination
- Experimentation
- Persistence
- Wonder
- Playfulness
- Exploration
- Optimism
- Learning
For example:
> As a child, wasn't it fascinating how easily you could imagine possibilities?
The conscious content is about childhood.
The subconscious effect is:
> "Imagine possibilities."
The state becomes available in the present.
---

#### 2. It Temporarily Suspends Adult Limitations
Adults often ask:
> Will this work?
Children often ask:
> What happens if I try it?
Those are radically different frames.
Example:
> As a child, remember how normal it was to try something simply to see what happened?
This gently moves someone from:
> Evaluation
to
> Exploration
which is often where persuasion becomes easier.
---

#### 3. It Provides Evidence
One reason people resist new behaviors is:
> "That's not who I am."
This pattern provides counter-evidence.
Example:
> As a child, wasn't persistence almost automatic?
Now persistence isn't foreign.
They've already been persistent before.
The pattern says:
> You already possess this quality.
You may simply not be using it currently.
---

#### 4. It Accesses Episodic Memory
When people remember experiences, they often partially re-experience them.
Example:
> Do you remember when a cardboard box could become a spaceship?
This reactivates 
- imagination
- possibility
- creativity
Those emotions become more accessible during the conversation.

#### 5. It Lowers Defensiveness
If I say:
> You need to be more creative.
Resistance.
If I say:
> As a child, wasn't it amazing how easily ideas came to you?
No resistance.
The same suggestion enters indirectly.

### The Power of States
The states are in italics:
A curious person evaluates differently than a skeptical person.
A creative person evaluates differently than a fearful person.
A playful person evaluates differently than a defensive person.
The pattern changes the state through which information is processed. We are all both curious and skeptical, creative and fearful, playful and defensive… it just matters which state we are in.

#### MILTON ALERT
I first discovered this pattern from Milton Erickison. 
One reason Erickson would occasionally use phrases like:
> "Speaking to you as a child..."
is that the statement is not fully specified.
The conscious mind often wants to know:
- Am I the child?
- Is he speaking about himself as a child?
- Is he speaking to my childhood self?
- Is he speaking about children generally?
The ambiguity creates a moment where the listener searches for meaning.
During that search, attention shifts inward.
Instead of analyzing the speaker's argument, the listener begins exploring their own memories, associations, and experiences.
In Ericksonian hypnosis, this kind of ambiguity was often useful because the conscious mind becomes occupied trying to resolve the meaning while the unconscious mind begins generating its own interpretations.

##### Regression to an Earlier Version of the Self
Another reason Erickson frequently referenced childhood is that childhood represents a time before many limitations became fully established.
An adult might think:
> "I've always been shy."
Yet childhood memories often contain evidence to the contrary.
A person who currently believes they lack confidence may remember:
- learning to ride a bike
- climbing a tree
- making new friends
- performing in front of a class
The childhood reference helps reconnect them with experiences that existed before the limiting narrative became dominant.
