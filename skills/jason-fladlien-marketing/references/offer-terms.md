# Jason Fladlien Offer TERMS: Canonical Runtime Reference

## Purpose and Evidence Boundary

TERMS evaluates an offer through the burden it places on the buyer: **Time, Effort, Routine, Money, and Status**. It is a diagnostic and design layer. It cannot rescue a bad game, weak proof, unsafe delivery, or an offer nobody wants.

## Primary-Burden Selection

Assess all 18 levers, but change no more than three. Select one primary burden using these **system-derived** dimensions:

1. **Buyer consequence:** How directly does the burden block purchase, adoption, use, or the promised outcome?
2. **Evidence strength:** Is it a guess, an artifact-backed inference, direct buyer language, or repeated observed behavior?
3. **Operator control:** Can the offer owner change it now without violating safety, truth, fulfillment, or preservation locks?

Use a 0–3 working score per dimension only to force comparison; the score is not Jason’s claim and must not override judgment. A `BLOCKER` can override arithmetic. An `UNKNOWN` cannot become primary merely because it feels important. Return one primary burden and at most two supporting issues.

### Change-Class Calibration

Use the smallest honest overall decision label:

- `PRESERVE`: no accepted offer decision changes.
- `PATCH`: accepted changes repair burden, sequence, responsibility, or safeguards while preserving the core buyer, purchased outcome, scope, delivery model, and commercial architecture.
- `REBUILD`: accepted changes materially alter at least one of those core dimensions.
- `HOLD`: a truth, authority, evidence, safety, consent, fulfillment, or owner-decision gap blocks responsible use.

When the Revenue Offer Agent and TERMS converge on the same accepted changes, framework coverage and extra held ideas are not additional value. Prefer the narrower label and report comparative uplift as unproven unless the final offer decision is materially better.

## Time: RAW

### Recover (`RAW-R`, 03:30–06:47)

**Mechanic:** Study the buyer’s real day and recover capacity before asking for new time.

Inspect:

- A representative day and week, including calendar, phone use, problem-solving time, transitions, and decompression.
- Work the buyer believes is necessary but does not produce the promised result.
- Existing work the offer can remove, absorb, automate, combine, or defer.

Design move: explicitly name time returned, then request only the portion needed for the new behavior. Never call essential recovery, caregiving, safety, or regulated work “waste.”

Pass evidence: the offer removes or absorbs named work before adding work.

### Available (`RAW-A`, 06:47–09:15)

**Mechanic:** Design the unlimited-time ideal, then preserve the useful outcome with materially less time and compress again.

Inspect:

- The time the complete theoretical solution would require.
- The smallest dosage that protects the core result, safety, and quality.
- What can be done for the buyer, pre-decided, templatized, batched, or delivered just in time.

Jason’s 95% and 90% examples are source-reported heuristics, not guaranteed ratios. Do not remove effort that is causally necessary, legally required, or safety-critical.

Pass evidence: the offer states what was compressed, what remains essential, and why the protected outcome is still credible.

### Win (`RAW-W`, 09:15–11:42)

**Mechanic:** Design three distinct wins:

1. **Initial decision win:** what becomes better or clearer immediately after choosing.
2. **First tangible win:** the earliest observable progress created during use.
3. **Ideal win:** the full credible outcome, with conditions and timing.

Do not rename the first tangible win an “implementation win” and attribute that phrase to Jason. Future-pacing does not prove the win will occur.

Pass evidence: every win has a real event, an honest time horizon, and a boundary between process progress and outcome proof.

## Effort: FAT

### Feel (`FAT-F`, 12:41–16:20)

**Mechanic:** Reduce emotional load without pretending emotion disappears.

Inspect shame, fear, sadness, uncertainty, embarrassment, identity threat, and the competence the buyer already has. Remove unnecessary exposure; clarify what happens if they struggle; validate usable strengths; and relabel an exaggerated difficulty only when evidence supports the reframe.

Reject shame-based urgency, false reassurance, trauma diagnosis, emotional invalidation, and promises that the buyer must become fearless.

Pass evidence: the buyer can act while uncertainty remains and knows what support or exit exists.

### Act (`FAT-A`, 16:20–19:26)

**Mechanic:** Delete features, clicks, taps, steps, movements, and handoffs until every remaining action has a result-producing role.

Inspect the entire path from interest to purchase to first value to repeat use. Distinguish core action, evidence collection, compliance requirement, optional enhancement, and seller convenience.

Pass evidence: each kept action has a named causal or safety role; optional complexity is hidden or removed.

### Thinking (`FAT-T`, 19:26–22:45)

**Mechanic:** Cut the number of decisions, the need for interpretation, the jargon, and the sequencing load. Reduce what the buyer must remember at once.

Inspect:

- How many decisions arrive at once.
- Whether the buyer can repeat the next step in one sentence.
- Whether defaults, examples, pre-decisions, or progressive disclosure can remove thought without removing agency.

The source label is **Thinking**. “Think” is allowed only as shorthand. Do not treat buyers as incapable or hide consequential terms.

Pass evidence: one next decision is obvious, consequential choices remain visible, and language survives the repeat-back test.

## Routine: HOP

### Habit (`HOP-H`, 23:47–25:43)

**Mechanic:** Preserve what already works, release a habit that is genuinely easy to change, and require no more than one pivotal new habit at a time.

Inspect existing routines, stable identity cues, behavior the offer would displace, and the one recurring action with the greatest effect.

Pass evidence: the offer names the single habit change, what existing behavior supports it, and what does not need to change.

### Order (`HOP-O`, 25:43–28:49)

**Mechanic:** Attach action to an existing cue, identify what happens first, and chunk work to the minimum threshold.

Design:

- A specific cue or preceding event.
- A first action small enough to start.
- A visible sequence of meaningful chunks.
- A clear resumption point after interruption.

Pass evidence: the buyer knows “when X happens, do Y,” and the first chunk produces or reveals progress.

### Process (`HOP-P`, 28:49–30:26)

**Mechanic:** Change the context, conditions, setting, or role. Make the desired behavior feel natural. This is context over content, not merely a clearer SOP.

Inspect location, defaults, access, tools, social setting, time of day, role expectations, and who is present. Prefer changing the environment over adding another lesson.

Pass evidence: a named context change makes the desired action easier without demanding more motivation.

## Money: FAVOR

### Free (`FAVOR-F`, 31:13–34:41)

**Mechanic:** Make a familiar component buyers normally expect to pay for feel free inside the paid offer so the comparison is easy to understand.

“Free” is a comparison frame, not permission to disguise costs. State total economics, eligibility, and trade-offs. Do not manufacture an inflated component price or call required core delivery a bonus merely to use the word free.

Pass evidence: the free component is legitimately included, familiar enough to compare, and economically disclosed.

### Anchor (`FAVOR-A`, 34:41–36:39)

**Mechanic:** Control the first comparison responsibly: show full price before installments and compare the visible purchase cost with the grounded cost of leaving the problem unresolved.

Allowed anchors: documented market rate, actual component price, credible internal cost, buyer-supplied current cost, or full commitment before payment schedule. Label assumptions.

Reject invented “value” totals, unverified market rates, fear inflation, and treating an installment as the full price.

Pass evidence: a reviewer can trace every number and understand total commitment.

### Value (`FAVOR-V`, 36:39–38:39)

**Mechanic:** Make value clear for a specific segment and easy to demonstrate. When a competitor census exists, use it to make the offer responsibly incomparable.

Inspect what this buyer already values, the expensive workaround being replaced, how quickly the benefit can be demonstrated, and whether added modality increases or reduces adoption.

Pass evidence: the intended segment can explain why the offer matters in its own situation without a long seller explanation.

### Outcome (`FAVOR-O`, 38:39–40:17)

**Mechanic:** Increase certainty by shrinking the initial promise and starting with the easiest legitimate market to serve; expand only from results.

Jason’s examples are source-reported heuristics, not a statistical law. Do not disguise a smaller outcome as the full transformation or generalize results beyond the evidence population.

Pass evidence: the first promise is both meaningful and supportable, and any expansion gate names the proof required.

### Resistance (`FAVOR-R`, 40:17–42:31)

**Mechanic:** Prefer willing-and-able buyers who already invest in solving the problem rather than discounting for everyone.

Evidence may include prior category spend, current workaround cost, active search, authority to buy, and demonstrated intent. Reject targeting based on desperation, financial vulnerability, stereotypes, or reckless risk tolerance.

Pass evidence: segment selection reflects ability, intent, and fit rather than exploitability.

## Status: RISE

### Relative (`RISE-R`, 43:30–45:36)

**Mechanic:** Give the buyer a truthful advantage against a relevant comparison set or reframe a perceived weakness into a legitimate strength.

Name the actual comparison set and why the advantage matters. Prefer elevation over enemy manufacture. Reject group denigration, false superiority, and status claims detached from the outcome.

Pass evidence: the advantage is specific, supportable, and useful to the buyer’s result.

### Internal (`RISE-I`, 45:36–48:04)

**Mechanic:** Repair unjustified negative self-judgment, validate a positive identity without exaggeration, and permit progress while fear remains.

Inspect the identity the buyer believes success requires and the identity evidence they already possess. Do not promise identity transformation as a substitute for delivery.

Pass evidence: the buyer is allowed to begin as they are and can point to a truthful competence or decision.

### Social (`RISE-S`, 48:04–50:12)

**Mechanic:** Increase connection and reduce isolation through care, visibility, and positive-intent check-ins.

A positive-intent check-in communicates concern, names available help, and gives the buyer an easy re-entry or exit. It does not ask why they failed to use what they bought, shame non-use, or compel community participation.

Pass evidence: the buyer has a named human or group connection, a respectful check-in trigger, and a non-coercive response path.

### External (`RISE-E`, 50:12–52:34)

**Mechanic:** Design earned visible progress, acknowledgements, status signals, and naturally captureable moments.

Keep five events separate:

1. **Usage:** the buyer performed an action.
2. **Observed outcome:** a result or meaningful progress occurred.
3. **Voluntary sharing:** the buyer chose to share it.
4. **Permission:** the buyer approved a named use, audience, duration, and level of identification.
5. **External reuse:** the seller used it within that permission.

Reject fake badges, vanity milestones, automatic screenshot harvesting, compelled sharing, implied testimonial consent, and claims broader than the captured event.

Pass evidence: the proof object is earned, accurately labeled, permissioned, and no broader than what it shows.

## Cross-Term Non-Regression

Every proposed change must answer:

- Which burden does this reduce?
- What evidence supports that diagnosis?
- What new burden might it create in the other four terms?
- What essential quality, safety, consent, or fulfillment constraint must remain?
- Is the net change better for this buyer, or merely easier to sell?

Examples of regressions:

- Shorter delivery that lowers outcome quality.
- More formats that increase cognitive load and routine disruption.
- A “free” component that hides a higher total commitment.
- Status mechanics that create shame or coerced proof.
- A smaller promise that no longer solves a meaningful problem.

Reject or offset a change when its new burden exceeds the burden removed.

## Adoption and Proof Trigger

Invoke `offer-adoption-and-proof-loop` only when one or more are true:

- Time to first tangible win is unclear or too long.
- The buyer must form or preserve a recurring behavior.
- Non-use, drop-off, retention, or re-entry is a material risk.
- Social connection is part of the outcome or compliance mechanism.
- Real progress exists or is expected but proof capture and permission are undefined.

Do not invoke it merely to add a badge, testimonial request, community, or onboarding screen.

Once invoked, activate only the submodules supported by the named trigger. Three-win design, routine hinge, check-in, visible progress, and permission are independent modules. Return `NOT APPLICABLE — no evidence` for the rest. Every accepted module-level offer change counts inside the public workflow’s global three-change ceiling.

## Result-State Boundary

Report three verdicts separately:

| Verdict | Question |
|---|---|
| Component validity | Did the diagnostic produce a better, evidence-respecting offer decision on the fixtures? |
| Cold-start portability | Can a fresh operator run it from the written inputs without hidden context? |
| Market proof | Has this exact offer progressed through real commercial interaction? |

Commercial states remain distinct:

- `sent`: exposed to a real intended buyer.
- `held`: substantive buyer interaction occurred.
- `sold`: buyer made a commitment.
- `collected`: cleared revenue was received.

Source claims, static fixtures, adoption events, screenshots, and operator approval do not substitute for these states.
