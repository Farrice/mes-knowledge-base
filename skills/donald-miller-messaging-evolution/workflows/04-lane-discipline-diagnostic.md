# 04 — Lane-Discipline Diagnostic

Growth tempts every founder to widen the net. The counter-intuitive physics: **a wider net catches fewer fish** — *"the broader the net, the bigger the holes in between the rope. The smaller the net, the more fish you catch."* This diagnostic is the gate you run before every "what if we also..." expansion.

## Pre-Flight Gate

Run this when:
- A new revenue stream, audience, or product is "coming across the desk" and feels like easy money.
- The brand is getting leads it can't serve and is tempted to monetize them (Belay's 9,950 un-hireable resumes/month).
- Positioning feels like it's blurring — the one-liner no longer fits everything the company does.
- Before any "let's also become a..." decision.

**Do NOT** use this to *write* the positioning — that's `donald-miller-storybrand/04-one-liner-generator`. This *defends* it.

## Skill Acquisition

Load `genius.md` Pattern 3 (Lane Discipline) + the "lane flooding" anti-pattern + the "expansion that goes well still costs you" hidden-knowledge note.

## Execution

**Step 1 — State the lane in one sentence.** Who are we, what one problem do we solve, what do we refuse to be? Belay: *"We are fractional. We are not on site. We are not full-time. We are not these things. This is our lane."* If the lane can't be stated in a sentence, the diagnostic's first job is to recover it.

**Step 1.5 — Apply the rule of three (the cognitive ceiling).** Miller, *How to Grow Your Small Business*: *"The human brain has trouble prioritizing more than three objectives at a time. The adage is true: if you prioritize everything, you prioritize nothing."* Force the brand to name its **three economic objectives** — the three dominant ways it makes money. Anything the proposed expansion adds is a *fourth* priority competing for a slot that cognitively does not exist. If the lane already has three live objectives, a new one doesn't add reach — it evicts an existing priority. This is the hard constraint beneath the net metaphor: the catch falls when attention exceeds three.

**Step 1.7 — Check the rule of proportion (the scaling test).** Lane discipline isn't only "don't add a new lane" — it's "don't grow one part out of proportion to the rest." Miller's airplane: *"In order to grow your business safely, you'll want to grow its parts in proportion to each other."* A new product (wing) with no marketing/sales engine to move it, or an expansion that bloats overhead (the body) before the engines can carry the weight, crashes the plane — *"it will fly for a little while and then crash."* Ask: does this expansion grow in proportion to the engine that has to sell it, or is it a heavier body with the same wings?

**Step 2 — Run the net test on the proposed expansion.** Ask the three questions:
1. **Does it blur "who we are"?** Would adding this make the one-liner less true or more generic?
2. **Does it widen the net?** Are we now "fishing more ocean" — reaching a broader, less-qualified audience? More reach ≠ more catch.
3. **What confusion does it create?** Miller: adjacent revenue *"always created confusion — sometimes so much that the money's not worth it."* Estimate the confusion cost as a real line item.

**Step 3 — The "smell money" check.** Is the motivation *"there's money on the other side of the fence"*? Name it honestly. Smelling money is the single most common trigger for lane-destroying expansions, and recognizing it is half the discipline.

**Step 4 — Classify the expansion:**
- **Same lane, new expression** → SAFE. (Belay's "the right hire right now" extended *within* identity to non-EA roles — same lane, wider expression.)
- **New lane, separate house** → ALLOWED only if siloed as a distinct brand/team (route to `05-message-siloing-system`; Belay's financial-solutions arm). Never run two lanes through one message.
- **New lane, same message** → REFUSE. This floods the river.

**Step 4.5 — Run it through the product brief (doubt is your friend).** If the expansion survives the net test, do NOT green-light it on enthusiasm. Miller's product brief is a deliberate vetting gate: *"The purpose of a product brief is to create doubt. Doubt, it turns out, is your friend. If your team's ideas can make it through the gauntlet of questioning... the idea is much more likely to evolve and ultimately positively affect the bottom line."* Put the new product through the "wind tunnel" before launch — interrogate: will it confuse the existing message? interfere with current revenue? bloat overhead? The brief slows the run-and-gun founder down by a week and saves a launch that would "hit the market with a thud."

**Step 4.5b — Apply "kill your darlings" to the existing lane.** Lane discipline cuts both directions. Before adding anything, ask what to *remove*: *"In storytelling there is a famous phrase that applies just as strongly to business: kill your darlings... If you can cut products and streamline your product offering, do it today."* An overloaded lane is as off-position as a blurred one. Surface the unprofitable/low-demand products dragging the wing and recommend cutting them — *unless* they're genuine loss-leaders that pull profitable purchases.

**Step 5 — Output the verdict.** Hold, silo, or refuse — with the net-test reasoning and, if "hold," the script for telling the team *why* (lane discipline must be said out loud: *"Nope, we're staying in here."*).

## Content Type Adaptations

| Expansion type | Lane emphasis |
|---|---|
| New product line | Same-lane-new-expression vs. new-lane-separate-house test |
| New audience segment | Net width — are we losing qualification for reach? |
| Monetizing surplus leads | The 9,950 test — surplus ≠ obligation to monetize |
| Geographic / channel expansion | Does the core message survive the new context unblurred? |
| Acquisition / merger | Pair with `06-rebrand-patience-protocol` (two lanes = 3-year integration cost) |

## Output Requirements

- **The lane**, in one sentence (who we are / one problem / what we refuse).
- **The three economic objectives** (and whether the expansion would evict one).
- **Net-test scorecard** (blur? / width? / confusion cost?) + **proportion check** (does the engine grow to carry the new wing/body?).
- **"Smell money" honesty line** — is that the real driver?
- **Verdict**: Hold / Silo / Refuse, with reasoning; if not Refuse, the **product-brief questions** to clear before launch.
- **Kill-your-darlings list**: existing products to cut to keep the lane lean.
- If Hold: the **out-loud script** for the team on why we're staying in the lane.

## Quality Gate

Reject if (per `genius.md`):
- It approves an expansion on revenue alone without pricing the confusion cost.
- It green-lights a new product on enthusiasm without running the product brief (no manufactured doubt).
- It only adds and never asks what to cut (no kill-your-darlings pass).
- It runs a new lane through the existing message instead of siloing it.
- It treats more reach as more catch (the broad-net fallacy), or grows one part out of proportion.
- The lane was never stated, so there's nothing to defend.
