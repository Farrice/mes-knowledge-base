## Date-Sequence Messaging (Pattern 1 / Workflow 02)

> "We call this first date, second date, third date, fourth date messaging. In other words, there's things that you say to get them to go on a date with you and then on the date there are things that you say and you can't mess this up. If you say all the stuff that you say on the fourth date on the first date, they're not going to go out with you again."

> "The initial capture was 'I need somebody fractional' but then the objection was 'but how does this work when you don't live near me?'"

> "Did you keep your message simple on the landing page and the introduction to the product? Yeah, it was very very simple."

## Bridge Word (Pattern 2 / Workflow 03)

> "Did you know that Milwaukee tools when they released their line of electric tools, they didn't sell well? Because people were used to plugging in their tools or they were used to gas powered tools. And they just didn't think the quality was going to be there with a battery. And so a hockey stick happened in sales — sales exploded — when they started calling their electric tools fuel. They built a bridge from the old world to the new world with just a word. And all of a sudden these blue collar plumbers went, 'It's the same as gas. It's fuel.'"

> "What don't you like about the word virtual? I feel like... it undermines the human element and quality of person."

> "[managed service] I think it would require too much explanation. Might be too high cognitive load."

## Lane Discipline / The Net (Pattern 3 / Workflow 04)

> "The broader the net that you're trying to catch fish in, the bigger the holes in between the rope. So the smaller the net, the more fish you catch. The broader the net, the more ocean you fish and the more fish swim through the holes."

> "They smell money on the other side of the fence and they think, well, if we just move this fence, we can have some of that money."

> "We are fractional. We are not on site. We are not full-time. We are not these things. This is our lane. It's working well for us. Let's not create a flood in our river."

> "Have you figured out something to sell the 9,950 people you can't hire? ... Nope, we're staying in here."

> "It's hard to be known for solving two problems."

## Message Siloing (Pattern 4 / Workflow 05)

> "Silo your messaging. So, one message for the the finances, one message for the executive assistant, one message for the marketing assistants... And then take it a step further, we will have a message for a Donald Miller prospect that's different than a message from a Google prospect."

> "It's two completely different second dates. It's two completely different sales calls."

> "The year we separated those teams apart... it just grew. ... When we reduced the cognitive load on our internal team and on the customer, sales went up."

> "It's the reason that Procter & Gamble and Johnson & Johnson separate those brands and you have no idea what the mother company is. Otherwise it would be Procter & Gamble soap, Procter & Gamble chicken nuggets."

Supply-side / two-sided:
> "We have never had to spend a dollar to recruit... the message is exactly why we created the company... you mean I can take my career, put it aside, take everything I've learned and go do something for 20 hours a week from home? ... You can choose your journey." (6,000–10,000 resumes/month inbound.)

## Rebrand Patience / 3-Year Rule (Pattern 5 / Workflow 06)

> "When you make a decision like that you should not expect the ship to be righted for 3 years. And also 3 years only if you're aggressive. Only if you're constantly saying it."

> "It was 2017... we called it Mission Possible and we merged those two companies and became Belay... it was a very difficult year in the market... It took us a long time to get traction." (~18 months to recover.)

> "Your team has to be on board... when you realize they're not, it's very possible we can get this done. But if they're not on board you can't get it done."

> "Even if I say that today people don't realize Belay was EA Help."

## Abracadabra Testing Loop (Pattern 6 / Workflow 01)

> "It's literally just you're trying to find your abracadabra sometimes. You're trying to find what are the words that I say, and when I say them, people place orders. That's the game often in just messaging."

> "Play with your messaging until you find something that works. And keep going. Keep going."

> "VA became a very big thing... So then we changed our messaging to VAs. Then AI and automation came out and now a VA is a bot. So now we're back to EAs again."

> "[20 to 25%] of my time is literally just how are we going to word that?" — and the marketing team "spending 120% of their time" AB-testing wording.

> "We would be antiquated Blockbuster. I mean, we are fighting against becoming Blockbuster. We must constantly innovate. If we did not innovate and change and evolve, we would have lost people a long time ago."

## The Cognitive-Load Bridge (already covered by donald-miller-cognitive-load — included for context)

# SOURCE 2 — "How to Grow Your Small Business" (direct teaching / hardening pass)

## Relationship Stages = the Date Sequence (hardens Pattern 1 / Workflow 02)

> "All relationships, all of them, go through three phases. The first phase is curiosity. When we meet somebody we are curious about them... for one reason — they might be able to help us survive."

> "Phase two of a relationship is enlightenment... we begin to subconsciously test whether or not this person is actually able to help me survive. So if it's a dating relationship, we go on a few dates... that's what dating is, that's what experimenting with brands, that's what reading catalogues are — it's due diligence."

> "The third phase of relationships is commitment. And commitment is when you put skin in the game. You pull out your credit card or you get down on one knee and you say, 'Will you marry me?' ... commitment does not come fast."

> "The salesfunnel automates the curiosity and enlightenment phases of a relationship so that when that person now says 'I trust you,' they're willing to pull out their credit card and make a purchase."

## The 6-Part Second-Date Sales Formula (hardens Workflow 02)

> "Start with the problem the customer has. Don't start with your problem... Position your product as the solution to that problem. Give them a three-step plan on how to buy that product. Talk about the negative stakes that will happen if they don't buy it. Talk about the positive stakes that will happen if they do. And finally, in the last sentence, just ask them to buy it. If you do those five things in that order, you will close sales."

> "We took an hour. People wrote their emails... 'Hit send.' By the next day, we closed $2.4 million in sales... not because we coerced them or manipulated them — we made it extremely clear why you should buy this product. Clarity wins."

## The Rule of Three / Cognitive Ceiling (hardens Pattern 3 / Workflow 04)

> "You actually have three economic objectives. You figure out the three dominant ways that you're bringing revenue into this small business and you focus on those. Now you may have five, six, or seven, but the human mind has a limitation — it can really only prioritize three things... your mind will literally not prioritize more than three things."

> "When you take your eye off the fuel gauge and you don't realize you've got to keep putting gasoline in this engine in order for it to run — and gasoline is cash in the business world — you have a serious problem heading toward you."

# SOURCE 3 — *How to Grow Your Small Business* (the published book — PRIMARY)

## The 6-Part Second-Date Sales Formula + Color Key (hardens Workflow 02)

> "Start with the problem the customer has. Don't start with your problem. Start with their problem. Think about them. Position your product as the solution to that problem. Give them a three-step plan on how to buy that product. Talk about the negative stakes that will happen if they don't buy it. Talk about the positive stakes that will happen if they do. And finally, in the last sentence, just ask them to buy it. If you do those five things in that order, you will close sales."

> "The 'The Customer Is the Hero' color key looks like this: The customer's problem: **red**. Your product positioned as the solution: **purple**. The three- or four-step plan: **brown**. The negative consequence you are saving the customer from: **yellow**. The positive result your customer will experience: **blue**. Your call to action: **green**." (When a document is "covered in one color: purple" — all you've done is talk about yourself.)

> "Three-step plans work well; four-step plans work almost as well; five-step plans hardly work at all. A plan exists to let the customer know the journey from their problem to your solution will be easy."

> "When you present a plan to the customer, you essentially 'lift the fog' so they can see much more of the terrain they will be venturing into."

> The at-home chef (canonical example): "You know how most families don't eat together anymore? And when they do, they don't eat healthy. I'm an at-home chef..." → vs. the flat version "I'm an at-home chef. I come to your house and cook." Same job; only the second invites the customer into a story.

**Close the Client Kit (collateral) — the follow-up email has two jobs:**
> "1. To drive a deadline. 2. To initiate communication. Research shows over and over again that people are more likely to take action when there is a deadline involved... You don't want to just send the proposal and then go silent." (Sample subject line: "Friendly reminder: your proposal expires soon.")

## The Rule of Three / "If You Prioritize Everything, You Prioritize Nothing" (hardens Workflow 04)

> "The human brain has trouble prioritizing more than three objectives at a time. The adage is true: if you prioritize everything, you prioritize nothing."

> "You will find that Step One alone—rewriting your Mission Statement to include three economic priorities—will help you find the focus you need to grow revenue and improve morale."

## The Rule of Proportion (hardens Workflow 04)

> "In order to grow your business safely, you'll want to grow its parts in proportion to each other."

> "Sometimes, the business owner hires before the right and left engines can support the added weight. Other times, the business owner waits too long to hire... When we don't have the visual metaphor of the airplane in mind, we are still building an airplane; we are just building it wrong. If you build an airplane the wrong way, it won't fly. Or, worse, it will fly for a little while and then crash."

## Kill Your Darlings (hardens Workflow 04)

> "In storytelling, there is a famous phrase that applies just as strongly to business: Kill your darlings. This phrase has helped create many literary masterpieces and can help your business take off, too. I can't emphasize this enough: If you can cut products and streamline your product offering, do it today."

## The Product Brief — Doubt Is Your Friend (hardens Workflow 04)

> "The purpose of a product brief is to create doubt. Doubt, it turns out, is your friend. If you and your team's ideas can make it through the gauntlet of questioning involved in a product brief, the idea is much more likely to evolve and ultimately positively affect the bottom line."

> "When engineers design an airplane, they test wing designs in wind tunnels before ever installing them on an airplane... Put your products through the wind tunnel of the product brief before launching those products."

## The S-Curve — the Lifecycle Model (hardens Workflow 01)

> "Every successful business has come face-to-face with the 's-curve.' The s-curve follows a specific pattern—the business begins to grow, which is a great thing, and then a dreadful series of events that could make or break a company is put into action... The business owner is pulled out of their sweet spot... They spend too much time trying to put out fires, and the business starts to decline because the owner is managing problems rather than continuing to create the magic that grew the company."

> "'You need to professionalize your operation.' ... Until you professionalize your operation, its potential is limited."

> The airplane as decision-filter: "whenever we hired a new team member, I'd ask myself how we would offset their salary. Would this investment make the wings larger... or increase thrust... or enlarge the body of the airplane (increasing overhead and making it heavier, contributing to a crash)?"

> "Beware of looking successful without being successful." (Funded start-ups that build a pretty brand and bloated overhead before the engines can carry it: "an airplane with a massive body, tiny wings, weak engines.")
