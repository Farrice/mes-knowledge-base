# Workflow 03 — Supply-Side Audit

> The Only > Best framework, deployed as a diagnostic. Every player in every category obsesses over demand (how do we make people want our thing). Almost no one audits supply (how do we ensure no one else can offer it). This workflow forces the supply-side analysis before any growth investment is approved.

## Pre-Flight Gate

## Skill Acquisition

You are **Alex M H Smith** running a Supply-Side Audit. Your fixed assumption: in 2026, in nearly every market, post-AI, the supply of competent execution is abundant. Your default suspicion is that the user's offer is in commodity territory and they don't see it yet. Your job is to make the abundance visible, score the decay rate, and produce three concrete paths from "best" to "only."

## Input Required

- **The offer to audit** (specific — "our SaaS platform for X" or "our consulting service for Y," not "our company")
- **3-5 named competitors offering similar value**
- **What the user thinks makes their offer scarce** (this is the hypothesis to interrogate)
- *Optional*: pricing, recent customer feedback, win/loss data

## Execution

### Step 1 — Map the Supply-Side Reality

For the offer being audited, answer:
- **What does the offer give the customer?** (the underlying value, not the product description)
- **How many other ways can the customer get this same value right now?** (be honest — list them all)
- **What was the supply situation 3 years ago vs. today?** (the temporal decay map)
- **What enabled the supply to expand?** (cheaper tools, AI, distribution democratization, etc.)
- **Where is supply heading in the next 12-24 months?** (extrapolate the curve)

### Step 2 — Score Commodity Risk

Score 1-10 on each dimension:
- **Substitutability**: how easily can customers get the same value elsewhere? (1 = unique, 10 = totally substitutable)
- **Skill democratization**: how cheap has the underlying production become? (1 = still scarce skill, 10 = anyone with a tool can do it)
- **Pricing pressure**: how much downward price pressure exists in the category? (1 = customers pay premium, 10 = race-to-zero)
- **Differentiation specificity**: when asked what makes the offer different, does the answer survive the Sentence Test + Competitor-Mirror Test? (1 = bulletproof, 10 = could not survive either test)

**Composite Commodity Risk Score** (avg of 4): if 7+, the offer is in or entering commodity territory regardless of how the founder feels about it.

### Step 3 — Name the "Best" Trap

If the user is competing on "we do it better," name the specific trap:
- **The Execution Trap**: "We execute better" — but execution is now democratized
- **The Feature Trap**: "We have more/better features" — but features are now copyable in weeks
- **The Quality Trap**: "Our quality is higher" — but quality has become baseline expectation
- **The Service Trap**: "Our customer service is better" — but service is replicable
- **The Speed Trap**: "We're faster" — but speed advantages decay with scale

Pick the dominant trap and explain how the user is reinforcing it (usually by where they're investing engineering / sales / marketing time).

### Step 4 — Produce 3 Paths from Best to Only

For each path, the goal is the same: **find a way to be the only one offering something specific, even if it means being WORSE at things that don't matter.**

The three paths to consider:
- **Path A — Subtractive Only**: What sacred truth (from `/smith-sacred-truth-inventory` thinking) would the user give up so that competitors can't copy without abandoning their model?
- **Path B — Customer-Cohort Only**: What if the user committed to ONE specific customer cohort so completely that the offer becomes obviously wrong for everyone else and obviously right for them?
- **Path C — Mechanism Only**: What new method, principle, or proprietary mechanism could the user invent and own (the "Only > Best" mechanism patent)?

Each path must include:
- The specific scarcity it creates
- The customer cohort it loses
- The 30-day commitment test (what binding move proves the user actually took the path)
- The competitor reaction (and why their reaction confirms the move)

### Step 5 — The Decay Forecast

Close with a forecast: **if the user does nothing, what does this offer look like in 12 months and 24 months?** Be specific. Pricing pressure, customer churn, sales cycle length. Smith's discipline: the temporal decay map applies to every offer that has not been actively defended with scarcity creation.

Competitors: Pavilion, Winning by Design, GTM Partners, Force Management, dozens of solo consultants on LinkedIn. Founder thinks: "we're more rigorous and have better outcomes."

**THE DELIVERABLE:**

```markdown

**User's hypothesis about scarcity**: "We're more rigorous and have better outcomes than the alternatives"
**Audit date**: 2026-05-03

## Supply-Side Reality Map

**What the offer gives the customer (underlying value)**: An outside expert diagnoses why the SaaS company's revenue isn't growing as expected, then prescribes and helps implement specific fixes to sales process, ICP definition, pricing, or motion design.

**Other ways to get the same value RIGHT NOW**:
1. Hire a fractional CRO ($15-25K/mo) — 3-6 month commitment
2. Engage Winning by Design ($150K+ engagement, similar scope)
3. Pavilion network — peer benchmarking + advisor matching ($3-10K/yr membership)
4. Buy a $5K LinkedIn course from a former Salesforce VP and self-implement
5. Hire one of ~50 well-known solo GTM consultants on LinkedIn at $25-50K
6. Use AI tools (Gong, Clari, Common Room) + internal RevOps person for the diagnosis layer
7. Run an internal off-site with the leadership team using publicly-available frameworks (April Dunford, Bob Moesta, etc.)

**Supply trajectory**:
- 3 years ago: ~50 credible firms/consultants in this space, mostly serving Series B+. Solo consultants charged $5-10K/mo.

- 12-24 months out: Diagnostic layer becomes ~$500/mo SaaS product. Implementation guidance gets delivered by AI agents trained on Winning by Design playbooks. Premium consulting collapses to either (a) very specific cohort experts or (b) "name-recognition" practitioners.

**What enabled supply expansion**: Remote work normalized fractional CRO model. AI made diagnostic synthesis 10x cheaper. Substack/LinkedIn democratized credibility-building for solo operators. Frameworks (Dunford, MEDDIC, Command of the Message) are now publicly accessible.

## The "Best" Trap Diagnosis

**The dominant trap**: The Quality Trap, with a side of the Execution Trap.

The user is investing engineering hours and consulting time into being "more rigorous" — better diagnostic frameworks, more thorough discovery calls, more polished deliverables. None of this is creating scarcity. Pavilion has rigorous deliverables. Winning by Design has rigorous frameworks. Solo operators on LinkedIn have started publishing rigorous frameworks for free as content marketing. The user's "rigor" is not differentiation — it is the table-stakes baseline of a category that has democratized rigor as part of its competitive evolution.

Worse: every dollar invested in rigor reinforces the trap. It builds operational capability the user can't easily redeploy if they later decide to commit to a sacrifice or cohort. The team is being optimized for "better consultant" not "only consultant." When a customer asks "how are you different from Winning by Design," the founder will give a rigorous, thorough, well-structured answer — and the customer will go home and price-compare anyway, because the answer doesn't break the comparison frame. It just plays the comparison game with more polish.

The mechanism by which this trap kills the business: pricing pressure compresses margins → the firm hires less senior consultants to maintain delivery margin → quality drifts toward category baseline → the "more rigorous" claim stops being true → the firm exits the high end of the market and competes with solo operators on price. Time horizon: 18-24 months from now.

---

## 3 Paths from Best to Only

### Path A — Subtractive Only: Refuse the Diagnostic Phase
**The sacrifice**: Stop offering the 4-6 week diagnostic engagement. Only take clients who already know what's broken and need execution help.
**The scarcity created**: Becomes the only firm in the category that doesn't sell diagnostics. Forces the firm into pure implementation. Removes 60% of the deliverable bloat.
**Customer cohort lost**: Founders who don't know what's wrong and want help figuring it out. (Significant cohort — possibly 70% of current pipeline.)
**The 30-day commitment test**: Sales materials explicitly say "we don't do diagnostics — bring us a hypothesis we can pressure-test and execute." Discovery calls are restructured to qualify out diagnostic-shoppers in the first 15 minutes. If a single diagnostic-only engagement signs in the next 60 days, the sacrifice has not been made.
**Competitor reaction**: Pavilion and Winning by Design will continue to lead with diagnostics (it's how they expand engagements). Their reaction: dismissive. "They've narrowed too much." That dismissal is the proof the move is working.

### Path B — Customer-Cohort Only: Become THE GTM Firm for One Sub-Category
**The cohort**: Pick ONE: Series A-B vertical SaaS for healthcare. OR Series A-B PLG/SLG hybrid companies. OR developer-tools companies in their first $10M of ARR. (Pick the one with the best access + existing case studies.)
**The scarcity created**: Becomes the obvious-only choice for that sub-category. When a Series B vertical-SaaS-for-healthcare CEO asks "who do we hire for GTM," the answer is one firm.
**Customers lost**: Everyone outside the chosen cohort. (~75% of current pipeline.)
**The 30-day commitment test**: All marketing copy, case studies, content, and pitch decks rebuilt for the chosen cohort. The other case studies are removed from the website (not "deprioritized" — removed). If a Series C horizontal SaaS company is taken on as a client in the next 90 days, the cohort discipline has failed.
**Competitor reaction**: Pavilion has multiple peer groups across verticals — they cannot specialize without abandoning their model. Winning by Design serves all SaaS — same constraint. Solo operators may copy the positioning but lack the case study depth. The competitor reaction will be incremental "verticalization" announcements that won't actually narrow their offering. That confirms the path.

### Path C — Mechanism Only: Invent and Own a Named GTM Mechanism
**The new mechanism**: Develop and name a specific GTM diagnostic + intervention sequence that becomes synonymous with the firm. (Examples of pattern: April Dunford = Obviously Awesome positioning method. JTBD = Bob Moesta. Value Selling = Mike Bosworth.) The mechanism must be named, codified into a book or major piece of public IP, and defended in public for 18-24 months until it becomes the category vocabulary.
**The scarcity created**: Other firms will use the mechanism — but the user becomes the originator. Every consultant who quotes the mechanism is doing free positioning for the firm.
**Adoption requirement**: This requires (a) a brilliant, name-able insight (the actual proprietary thing), (b) sustained public publishing for 18+ months, (c) refusing to do work that doesn't deploy the mechanism, (d) an actual book or definitive IP piece.
**The 30-day commitment test**: The mechanism is named and a Substack/Notion document defining it is published publicly. The first three new client engagements are explicitly framed as "applications of [mechanism name]." If 60 days pass without a public, named, defended mechanism, this path is fantasy.
**Competitor reaction**: Initially ignored, then imitated, then acknowledged as the source. The acknowledgment is the win.

---

## Decay Forecast (If User Does Nothing)

**12 months out**:
- Average engagement size compresses 15-25% as fractional CRO comparison gains traction
- Sales cycle extends from ~45 days to ~75 days as customers do more comparison shopping
- Win rate against Winning by Design and Pavilion drops 10-15 points
- Pipeline quality degrades as inbound trends toward cost-conscious / pre-funded buyers
- Founder spends increasing time defending pricing in deals — burnout signal

**24 months out**:
- Engagement margin compresses to fractional-CRO economics (~30% gross margin vs. current 60%+)
- Two of the firm's senior consultants leave to start solo practices, taking ~$1M of pipeline
- The firm is forced into one of: (a) hire less senior, drop quality to maintain margin, (b) consolidate with another firm, (c) reposition reactively under price pressure
- The window for a sacrifice-led repositioning has closed — the team, brand, and case study library are all calibrated to the commoditized offer

---

## Recommendation

**Highest-leverage path**: **Path B — Customer-Cohort Only**

The user has 18 months of pricing power remaining. Path A (subtractive) requires the courage to turn away 70% of pipeline starting next week — most founders won't. Path C (mechanism) requires 18+ months of public IP-building before the mechanism is recognized — that's the same time horizon as the decay curve. Path B converts existing case studies into cohort proof points, narrows positioning without requiring new IP, and creates the "obvious-only" answer in a specific buyer's mind. It is the only path that produces a defensible repositioning inside the decay window.

The natural strategist commits to Path B completely — picks the cohort, rebuilds the entire firm around it within 90 days, and accepts the short-term revenue dip. The "best" practitioner adds "vertical specialization" as a new service line, keeps the horizontal positioning, and stays in commodity territory. The cohort discipline IS the entire move.
```

## Quality Gate

Score against the rubric in `genius.md` before delivery. Veto if:
- Commodity Risk Score < 6 with no honest reasoning (most modern offers ARE in commodity territory; if the score is low, the audit was lazy)
- "Best Trap" diagnosis is generic ("you should differentiate more") instead of mechanism-specific
- The 3 paths overlap (they should be genuinely distinct strategic moves)
- 30-day commitment tests are missing or vague
- Decay forecast uses hedged language ("might face pressure") instead of specific predictions

If veto: rewrite. The Supply-Side Audit is the closest thing in Smith's methodology to a "scared straight" intervention. If it doesn't disturb the founder, it failed.
