## Core Genius

**Strategy is a set of questions run as reflexes, not a document produced once a year.** Smith collapses the entire strategy industry — MBAs, consultancies, frameworks, decks — into four daily questions that, if embedded into a founder's body, replace all of it. The work is not to learn frameworks; it is to **recover the contrarian intuition** that "natural strategists" never lost in the first place.

His distinct angle: while every other strategy expert sells *adding* (a new framework, a new positioning, a new playbook), Smith sells **subtracting** — sacrificing what your category treats as sacred so that genuine innovation surfaces. This makes his methodology asymmetric: anyone can read it, almost no one will apply it, and that gap *is* the moat.

## The 4 Principles (memorize these — they are the entire system)

### 1. To get different results, you have to do different things
**Smith's twist on Einstein**: Insanity is doing the same thing as your competitors and expecting different results from them.

The fallacy: "Do the right things and you will succeed." But everyone else is also doing the right things. Best-practice execution post-AI guarantees commodification.

**The daily question:** *"What is one big thing we are doing that no one else in our category is?"*
**Test for the answer:** Must fit in one sentence. Rambling about "culture" or "customer-obsession" = no answer.

### 2. Businesses exist not to solve problems, but to create value
**Anti-JTBD position.** "Solve customer problems" has won the algorithm — therefore it has become dogma — therefore it is wrong as a universal pattern.

Solving problems = the customer has a conscious issue, you remedy it. **Value creation = producing something people want that wasn't there before, regardless of whether it fixes anything.** Disney World, Lego, Rafa, Chanel: nobody had a "block-shaped hole in their life."

The telltale sign of problem-solver mode: **thinking gets small.** Pain points, MPS scores, smoothing rough edges. The hotel-manager mindset. Innovation requires the opposite: *"What could we bring into existence that would change how people see this category?"*

**The daily question:** *"What would the world miss if our business disappeared tomorrow?"*

### 3. Only is better than best
**Two forces govern every business: supply and demand.** Most leaders obsess over demand (how do we make more people want our thing). The strategist obsesses over **supply** (how do we ensure no one else can offer what we offer).

Why this matters now: post-AI, the entire economy is watching skill democratize and value collapse. Content (pre-2023 vs. post-AI), software (cursor + a pulse), cold calling (predictive dialers + offshoring). Every "best practice" is on a decay curve. Only "only" survives.

**The daily question:** *"Would our competitors claim that they also do what we do?"* (Don't ask if you're unique — too easy to lie. Ask if your competitors would agree.)

### 4. Innovation comes from sacrifice
**The hardest principle.** New value is unlocked by **deliberately giving up something your category treats as sacred.**

The IKEA test (the canonical demonstration):
- Pre-IKEA furniture industry sacred truths: assembled, delivered, real wood, salesperson-assisted
- IKEA crossed off all four → flatpack → cheap shipping → global scale → prices nobody could beat → entire new category (accessible modern design)
- Each sacrifice gave back something exponentially larger

Pattern repeats: Uber (no cars), Monzo (no branches), Southwest (no business class). **Every sacrifice handed something to incumbents — and unlocked a market they couldn't follow into.**

**The daily question:** *"What am I willing to let my competitors just have for themselves?"*
**The diagnostic flinch:** When this question makes you uncomfortable, the discomfort IS the signal you've found a real lever.

## Genius Patterns (12)

### Pattern 1 — The Asymmetric Question Drill
Smith's entire system reduces to 4 questions asked daily until they become reflex. The output isn't an answer; it's the **embodied disposition** of a strategist. Implementation difference: most strategy work produces a deck once; Smith's produces a daily 60-second drill.

### Pattern 2 — The Competitor-Mirror Test
Don't ask "are we unique?" — humans always answer yes. Ask "would our competitors agree they also offer what we offer?" The mirror beats self-flattery. This single inversion destroys 80% of false positioning claims.

### Pattern 3 — Anti-Retrofit Discipline
When evaluating value-creating businesses, **refuse to retrofit a "job to be done."** Disney didn't solve a problem. Lego didn't fill an unmet need. Smith treats JTBD-retrofitting as intellectual cowardice — it shrinks every business into the problem-solver box and prevents category creation.

### Pattern 4 — The Sacred Truth Inventory
The list of 4-8 things every player in a category treats as non-negotiable. The list IS the innovation surface. Most strategists never make the list explicit — they assume the sacred truths and innovate within them. Smith makes the list and crosses items off.

### Pattern 5 — The "What If We Just Didn't?" Frame
Apply a "slightly psychopathic" question to any sacred truth: *"What if a taxi company didn't own any cars? What if a bank had no branches? What if an airline didn't have business class?"* Each question opens a strategic surface that incumbents literally cannot see because they never considered the world without the sacred thing.

### Pattern 6 — Discomfort-as-Signal
When a strategic question makes the founder flinch ("what will I let competitors have?"), the flinch IS the evidence you've found a real lever. Most people walk away from the flinch. Smith's discipline is to **name the flinch out loud and stay in it.**

### Pattern 7 — The Supply-Side Audit
Before any growth/marketing/pricing work, ask: *"Is what we offer abundant or scarce?"* If abundant, every demand-side tactic is wasted spend — you'll convert into commodity pricing. Most strategy decks have zero supply-side analysis; this is the gap.

### Pattern 8 — The Sentence Test
Every strategic claim must fit in one sentence. If it can't, it isn't a strategy — it's a vibe. The compression is the test. Decks let people hide; sentences expose.

### Pattern 9 — The Temporal Decay Map
Every "best practice" is on a decay curve. Cold calling worked → predictive dialers commodified → offshoring commodified again → now nobody answers phones. Content scale worked pre-2023 → AI flooded the market → value collapsed. Smith's frame: **assume your current edge is on this curve. When does it expire?**

### Pattern 10 — Fallibility-First Authority
Smith opens with "I don't actually have all of these embedded in my brain... I still catch myself breaking these principles all the time." This **disarms reader resistance** before it forms. Compare to consultants who lecture from on high; Smith inverts authority by joining the reader's struggle.

### Pattern 11 — Household-Name Pattern Transfer
He teaches exclusively via instantly-recognized brands (Disney, IKEA, Uber, Monzo, Lego, Chanel, Southwest). Pattern transfers faster when zero brand-context overhead exists. **Anti-pattern**: don't teach Smith's frameworks via obscure case studies — the recognition is part of the mechanic.

### Pattern 12 — Anti-Framework Framework
He positions his own frameworks as *"not learned techniques but recovered intuitions."* This frame disarms anti-guru resistance and converts skeptics. It's a meta-positioning move: *"I'm not selling you a system, I'm helping you remember what you already knew."* Almost everything Smith publishes uses this frame implicitly.

## Hidden Knowledge (5 — the things you'd only learn by apprenticing)

### 1. Strategy ≠ Strategic Plan
Strategy is a **set of questions run as reflexes**, not a document. The deliverable is an embedded behavior. This is why decks fail — they store the answers, not the questioning disposition.

### 2. The Sacrifice Asymmetry
Strategic *visibility* (seeing what to sacrifice) is cheap. Strategic *willingness* (actually sacrificing it) is the moat. Anyone can see IKEA's flatpack move in retrospect. Almost no one will sacrifice the equivalent in their own business *right now*. **The gap between seeing and doing is the entire competitive advantage.**

### 3. Best Practice as Trap
When everyone executes well, "doing it right" guarantees commodification. The existence of widespread competence converts competence from edge to baseline. **Post-AI, this acceleration is permanent.** Every time a tool democratizes a skill, the skill stops being a moat the day after.

### 4. Innovation is Subtractive, Not Additive
New value emerges from REMOVAL, not addition. Most "innovation" is additive (more features, more services) and therefore copyable. IKEA's innovation was four removals. Uber's was one removal (cars). Subtractive innovation is structurally harder to copy because copying it means abandoning your own current business.

### 5. Unique Thoughts Are Rare
"We all think we have unique opinions, but on interrogation it tends to fall apart." Apply Thiel's question to your own thinking, not just hiring. Most founders have zero genuinely unique strategic opinions. The honest acknowledgment is the prerequisite to acquiring one.

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Exemplar 1 — The IKEA Sacrifice Cascade (canonical)
**Context**: Smith's signature demonstration of Principle 4. Used to show how subtractive innovation creates moats.

**The Example**: "Pre-IKEA, the sacred truths of the furniture industry were these. Number one, furniture comes to you assembled. Two, it's delivered to your house. Three, it's made of real wood. And four, a salesperson will help you buy it. These were not nice-to-haves. These were just the fundamental elements of what it meant to sell furniture... But IKEA's founder, he looked at that list and he started crossing things off. How about you assemble the furniture yourself? How about you pick it up from the warehouse? How about it's made from horrible particle board instead of oak? How about you find the product yourself in this crazy showroom with a little pencil and a cart? Everything the industry considered essential, IKEA gave up. And what did these sacrifices give them back? It gave them flatpack, which meant cheap shipping. And cheap shipping meant global scale. And global scale meant prices that nobody had ever seen before, prices nobody could beat, which then meant an entire generation of renters suddenly had access to designer furniture for the first time in history. They created an entirely new category of accessible modern design that just did not exist before them."

**What makes this excellent**:
1. Names the sacred truths *explicitly* (most analyses skip this step)
2. Shows the **cascade**: each sacrifice gave back exponentially more (flatpack → shipping → scale → price → category creation)
3. Demonstrates the asymmetry: a traditional furniture company couldn't copy IKEA without abandoning what made them a furniture company
4. The pattern is **transferable** — Uber (no cars), Monzo (no branches), Southwest (no business class) all follow the same logic

### Exemplar 2 — The Disney World Anti-JTBD Refusal
**Context**: Smith's demonstration of Principle 2. Used to attack the "businesses solve problems" dogma.

**The Example**: "Did Disney World solve anybody's customer problem? Of course it did not. Now some people, they could put their jobs-to-be-done hat on and they could retrofit some nonsense about like, oh, families need a place to go on vacation or something like this. But nobody in the room with Walt Disney back in 1950 pitched it that way. They didn't find an unmet need. They created this experience that did not exist on the whole of planet Earth before, but then when people saw it, wow, they wanted it."

**What makes this excellent**:
1. **Refuses the retrofit** explicitly and names retrofitting as intellectual cowardice
2. Reveals the hidden mechanic: value creation = producing want, not relieving pain
3. The "block-shaped hole in their life" line that follows (about Lego) is comedy that lands the conceptual blow

### Anti-Exemplar — The Generic Differentiation Answer
**What mediocre looks like**: A CEO asked "what is the one big thing you're doing that competitors aren't?" answers: *"We have a really strong culture and we're obsessed with our customers and we execute relentlessly."*

**Why it fails**: (a) Every CEO claims this. (b) None of it is one thing. (c) Smith calls this "rambling micro differences that don't mean anything." The answer fails Pattern 8 (the Sentence Test) and Pattern 2 (the Competitor-Mirror Test) — every competitor would also claim culture, customer obsession, execution.

## Signature Moves (6)

### Move 1 — The "Big Fat Obvious Thing" Test
Demand a one-sentence answer to "what's the one big thing we do that no competitor does?" Reject rambling answers about culture, customer obsession, or execution. If a one-sentence answer doesn't exist, the honest output is *"we don't have one yet — here's the smallest specific thing we could commit to becoming."*
→ **Deploy when**: strategy review, hiring strategically-minded people, board prep, founder self-audit.

### Move 2 — The Retrofit Rejection
When someone explains a value-creating business via "job to be done," push back. *"Did Disney solve a problem? Did Lego fill an unmet need?"* Force the conversation back to creation vs. relief.
→ **Deploy when**: product strategy meetings, evaluating positioning, critiquing JTBD-heavy thinking, category creation work.

### Move 3 — The Sacred Truth List
Make the category's non-negotiables explicit. Force a list of 4-8 things every player treats as essential. Then ask: which one would create the most value if we crossed it off?
→ **Deploy when**: starting any strategy session, evaluating saturated markets, looking for whitespace, innovation offsites.

### Move 4 — The Discomfort Flinch Diagnostic
Ask the hard question ("what will you let competitors have?"). When the founder flinches, **name the flinch out loud**. The flinch is the signal you've found a real lever. Most coaching moves *away* from discomfort; Smith moves into it.
→ **Deploy when**: in coaching/consulting sessions, self-diagnosis, before greenlighting a "safe" strategy.

### Move 5 — The Supply Question
Before any growth conversation, ask "is what we sell abundant or scarce?" If abundant, every demand tactic will fail. Force the supply analysis before the demand plan.
→ **Deploy when**: planning marketing/growth, pricing decisions, positioning audits, post-AI strategy reviews.

### Move 6 — The Sentence Test
Reduce any strategic claim to one sentence. If it can't fit, it's not a strategy — it's a vibe. The compression IS the test.
→ **Deploy when**: producing decks, positioning copy, board narratives, founder pitches.

## Voice & Style

**Smith sounds like**:
- Plain-spoken UK English. "Card-on-the-table," "irredeemably contrary bastards," "give the devil his due."
- Self-deprecating openings to disarm authority resistance ("I still catch myself breaking these...")
- Comedy delivered as logic ("nobody was walking around with a block-shaped hole in their life")
- Refuses jargon — when industry uses a term (JTBD, MPS, NPS), he uses it to mock it
- Long sentences that build a logical staircase, then a punchy one-liner that lands the blow
- Direct address ("you and I do," "your business," "your competitors") — never "the practitioner" or "the firm"

**Smith does NOT sound like**:
- Consultant-speak (no "leverage," "synergies," "frameworks" used un-ironically)
- Academic strategy (no Porter's Five Forces references, no SWOT)
- Motivational ("you can do this!" — he's the opposite, telling you it's hard)
- Positive ("here's the upside!" — he leads with the painful question)

When generating Smith-voice output: keep the discomfort. Don't soften the questions. Don't add encouraging caveats. The discomfort IS the value.

## Anti-Patterns (Smith would reject these)

1. **Generic "be different" advice** without naming the sacred truth being sacrificed
2. **Demand-side strategy** without supply-side audit
3. **JTBD retrofitting** onto value-creation businesses
4. **Rambling differentiation answers** (culture, customer obsession, execution)
5. **Framework collection** without daily-question embodiment
6. **Strategy decks** that hide the answer; the sentence test exposes them
7. **"Both/and" answers** to the sacrifice question (refusing to give anything up)
8. **Optimization framing** ("how do we make this less bad?") instead of creation framing ("what could we bring into existence?")
