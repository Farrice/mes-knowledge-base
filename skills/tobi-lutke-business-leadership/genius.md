# Tobi Lütke — Genius Context

Founder/CEO of Shopify. Programmer-turned-builder who runs a $60B+ company as executable systems: rerunnable decision functions, legitimacy as a bank account, subtraction as the founder's core service, and human potential as the actual product. Source material: Knowledge Project (Shane Parrish) and Lenny's Podcast interviews.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Keep the Main Thing the Main Thing
The thing you protect during chaos is never the current tactic — it's the North Star, the reason the company exists. Obstacles ("no one can reach retail stores anymore") don't change direction; they change routing. Tobi: "If you know in which direction you want to go, you just know — clearly we have to get around this obstacle."
**Execute**: When volatility hits, write the mission-level sentence first (Shopify's: "make entrepreneurship simpler on the internet"). Then classify every active workstream as helpful / not-helpful *right now* against that sentence. Reroute around obstacles; never re-litigate the direction.
**Success Metric**: Under crisis, zero energy spent debating direction; 100% spent on routing. (Warning light: "if people start working on the stock, things are going pretty poorly in the company.")

### Pattern: The Rerunnable Decision Function
Roadmaps and plans are overrated. The best possible roadmap is: (a) a very clear view of what matters to your customer, (b) a super strong model of your own capabilities as a company, then (c) rerun the function "given what's going on right now, what's the best thing we can do?" — over and over, with teams ready to pick the next task instead of dutifully executing a plan that reality will interrupt.
**Execute**: Store decisions as functions, not conclusions: record the inputs (customer model + capability model + current reality). When any input changes, recompute instead of defending the old output. Run the recompute on a cadence and on every reality shock.
**Success Metric**: When conditions shift, you can name which input changed and re-derive the priority list in one sitting — no attachment to the stale plan. (In H1 2022 this let Shopify stop ~60% of in-flight work without confusion.)

### Pattern: Legitimacy Banking
Legitimacy is one of the most underappreciated resources that exists (Tobi cites Vitalik Buterin's essay on it). Every retelling of the founding story deposits a little legitimacy into a bank account. A present founder can withdraw from that account to make hard, unpopular, necessary changes — "I have seen every single version of this company... I know what to do." Professionally managed companies substitute plans + stakeholder management to manufacture the same legitimacy at ~10-100x the cost. If the founder leaves, the account keeps growing but no one can draw on it.
**Execute**: Before a hard change, audit your legitimacy balance: What story gives you the right to make this call? Spend legitimacy on subtraction and direction changes (highest ROI); never spend it on things a plan or a delegate could legitimize instead. Refill by being visibly in the work.
**Success Metric**: You can cut or redirect major workstreams and the organization's reaction is "he's seen this before," not revolt.

### Pattern: Subtraction Is the Founder's Superpower
Saying no to a thing says no to one thing. Saying yes says no to every other thing you could have done with that time. Additions pile up as "sediment layers" — every layer individually a good idea, the sum something no one can steel-man. Eventually more people maintain the status quo than build. The best service a founder renders is removal: Tobi subtracted 60% of everything in 2022. Very few non-founder CEOs can do it (Nadella is the rare exception, and it's 10-100x harder for them).
**Execute**: Run a periodic sediment audit: list everything being maintained, ask "would we start this today?" Kill what fails, even if every step that created it was rational. Treat each new yes as an explicit no to alternatives — price it that way before committing.
**Success Metric**: Maintenance headcount share falling or flat while output rises; at least one meaningful subtraction per cycle that people initially resist and later cite as obviously right.

### Pattern: Best Practices Are Average, Risk-Adjusted
"Best practices actually just simply means don't take risk and do what everyone else is saying you should be doing... they're average." Everything depends on innovation, and innovation requires doing things differently — which means sometimes underperforming the status quo. That's fine, because if you can subtract, you can remove what underperformed and go again. At Shopify a miss is reframed: "it's not failure — it's the successful discovery of something that didn't work."
**Execute**: For every area of the business, ask: could this team give a conference talk on how our version beats the general implementation of this discipline? If not, apply first-principles thinking there (Shopify did this to compensation — an area that "never sees innovation"). Use orthodoxy as an input and a starting point, never a ceiling.
**Success Metric**: Count of areas where you run a genuinely differentiated approach; underperforming experiments get subtracted and retried, not buried or punished.

### Pattern: Goodhart's Law Is Just Overfitting
Any metric that becomes a goal ceases to be a good metric — the business version of a model learning to cheat the loss function. School grades, OKRs, growth KPIs: same failure. Shopify's core product runs without specific goals — metrics take a *support function* while taste, intuition, and "less quantifiable things" (joy, fun, delight — "the most powerful unquantifiable things in the world of business") lead. Corollary: universal truths recur across fields under different names ("hidden harmonies"); learning a lot about a lot lets you spot them.
**Execute**: For each metric you steer by, name the real thing it proxies. If people can move the number without moving the real thing, demote the metric to a supporting instrument and put a tasteful human judgment ("this is actually just really great — we're shipping it") in charge. Optimize for the unquantifiable directly; measure around it, not at it.
**Success Metric**: You can name the proxy gap for every KPI; no team's promotion path depends solely on driving a number up.

### Pattern: The Toby Tornado — Compress the Change
When something isn't right: have the conversation *now*, steel-man the other side, and either update your prior quickly or kill the project immediately. The logic is a trilemma: "I'm either incorrect — at which point it's super important I understand why — or I'm correct — at which point it's super unfair to let people keep working on something that isn't going to make it. Ignoring it is an abdication of responsibility." Then reframe: the team members aren't losing a project, they're the founders of the next version. Careers are short; compress time so people ship the maximum number of things they'll be proud of.
**Execute**: On spotting a probably-wrong project: (1) schedule the real-talk conversation within days, not quarters; (2) demand the steel-man of continuing; (3) decide — update your prior or stop the work; (4) immediately recharter the same people as founders of v2. Don't optimize for the softest delivery; optimize for the shortest time-to-truth.
**Success Metric**: Days (not months) between "this feels wrong" and a decision; people on killed projects report relief and re-energization, not whiplash.

### Pattern: Harvest Disagreement for Divergent Assumptions
Courage is rarer than IQ — "I found a lot more high IQ, maybe even genius, than courage." So when someone disagrees with him, Tobi immediately stops and treats it as a gift: the disagreement is almost never surface preference; it points to *unstated foundational assumptions*. The question is always: what is our divergence point? People are often right when it gets there — and disagreeing publicly earns trust, because it proves they'll do what's right over what's convenient.
**Execute**: On disagreement, stop advancing your case. Ask both sides to state the assumptions underneath their position until the single divergent assumption surfaces. Test that assumption, not the conclusions. Visibly reward the disagreer regardless of outcome so the courage price drops for the next person.
**Success Metric**: Disagreements resolve at the assumption level with a named divergence point; frequency of public pushback to you increases over time.

### Pattern: No Speed Limit on Human Potential
"There is not a single person on this planet who is even close to their maximum potential." The blockers are undiscovered approaches and environments that narrow focus onto unambitious things — where you end up competing with literally everyone, because everyone's unambitious. Tobi's method: constantly remind people of their potential, hold them accountable to their *potential* rather than current-level-plus-epsilon, and deliver the hard version of feedback ("I actually expected better of you — you saw path A and path B, and chose B out of convenience"). It's hard to hear because it's right — and it's how people accomplish things they didn't imagine they could. This is also the product: Shopify exists to make merchants more ambitious than they set out to be.
**Execute**: Calibrate feedback to the person's ceiling, not their baseline. Name the specific moment they chose the convenient path over the right one. Follow curiosity and optimize each career step for maximum learning, not maximum comfort — for yourself first (be harder on yourself than anyone else, then apply it at a discount to others).
**Success Metric**: People regularly ship things they explicitly said they couldn't; your predictions of someone's potential outperform their own self-assessment.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: "Bureaucracy" Only Ever Describes Broken Process
**Insight**: People never call things that work well "bureaucracy" or "process" — those words are exclusive to things that failed. So every complaint about bureaucracy is a free map to a first-principles streamlining opportunity. But beware the counterfeit: some "bureaucracy" is actually good checks-and-balances, where the person with the wider perspective (remodel the floor / go fully remote) correctly overrides the local fix (patch the hole in the wall). Ideas must come from everywhere; decisions must come from the place of right perspective.
**Deploy**: Treat every bureaucracy complaint as a diagnostic ping. First ask: is this sediment (streamline it) or is this a wider-perspective decision that was never *explained* (explain it)? Hand executives Parkinson's Law (the 80-page book Tobi gives his own) so the org can laugh at its own drift and replace it with something better.

### Insight: River Stone vs. Volcanic Rock
**Insight**: Professionally managed companies are river stones — smooth everywhere, uniformly competent, excellent at everything measurable, with stakeholder management as the leadership team's core competency and planning as their legitimacy engine. Founder-led companies are volcanic rock — spiky, with extreme aptitudes and surprising weaknesses, ideally shaped to the specific problem set the company serves. Neither is wrong; they run on different physics, and the gravitational pull on both is reversion to "ambient industry competency," which is quite low.
**Deploy**: Diagnose which physics an organization runs on before advising it. Founder-led: spend legitimacy directly, stay spiky, don't sand down the edges to look professional. Professionally managed: don't cargo-cult founder moves — build the plan-and-stakeholder legitimacy machine properly, and protect the few spikes you have.

### Insight: Think 100 Years Forward, Then Backwards
**Insight**: You can't talk about a software project on a 100-year timeframe, but you can talk about the *mission* that way ("entrepreneurship is just precious"). Tobi's operating move is future-backwards: "what would we want to have done 20 years ago on this?" Paired with his energy source — dissatisfaction with the status quo ("today is the dystopia of the future") — it produces grounded urgency: pessimism sounds sophisticated and optimism sounds naive, but the suit-and-pie-chart aesthetic has near-zero overlap with outperformance.
**Deploy**: Anchor strategy in a mission durable enough to survive 80-100 years, then ask the 20-years-ago question to find what to start now. When a proposal sounds naive but compounds toward the mission, weight it up; when it sounds sophisticated but only manages the present, weight it down.

### Insight: Founders Are Exothermic — Energy Is the Real Input
**Insight**: Companies can't run hotter than the energy being injected into them. Founders and the best leaders are "exothermic" — wellsprings of energy — and that injection, not process, is what leads to outsized results. Culture design is downstream of keeping a cast of energy-positive characters and converting dissatisfaction into building (Jordan-style: even manufactured slights become fuel).
**Deploy**: Staff leadership roles by net energy contribution, not just competence — one energy sink at the top cools the whole system. Audit your own energy source; if dissatisfaction with the status quo isn't converting into building, that's the first system to fix. Aim for the 99.99% SLA on good individual experiences (like server uptime), and expect the rare bad one to be told as the whole story — design for the society level anyway.
