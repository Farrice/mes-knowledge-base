# Cross-Domain Patterns — The Domain-Translation Layer

> Load alongside `genius.md`. This file does one job: it carries Stanton's feature-film mechanics across every surface you actually ship. The governing analogy holds the whole thing together — **every deliverable is a story you must uncover, not force, and keep the audience clamped through to the end.** You are not "writing a newsletter" or "building a deck." You are running a cable-car chain under the reader's attention and refusing to let them unclamp before the change lands.

The trap on every surface is the same one Stanton names in the interview: forcing a premise because you ran out of patience, then building on a lie. A LinkedIn post forced to a hook is the same failure as a forced theme — it just fails faster. So the dig comes first on every surface. Find the true one-sentence premise, give the protagonist a spine, locate the change, then engineer the telling so the audience is pulled forward and never once feels the engineering.

Keep the architecture invisible. None of these moves get named on the page. A reader who can feel the clamp has already unclamped.

## The Move in Action, One Surface at a Time

### Substack / newsletter
Run the teeter-totter before you run the template. Jam a messy draft with no plan, then step back and analyze the patterns you accidentally made — one of them is the premise-sentence the issue is actually about. Most newsletters fail because the writer outlines from the topic instead of digging for the line; you end up with a competent file of information and a dropped beach ball by paragraph three. Find the true premise, pace the prose with line breaks the way Stanton broke the WALL·E description so the reader's heart rate matches the issue, and end somewhere that was inevitable but unseen until the last paragraph. The reader should close it slightly changed, never having read a thesis stated twice.

### LinkedIn / short-form social
Here the premise-sentence *is* the entire deliverable, so the dig matters more, not less — there's no second act to rescue a forced line. Clamp at the line level: every line exists to make the reader need the next one, not to deliver a payoff at the bottom. The cable car runs the whole way down the scroll or it never clamped at all. Compress one five-second change into the post, set the obvious belief against its opposite to hold the eye, and land on something surprising-then-inevitable. No generic question close — that's the beach ball dropping on the last beat, and the reader goes back to checking their watch.

### Sales page / VSL
Stop selling the symptom. The smart notes on a stalled page always say "tighten the copy, harder CTA" — the same near-unanimous, wrong fix Stanton got on Marlin ("too neurotic, recast him"). The root is almost always an unbuilt premise: the page never named the one belief that denies a capable buyer from acting. Build that prologue of empathy first and the downstream objections evaporate untouched. Then treat the whole VSL as one cable-car chain — re-clamp every fifteen to twenty seconds before attention can drop, dramatize the change instead of asserting it, and let the close feel like the buyer's own conclusion: surprising in the moment, of course in retrospect.

### Brand / origin story
A founding story without a spine is a list of dates. Give the brand the Weston wiring — the one thing it would still chase if the category disappeared — and the conflict, tension, and arc fall out of it the way they fell out of Michael Corleone wanting to please his father. The drama lives in opposition: founder against the industry orthodoxy, the collision that made the brand necessary. Invoke wonder once — the moment that makes someone feel something larger than a transaction — and let the brand's destiny read as both unexpected and the only path it could have taken. Tell it well; a true founding told flat loses to a thinner one told with restraint.

### Ad / Reel (pre-skip)
The clamp has to fire before the skip button is even a thought. The first frame is the chain; miss it and there is no second chance to clamp. Within the clip, one change must be *visible* — a state that flips on screen, not narrated — because the telling carries the belief here more than anywhere: editing rhythm and sound cut to the exact heart rate you want, voiceover withheld the way Stanton withheld dialogue. Cut two opposed images against each other so conflict precedes resolution, and earn one involuntary "oh" that buys the save and the share. The payoff surprises, then feels like the only ending the cut could have had.

### Real-estate listing
The buyer is not purchasing a change of address; they're purchasing a change in their daily life, and that's the premise — "the wrong-sized life keeps a right-sized family from arriving." Name the buyer's spine (the thing they're solving in everything they do — to stop renting their future) and write to *that*, not to the bedroom count. Clamp by pulling them through the house: each line opens the next room instead of stacking features down a list, which is the beach ball dropping spec by spec. Hold the life-they-have against the life-the-home-enables, land one line that lets them feel *home* rather than *house*, and let "this is the one" arrive surprising at the door and inevitable by the kitchen.

### Knowledge-work explainer / memo
A memo is a story that forces a decision, and the premise-sentence is the decision: "old assumption denies a good team from shipping." If an executive can skim straight to the recommendation, the memo never clamped — every section must earn the next so the reader arrives at the conclusion under their own power. Diagnose the root, not the symptom: the loudest objection in the room is usually the prescribed fix for a problem one level upstream, and acting on it changes the wrong thing for the wrong reason. Set the status-quo approach against the proposed one in honest tension, underwrite what the reader can infer, and make the recommendation land surprising-then-obvious — the reader's own conclusion, not yours imposed.

### Pitch deck
The premise is the *business*, not the slide — character, conflict, conclusion in one true line, and if you can't write it the company isn't ready to pitch. Give the founder a visible spine, the obsession investors come to believe is durable enough to survive the hard years. Every slide makes the next one inevitable to want; no slide is a resting place where attention can drop, because a deck is a cable-car chain and an investor who can flip to the ask was never clamped. The opportunity *is* the opposition — the world as it is against the world as it should be. Make the vision land in the body, not on a bullet list, and shape the whole thing so the ask is the only rational conclusion by the last slide: unforeseen at slide one, inevitable by the end.

## Quality Gate

Before delivering any cross-domain output, confirm:

- The premise-sentence is **uncovered, not forced** — you can write one true character + conflict + conclusion line for this surface, and you didn't manufacture it to hit a deadline.
- The protagonist (reader, buyer, brand, founder, team) has a **spine**, not a list of behaviors.
- You can name the **one fundamental change** the deliverable leads to. No change, no story — start over.
- The piece is **clamped surface-appropriately**: line-level on social, section-level in long-form, frame-level pre-skip, slide-level in a deck. Walk it and find where the beach ball drops.
- The **telling** is doing real work — rhythm, restraint, withheld exposition — not just the content.
- The ending is **surprising + inevitable** for this format, and it is *not* a generic question close.
- The machinery is **invisible**. No mechanic is named on the page. A reader who can feel the engineering has already unclamped.
