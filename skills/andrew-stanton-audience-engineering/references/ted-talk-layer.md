## What only the TED talk carries (the five additions)

### T1. Storytelling Is Joke-Telling (Punchline-First)
The MacGregor joke opens the talk to *demonstrate* the principle before naming it. *"Storytelling is joke telling. It's knowing your punchline, your ending, knowing that everything you're saying, from the first sentence to the last, is leading to a singular goal, and ideally confirming some truth that deepens our understandings of who we are as human beings."*

**Execute**: before drafting anything, write the punchline — the final landing AND the truth-about-being-human it confirms. Then audit every earlier beat: does it lead there? This is the ending-side twin of the premise-sentence (P1): the premise says what it's about; the punchline says where it lands.
**Deploy when**: any piece whose ending is undecided, or whose beats wander.

### T2. Make Me Care (The Greatest Story Commandment)
From the Mr. Rogers wallet quote ("there isn't anyone you couldn't learn to love once you've heard their story"): *"probably the greatest story commandment… 'Make me care' — please, emotionally, intellectually, aesthetically, just make me care."* And the channel-surfing test: when you finally stop on something halfway over, *"that's not by chance, that's by design."*

**Execute**: before the clamp can hold, the audience must be made to care — emotionally (stakes), intellectually (a real question), or aesthetically (craft that signals worth). Name WHICH of the three your opening earns. A piece can clamp mechanically and still fail here; care is the current the clamp grips.
**Deploy when**: openings; any piece readers respect but don't finish.

### T3. The Promise (The Slingshot)
The *John Carter* mausoleum scene, the campfire invitation, "Once upon a time": *"It's making a promise to you that this story will lead somewhere that's worth your time… A well told promise is like a pebble being pulled back in a slingshot and propels you forward through the story to the end."*

**Execute**: engineer the opening as a promise with delayed payment — the pull-back that makes the release carry. State (to yourself, never on the page) exactly what the opening promises and exactly where the piece pays it. An unpaid promise is worse than none.
**Deploy when**: designing any opening; a payoff that lands weakly usually traces to a promise that was never pulled back.

### T4. The 2+2 Theory (Well-Organized Absence of Information)
Born writing *Nemo* with Bob Peterson — "the unifying theory of two plus two": *"Make the audience put things together. Don't give them four, give them two plus two."* The mechanism: *"the audience actually wants to work for their meal. They just don't want to know that they're doing that. That's your job as a storyteller, is to hide the fact that you're making them work."* The magnet image: infants and puppies pull us because *"they can't completely express what they're thinking… We can't stop ourselves from wanting to complete the sentence."* And the craft note: *"The elements you provide and the order you place them in is crucial."*

**Execute**: find every place the draft hands the reader "4" — the stated conclusion, the named emotion, the explained mechanism — and convert it to "2+2": the two elements, in the right order, that force the reader to assemble the meaning themselves. The reader's own deduction is the engagement; spelling it out refunds their meal.
**Deploy when**: over-explained drafts; reveals; any piece that informs but doesn't grip. This is the mechanism BENEATH the clamp-audit's "over-explained" cause — cite it when prescribing that re-clamp.
**Self-reference trap**: naming the withheld "4" on the page breaks the device. Execute the withhold; never announce it.

### T5. Guidelines, Not Rules + Define-by-Negation (The Secret List)
Pre–*Toy Story*, the team kept "a little secret list of rules": **no songs, no "I want" moment, no happy village, no love story** — a deliberate negation of everything 1993 animation "had to be" (Tom Hanks walked in asking "You don't want me to sing, do you?"). Disney panicked, a famous lyricist faxed the opposite advice, and being "too young, rebellious and contrarian" to comply *"just gave us more determination."* The lesson Stanton draws: *"storytelling has guidelines, not hard, fast rules."*

Paired with it, **conditional likability**: Woody could stay selfish and still be liked — kind, generous, funny — *"as long as one condition is met for him, that he stays the top toy… we all live life conditionally. We're all willing to play by the rules and follow things along, as long as certain conditions are met. After that, all bets are off."*

**Execute (negation)**: define a piece, brand, or series by the short list of what it will NOT do — specifically the moves its category treats as mandatory. The negation list is a positioning instrument: it forces original solutions where the defaults used to sit.
**Execute (conditions)**: an unlikable-on-paper protagonist (or brand) stays sympathetic if their one governing condition is legible and honored. Name the condition; break it only as the story's deliberate detonation.
**Deploy when**: positioning work (brand-origin, define-the-lane), category-cliché audits, or any protagonist/brand that must be flawed and still followed.
