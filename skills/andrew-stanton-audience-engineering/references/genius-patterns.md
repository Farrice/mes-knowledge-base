# Genius Patterns

15 executable patterns from Andrew Stanton's story-uncovering and audience-engagement practice. Each is grounded in his own words (the *How I Write* interview with David Perell, 2026) cross-checked against his filmography. Execute the move; don't name it on the page.

---

## Pattern 1: Find the Premise-Sentence
**Execute**: Distill the piece to one sentence with three parts — a character, a conflict, a conclusion. Keep a page open and write candidate after candidate; when a word is wrong, don't sweat it, let the story be lost for a while and write the next one. *Nemo* took three years to land on "fear denies a good father from being one" (fear is the character, *denies* is the conflict). Use the finished sentence as a litmus on what you've made, never as an outline you obey.

**Why it works**: One true sentence tells you the structure of the story, so it organizes every later choice without anyone seeing it do the work. A forced sentence builds the whole thing on a lie.

**Success metric**: Can you write a true character+conflict+conclusion line? If you can't, you don't understand the piece yet — keep digging. If the line is real, every beat should trace back to it.

> "It's more a litmus test of an analysis of what I think I've done." (Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing*)

## Pattern 2: Give Every Character a Spine
**Execute**: Hand the protagonist — person, brand, founder, customer — a fixed inner wiring that never changes no matter what's happening in life, then a one-liner that names it. Michael Corleone's spine is *to please his father*, intact even after the father dies. WALL·E's spine is *to find the beauty*; his one-liner is "irrational love defeats life's programming." Find the single thing they are solving in everything they do, and conflict, tension, and arc fall out of it.

**Why it works**: A spine governs how a character "opens a book or gets up in the morning," so behavior becomes consistent and alive instead of a scattered list. This is Wright Thompson's central complication by another name — the one knot every choice ties back to.

**Success metric**: Can you state the wiring and the one-liner, and does every action trace to it? A character you can only describe by listing behaviors has no spine yet.

> A spine is "a way they're wired up no matter what's going on in life — the core of you." (Judith Weston)

## Pattern 3: Keep Them Clamped
**Execute**: Audit every beat for forward pull. The bar is anticipation mingled with uncertainty — not just plot mystery (the low-hanging fruit) but making them dying to know the *next line of dialogue*. Ride the San Francisco cable car: a chain runs under the street always moving, and you clamp onto it to get pulled up the hill. Walk the piece asking "when did we accidentally unclamp?" The lesser image is the beach ball at a concert — the second it drops, the audience checks their watch.

**Why it works**: Anticipation and uncertainty are conjurable and at the ready — a tapping at the window that grows louder is uncertainty manufactured from nothing. Total forward pull keeps the audience so engaged they never think about anything else.

**Success metric**: Beat by beat, name where the beach ball dropped. If you can't find a drop, you're clamped the whole way; if you can, restore the pull or cut the beat.

> "Drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty." (William Archer — Stanton's favorite definition)

## Pattern 4: Uncover, Do Not Invent
**Execute**: Treat the story as already existing; your job is the dig, not the manufacture. Run a teeter-totter — one side analyzes (find a structure, find a meaning, find the patterns you happen to be creating), the other side just plays ("I just jam, I don't think about anything because it restricts me"). Jump between the two sides and never sit in the middle sweating a half-built sentence. When you run out of time, the trap is forcing one. Patience instead: keep at it until you've struck what feels like the truthful profile.

**Why it works**: The statue is in the marble; you take credit only for *where* you dig and for being aggressive in digging, never for what comes up. Forcing produces a false premise; the teeter-totter keeps you either discovering or composing, never stuck.

**Success metric**: When blocked, you can name which side you're stuck on — over-analyzing (go play with no plan) or lost in play (step back, read the patterns). If you're sweating a sentence in the middle, you've stalled.

> "A really great story already exists out there. The statue's in the marble, I just uncover it."

## Pattern 5: Fit the Dinosaur You Uncovered
**Execute**: When a late discovery contradicts the committed plan, change the work to the truth you dug up — not the one you promised. You find the neck bone and the tailbone, realize it's a Stegosaurus not the T-Rex you sold the executives, and you rebuild the story to fit the dinosaur you actually found. Build your process so the pivot is survivable: Pixar structured its production flow specifically so it can change late, where most studios "blink or shrink."

**Why it works**: The truth you uncovered is the real story; the plan was a guess. The courage to change the dinosaur — the fortitude, the temerity — is the difference between good and great, and a flow built to absorb the pivot is what lets the truth win.

**Success metric**: When an 11th-hour realization conflicts with locked work, the truth gets built, not buried. If the plan survived only because changing it was inconvenient, you shrank.

> "Wait a minute, it's not a Tyrannosaurus Rex, it's a Stegosaurus" — and then the temerity to rebuild around it.

## Pattern 6: Tell Me What You Do, Not What You Say
**Execute**: Weight behavior over stated opinion. At test screenings the Q&A is nearly worthless; what's true is the audience in the dark — laughing, smiling, leaning forward. Even if the lights come up and they say "I didn't like it," believe how they were when the lights were down. Apply the same lens to any feedback: read what people *did* with the work, not what they reported.

**Why it works**: People narrate a self-image in the Q&A but can't fake leaning forward in the dark. Behavior is involuntary; opinion is performed.

**Success metric**: Your read of the work rests on observed behavior (dwell, re-shares, leaning in), and where stated opinion contradicts it, you trust the behavior.

> "Even if the lights come up and they say 'I didn't like it,' I believe how they were when the lights were down."

## Pattern 7: Diagnose the Root, Not the Symptom
**Execute**: When smart notes pile up around one surface problem, distrust the prescribed fix and find the single upstream cause. On *Nemo*, every test audience said the father was "too neurotic, recast him." The real fix was the opposite of recasting — gather the scattered backstory flashbacks into one emotional prologue up front, which earned so much empathy that every later complaint evaporated untouched. Take the felt note seriously; throw out the proposed solution.

**Why it works**: A pile of notes is real signal pointing at a real wound, but the prescribed fix usually treats the symptom. One upstream emotional move can dissolve a dozen downstream problems at once.

**Success metric**: You acted on the felt note and the upstream cause, and a stack of separate complaints resolved without being touched individually.

> "If I'd compiled the notes from very smart people saying what to fix, I would have changed the wrong thing for the wrong reasons."

## Pattern 8: The Telling Is the Content
**Execute**: Craft the delivery, not just the substance. Control rhythm, withhold exposition, make moments worth savoring. On *WALL·E* Stanton stole Walter Hill's 1979 *Alien* draft trick — break description into short, left-justified lines broken at the exact rhythm you want it read, so it looks like dialogue and no one skims it. He made you read the script at the pace you'd one day watch the film. Underwrite the obvious; trust the gap.

**Why it works**: How you tell something is often the reason you enjoy it more than what's being said — a good joke told poorly loses, a bad joke told well wins. Adding dialogue and exposition past a point subtracts; the silent-film lesson is that rhythm and restraint carry more than explanation.

**Success metric**: The *how* outclasses the *what* — a reader slows to the intended pace, the moments meant to be savored are savored, and nothing obvious is over-explained.

> "You can have a really good joke told poorly, and I'd rather have a really bad joke told well."

## Pattern 9: Write For Yourself, Trust the Audience
**Execute**: Build what *you* would actually want to consume, and only check that you haven't accidentally excluded anyone. Don't dumb anything down for an imagined "them." Pixar never wrote for kids — they wrote for themselves, and never worried about children keeping up, because kids are "huge readers of gesture and tone." The real risk is treating the audience as too dumb.

**Why it works**: Writing to your own standard sets a true ceiling; writing down to an imagined market sets a false floor and the work goes flat. Audiences fill gaps and read subtext better than you fear.

**Success metric**: You'd genuinely want to consume this yourself, and you didn't simplify a single beat on the theory the audience couldn't follow.

> "We've never written for kids. We've just written for ourselves."

## Pattern 10: The Entertainment Filter
**Execute**: Run every draft, scene, line, and decision through one instinct — is this more entertaining now than it was a minute ago? The Brain Trust passed everything through that single filter, and nothing got through without becoming more entertaining. It's not a polish step at the end; it's the gate each element has to pass to stay in.

**Why it works**: A consistent, ruthless filter applied to every piece compounds — each pass raises the floor, so the whole work climbs without any one heroic move. The filter is binary and easy to apply, which is why it actually gets used.

**Success metric**: For each kept element you can say it became more entertaining on the way through. Anything that merely "stayed the same" got cut or lifted.

> "Nothing got through us without becoming more entertaining."

## Pattern 11: Invoke Wonder
**Execute**: Reach for the involuntary "oh" — the human-condition moment that makes someone humble and appreciative of just existing. A puppy or an anthropomorphized robot produces it on contact. Chase that feeling as a goal in itself: a piece that does *that* to you, again and again, the way the WALL·E seed started from wanting "some of that juice."

**Why it works**: Wonder bypasses analysis and lands as feeling, so it dissolves the audience's guard before any argument or plot does. It's the deepest engagement there is — not curiosity about what happens next, but appreciation of being alive.

**Success metric**: Somewhere the work produces the involuntary "oh," not a thought but a felt humility. If every beat is clever and none is wondrous, you've engaged the head and missed the chest.

> "I want some of that juice. Can I have a movie that just does that to you all the time?"

## Pattern 12: Opposites Create Drama
**Execute**: Build the engine from opposing agendas. When a seed is pure feeling and won't move, introduce its opposite — for *WALL·E*, a second robot from somewhere else, opposites, "John and I were very convinced it should be a romance." Make the opposition visual and unmissable: he was a box, she was a circle. Two agendas in collision generate conflict, conflict generates drama, drama generates will-they-won't-they.

**Why it works**: A single agenda has nowhere to go; two opposing ones produce friction at every contact point, and friction is what pulls an audience forward. The visual contrast (box vs. circle) makes the opposition legible before a word is spoken.

**Success metric**: You can name two opposing agendas and point to the drama each collision throws off. If the piece has only one agenda, it's static — import its opposite.

> "He was a box, she was a circle."

## Pattern 13: Change Is the Umbrella
**Execute**: Name the fundamental moment of change the whole thing leads to — no change, no story. Even a rock changes (it erodes); find the change even when it's small, even when it's a pause ("it was active, then it wasn't"). For short form, compress to the single moment: stories are about a five-second moment of change. For long form, make every beat earn that one change and keep the line to it active the whole way.

**Why it works**: Change is the umbrella under which premise, spine, and clamp all sit — it's what makes a sequence of events a story rather than a list. Stanton cites Sean Puri by name here; the viral compression of one change into a post and the feature-length architecture that makes the change land are the same instinct at different scales.

**Success metric**: You can point to the single moment of change the whole piece exists to deliver. If nothing fundamentally changes, the cable car has disconnected — find the change or there's no story.

> Stanton, citing Sean Puri: "stories are about a 5-second moment of change." (Egri: "even a rock changes.")

---

## Pattern 14: Surprising + Inevitable
**Execute**: Lock the ending and make it land as something you didn't see coming and yet, in retrospect, the only possible one. The audience should reach it and feel "of course it had to be like that" — surprise in the moment, inevitability looking back. Build the middle so the destination is hidden until it arrives, then revealed as having been earned all along.

**Why it works**: Pure surprise feels arbitrary; pure inevitability feels telegraphed and dull. The Aristotelian ending is both at once — the surprise grabs, the inevitability satisfies, and the combination is what makes an ending *mean* something rather than merely stop.

**Success metric**: A reader doesn't predict the ending, but on reflection can trace every step that made it the only outcome. If it merely stopped, or you saw it coming, it fails.

> The ending is "surprising in the moment, inevitable in retrospect." (Aristotle)

---

## Pattern 15: Just Write the Bad Sentence
**Execute**: Finish the sentence now, accept that it will be bad, and rewrite toward the good one. The pressure is off precisely because it's going to be bad. Refuse the seduction of "whistling on the steps of Carnegie Hall" — talking the symphony you'll write instead of writing it. Most people spend more time telling you the symphony they're going to write than the symphony's written. Writing is rewriting; the seventh or eighth draft is where it gets good, so commit to looking bad on the first six.

**Why it works**: The good sentence only exists on the far side of the bad one — you can't reach it without writing the bad one first, and you can't rewrite a sentence you never wrote. The talker substitutes the *idea* of the work for the work, and the symphony never arrives.

**Success metric**: The bad sentence is on the page and you're rewriting it. If you're still describing the masterpiece out loud and nothing is drafted, you're whistling on the steps.

> "Just freaking write the sentence. It's going to be bad, so pressure's off."
