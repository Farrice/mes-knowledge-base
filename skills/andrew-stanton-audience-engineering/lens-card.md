# LENS CARD — Andrew Stanton (story-uncoverer and audience-engagement engineer: premise-dig, spine, clamp)
> Compiled 2026-07-27 from genius.md (canonical). FIRING RULE: this card decides WHETHER this lens has something to say about a draft line; when a lens fires, READ the matching genius.md section before treating. Never treat from the card alone.

## Lenses
### The Core Paradox: The Architect Who Digs
Plan like an architect ("at some point they got to build this house and I want to be ahead of the game"), work like an archaeologist ("a really great story already exists out there… the statue's in the marble, I just uncover it") — and refuse to choose between them. Maximum structural discipline in service of maximum discovery. You take credit only for WHERE you choose to dig and for being AGGRESSIVE in digging — never for what comes up. "To me, writing is rewriting" — the seventh or eighth draft is where it gets good, so commit to looking bad on the first six.
**Test:** Is the plan serving discovery, or protecting a fixed vision? "I'm not a writer… I go, this is what I think I want to say, and hopefully people will help me mess with it correctly."

### The Premise-Sentence Litmus
Distill any piece to a one-sentence premise: character + conflict + conclusion (Nemo: "fear denies a good father from being one" — fear is the character, denies is the conflict). Crucially, do NOT follow the sentence like an instruction: "it's more a litmus test of… an analysis of what I think I've done." Keep writing sentence after sentence — "that's not the right word, that's not the right thing" — and when it's wrong, let the story "be lost for a while."
**Test:** Can you write a TRUE one-sentence premise? If not, you don't understand the piece yet — keep digging. Use it to audit what you've made, never to outline.

### The Spine + One-Liner
Give the protagonist (person, brand, customer, founder) a fixed inner wiring — "a way they're wired up no matter what's going on in life — the core of you" (WALL·E's spine: "to find the beauty"; his one-liner: "irrational love defeats life's programming"). It governs how they "open a book or get up in the morning."
**Test:** A character without a spine is a list of behaviors; a character with one is alive. Does every choice trace to the spine?

### The Clamp Check
"Drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty." Every beat must make the audience want to know what happens next — not just plot mystery (that's the low-hanging fruit); make them dying to know what the NEXT LINE OF DIALOGUE will be. The cable car: a chain always moving under the street; "When did we accidentally unclamp? Did we ever clamp?" The beach ball: the second it drops, the audience checks their watch. Anticipation and uncertainty are conjurable and at the ready — a tapping at the window that grows louder is uncertainty manufactured from nothing.
**Test:** Walk the piece beat by beat: when did the beach ball drop? Why? Goal: "keep you so engaged that you never thought about anything else."

### The Teeter-Totter
One side analyzes ("trying to find a structure, find a meaning, find the patterns I happen to be creating"); the other side just plays ("I just jam… I don't think about anything because it restricts me"). Jump between the two sides and never sit in the middle. When stuck, diagnose which side you're on: over-analyzing → go play with no plan; lost in play → step back and analyze the patterns you're accidentally making.
**Test:** Are you sitting in the middle "sweating" a half-built sentence? Jump to the other side.

### Fit the Dinosaur
When a late discovery contradicts the committed plan, the truth wins: "wait a minute… it's not a Tyrannosaurus Rex, it's a Stegosaurus" — have "the fortitude, the temerity" to rebuild the story to fit the dinosaur you actually uncovered, not the one you promised. Most studios "blink or shrink." Build the process so the pivot is survivable.
**Test:** Is this the dinosaur you dug up, or the one you promised? The courage to change the dinosaur is the difference between good and great.

### Diagnose the Root, Not the Symptom
Trust behavior over stated opinion: "you can literally see them laughing, smiling, leaning forward. Even if the lights come up and they say 'I didn't like it,' I believe how they were when the lights were down." When feedback piles up around a symptom (every smart Nemo note said "recast the neurotic father"; the real fix was an emotional prologue), find the upstream cause. "If I'd compiled the notes from very smart people saying what to fix, I would have changed the wrong thing for the wrong reasons."
**Test:** The note is real; the proposed fix usually isn't. Have you found the single upstream cause?

### The Telling Is the Content
"How you tell something can oftentimes be the reason you're enjoying something more than what's being said… I'd rather have a really bad joke told well." Craft the delivery, not just the substance: control rhythm, withhold exposition, make moments worth savoring. Underwrite the obvious; trust the audience to fill the gap (the Walter Hill trick: line breaks at the exact rhythm you want it read — slow the reader's heart rate to the pace of the piece).
**Test:** Would you rather consume THIS told well than a better idea told poorly? Is anything explained that silence or gesture would carry better?

### Write For Yourself, Trust the Audience
"We've never written for kids. We've just written for ourselves." Build what YOU would want to consume; only check you aren't accidentally excluding anyone. Kids "are huge readers of gesture and tone" — the worry is "adults being too dumb." Run every draft through the entertainment filter ("nothing got through us without becoming more entertaining") and reach for wonder — the involuntary "oh"; "I want some of that juice."
**Test:** Is this more entertaining now than the last draft? Does it invoke the human-condition moment that makes someone humble and appreciative of just existing?

### Just Write the Bad Sentence
"Just freaking write the sentence… it's going to be bad, so pressure's off." You don't reach the good sentence without writing the bad one.
**Test:** Are you writing, or narrating the masterpiece you'll write?

### TED Layer (secondary source; on emphasis conflict the Perell interview wins)
Punchline-first · Make Me Care · the Promise/slingshot · the 2+2 theory (don't give the audience 4 — let them add it up) · guidelines-not-rules, define-by-negation, conditional likability.

## Decision framework (run before any workflow)
1. What's the premise-sentence? 2. What's the spine? 3. Where's the change? Name the fundamental moment of change the whole thing leads to — no change, no story. 4. Am I clamped the whole time? 5. Where does it end — and is it surprising + inevitable ("surprising in the moment, inevitable in retrospect")? 6. Am I uncovering or forcing? 7. Is the telling as good as the content? If output can't answer 1–3, stop and return to them before producing.

## Anti-patterns / calibration
- **The forced premise/spine.** "The trap is that you force one when you run out of time." A forced premise is a false one. → Keep digging; patience over forcing.
- **Whistling on the steps of Carnegie Hall.** "Most people spend more time telling you the symphony they're going to write than the symphony's written." → Just write the sentence; it'll be bad; rewrite.
- **Changing the symptom, not the root.** Acting on the prescribed fix instead of the felt note. → Diagnose the Root.
- **Believing what they say over what they do.** Weighting Q&A opinion over behavior in the dark. → Trust leaning-forward, not "I liked it."
- **Over-exposition.** Explaining what silence or gesture would carry better. → Underwrite; trust the audience.
- **Unclamping.** A beat that doesn't pull forward. → Restore anticipation + uncertainty, or cut the beat.
- **Static.** Nothing changes — the cable car has disconnected. → Find the change, even a pause: "it was active, then it wasn't."
- **Rulebook worship.** Treating any storytelling "rules" (including the fake "22 Rules of Pixar" — "was not generated by us… it's become a myth") as law. → Method, not gospel: "there's never been a tenets that we follow… just because it's online doesn't mean it's real."
- **Writing for an imagined "them."** Dumbing down for a market you've decided can't keep up. → Write for yourself; trust the audience.
- **Naming the machinery.** Labeling the premise/spine/clamp on the page breaks the spell — the audience is "clamped" and "transported" and never once aware of the engineering. Do NOT enumerate patterns, label sections by pattern name, or describe the move — execute it. Keep the scaffolding off the page.
- **Voice calibration when embodying:** warm, self-deprecating, allergic to the auteur myth; metaphor-native, never abstract (reach for the picture, not the theory); patient, anti-rulebook; bittersweet by temperament.
- **Scoring rule:** score ≥8 on any rubric criterion only if you can name the anchor you're matching; if you can't, lower it. Then run the eight-point Stanton-Grade Gate in `references/exemplars.md`.
