# The 30-Second Arc (Andrew Stanton)
A feature runs ninety minutes on one moment of change; an ad has thirty seconds and the viewer's thumb is already moving. This compresses the whole arc to its irreducible core — one change, one clamp set before the skip point, one opposite for instant friction — and cuts everything that does not pull toward it.

For a 30-second arc the change question (#3) is load-bearing: if you cannot name the single moment of change in one line, there is no spot to build.

## Input Required
- The product, offer, or message, and what the viewer should walk away believing or wanting
- The format and exact runtime (6s bumper / 15s pre-roll / 30s Reel / paid social cutdown) and the skip point (when the viewer *can* leave — usually 2-5s)
- The one before→after the spot exists to deliver (current state of the viewer → changed state)
- The two opposing things already living in the brief (price vs. value, effort vs. ease, before vs. after, them vs. you) — raw is fine
- Any hook, line, or visual the author is already attached to

---

## Workflow

### Step 1: Find the Single Moment of Change
A thirty-second spot is not a tiny movie; it is one moment of change with packaging stripped off. Name the one five-second beat where something flips — a face, a number, a state, a belief. Everything else in the spot exists to load that flip or to bank it. If you have two candidate changes, you have two ads; pick the one the offer actually turns on.

Lower the threshold while you hunt: the change can be a held breath releasing, a price crossed out, a posture shift — it does not have to be plot. The test is whether the viewer feels *it was one way, then it wasn't*.

| Candidate change | Stated as before → after | Is it the change the offer turns on? |
|---|---|---|
| (change 1) | (e.g. "drowning in tabs → one clean dashboard") | |
| (change 2) | | |
| (change 3) | | |

Keep one. The other two go in the bank for other spots.

### Step 2: Clamp Before the Skip Point
The viewer can leave at the skip point, so the clamp has to be set *before* it — line one has to manufacture anticipation mingled with uncertainty, not deliver information. A tapping at the window that grows louder is uncertainty made from nothing; a "Hi, we're [Brand]" is the beach ball already on the floor. Write the open as the thing that makes them need the next second, then verify the clamp lands inside the pre-skip window.

| | Draft |
|---|---|
| Skip point (seconds) | |
| Opening beat (must hit before skip) | |
| What it makes the viewer *need to know* | |
| Beach-ball check — could they leave here without losing anything? | (yes = unclamped, rewrite) |

If the open could be removed and the spot still made sense, it was packaging. The first second carries the clamp or nothing does.

### Step 3: Set One Opposite for Instant Drama
Two opposing agendas in collision throw off drama on contact, and a spot has no time to build conflict slowly — so import the opposition and make it visual. Box versus circle, before versus after in the same frame, the cost they fear versus the cost of doing nothing. One opposite, legible before a word is spoken. More than one and the viewer is sorting; exactly one and they feel the friction instantly.

Name the two poles and the single frame that shows them touching:

| Pole A | Pole B | The frame where they collide |
|---|---|---|
| (e.g. "12 disconnected tools") | (e.g. "one screen") | (split-screen, both visible at once) |

### Step 4: Land the Change
Bank the flip from Step 1 at the moment of maximum loaded tension — surprising in the second, inevitable looking back, so the viewer feels "of course." The change is the payoff the clamp was holding for; it arrives once, clean, and is not explained afterward. If the spot keeps talking past the flip, the savor is gone. End on the changed state, not on a recap of it.

### Step 5: Cut Everything That Does Not Serve It
Walk every second and ask one question: does this load the change, deliver the clamp, or carry the opposite? If a beat does none of the three, it is packaging — logo holds, throat-clearing intros, the second benefit, the explainer after the flip. Cut it. Run the surviving cut through the entertainment filter one more time: each remaining second should be more compelling than the one before, or it goes too.

| Second / beat | Loads change? | Carries clamp? | Carries opposite? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (beat) | | | | KEEP / CUT |

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | Treat as a teaser/sting: one image-seed clamp in frame one, the opposite staged visually (box vs. circle), the change as the final beat with no tag after it. The trailer convention of explaining is the beach ball — withhold instead. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The "30-second arc" is the lede + first scroll. The change is the single reversal the piece turns on; clamp the opening line before the reader's scroll-skip; one opposing pair frames the dek. The body can breathe, but the first screen must do all three jobs. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | This is home turf. Line one = the clamp before thumb-stop (~1.5s on Reels). One change, one opposite, no second idea. Reels: the visual opposite is a same-frame before/after; LinkedIn/X: the opposite is the first-line tension, the change is the last line. Cut the CTA that explains. |
| Sales / marketing copy | Hook = clamp before the fold/skip; the offer is the change (their state without it → with it); the opposite is the cost-of-inaction vs. cost-of-the-offer, shown not argued. Cut the feature list that buries the one flip. |
| Brand / campaign | The 30-second arc is the campaign's hero cut; every other asset is a re-angle of the *same* change and the *same* opposite. If an asset introduces a new opposite, it belongs to a different campaign — pull it. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against genius.md § Anti-Patterns + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- Exactly **one** moment of change is named as a before → after; a second change means a second spot, not a busier one.
- The clamp lands **before the skip point**, and the beach-ball check passed — the open makes the viewer need the next second rather than receive information.
- Exactly **one** opposite, and it is visible/legible before a word is spoken; two or more means the viewer is sorting instead of feeling friction.
- The change lands once, clean, with nothing explaining it afterward, and reads surprising-then-inevitable.
- Every surviving second loads the change, carries the clamp, or carries the opposite; no second exists only as packaging.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Front-loading the logo / intro.** The brand bumper is the beach ball on the floor before the clamp ever sets. Recovery: move identity past the clamp; earn the second before you spend it on a name.
- **Two changes fighting for the runtime.** Both flips get half the tension and neither lands. Recovery: pick the change the offer turns on; bank the other for a separate cutdown.
- **Explaining after the flip.** The recap or CTA that talks past the change kills the savor. Recovery: end on the changed state; if a CTA is mandatory, make it the change's destination, not a description of it.
- **An abstract opposite.** A contrast the viewer has to read (a stat vs. a stat in words) is too slow for the window. Recovery: stage it as one frame where both poles are visible at once — box and circle, before and after, side by side.
