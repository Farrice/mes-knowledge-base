# The Change Engine (Andrew Stanton)
A piece is a story only when something fundamentally changes; everything else is a list of events with good production values. This workflow names the one five-second moment of change the whole thing exists to deliver, lowers the threshold for what counts as change so quiet beats still move, audits for the stretches where nothing changes and the cable car has disconnected, and rebuilds every prior beat into the run-up to that single turn.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Name the 5-Second Moment of Change
Before touching structure, find the single fundamental change the whole piece leads to — the turn that, if you cut it, the thing stops being a story. State it as a transition: *from X to Y*. Not "the character grows." From *believing he can protect his son by never letting go* to *letting go so the son can be found*. From *the reader thinks discipline is the answer* to *the reader sees discipline was the avoidance*. One line, two states.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| Where it opens (X) | |
| Where it lands (Y) | |
| The change in one line | from ___ to ___ |
| Premise-sentence it serves | (character + conflict + conclusion) |

If you can't name a real X→Y, you don't have a story yet — you have a topic. Keep digging the premise-sentence (Pattern 1) until the change surfaces; don't force one because the deadline arrived.

### Step 2: Lower the Threshold of Change
A change does not have to be a plot event. A pause is change. An eye shift is change: *it was active, then it was not.* The most powerful turn in the piece is often a micro-movement — a held breath releasing, a decision made in silence, a single word that wasn't there a line ago. Walk the piece and downgrade where you've been over-reaching for incident, and upgrade where a quiet beat actually carries the real turn.

For each candidate beat, decide the *smallest true change* it can carry and still pull the reader forward:

| Beat | Current change (if any) | Smallest true change it can carry |
|---|---|---|
| (beat 1) | | (e.g. "she stops arguing — that's the turn") |
| (beat 2) | | (e.g. "he reads the line twice; on the second pass everything is different") |
| (beat 3) | | |

The fix for a dead scene is usually a smaller, truer change, not a louder, bigger one.

### Step 3: Audit the Static Stretches
Walk the piece beat by beat and find every stretch where *nothing changes* — the cable car has disconnected from the chain. These are the places the reader's attention slides off, even when the prose is clean. Mark each one and decide its fate: a static beat either earns a real (often small) change, or it gets cut. There is no third option where it stays and stalls.

| Beat / paragraph / shot | Does anything change here? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| (beat) | yes (name it) / no | KEEP / ADD-CHANGE / CUT |

Any "no" that you can't convert into even an eye-shift of change is dead weight — cut it. Even a rock changes; it erodes. If a beat truly can't, it isn't a beat.

### Step 4: Make Every Prior Beat the Run-Up
Now reverse-engineer from the Step 1 change. Every beat before it exists for one reason: to make that single turn land as inevitable and earned. Re-order, re-weight, and re-cut so the line to the change stays active the whole way — each beat tightening the pressure toward X→Y, none wandering off it. A beat that doesn't push the reader closer to the change is either re-aimed at it or removed.

| Beat (in order) | How it tightens toward the change | On the line to X→Y? |
|---|---|---|
| (beat) | (the pressure it adds) | yes / re-aim / cut |

Check the destination one more time: when the change finally arrives, is it surprising in the moment and inevitable in retrospect — *of course it had to be like that*? If you saw it coming, the run-up over-explained; if it feels arbitrary, the run-up didn't earn it.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | One governing change per act, one for the whole; the run-up is paced so the turn lands visually, not in dialogue. Lower the threshold ruthlessly — the biggest moment is often a look, not an event. Static stretches get cut or jumped, never serviced with a busy C-scene. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The change is what the reader believes at the end minus what they believed at the start. Every section is a step in the run-up to that single reversal; cut the section that's true but doesn't move the belief. End on the turn, not a recap of it. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | Compress to the single five-second change — that's the whole post. Open at X, close at Y, nothing between them that doesn't tighten the line. No setup that doesn't earn the turn; if a line doesn't move the reader toward the change, it's the beach ball dropping. |
| Sales / marketing copy | The change is the prospect's state shift: from *the problem is unsolvable / not mine* to *this is the move*. Every proof beat is run-up pressure toward that turn. The static stretch is the feature paragraph where the prospect's belief sits still — convert it to a belief-change or cut it. |
| Brand / campaign | Name the one change the campaign exists to create in how the audience sees the category or themselves. Each asset is a beat in that run-up, angled at the same X→Y; the clever asset that wins applause but doesn't advance the change is static spend — cut it. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against genius.md § Anti-Patterns + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- A single fundamental change is named as a real X→Y transition, not "the character grows" or "the reader learns" — and you can point to the exact five-second moment it happens.
- At least one beat's change was lowered to a micro-movement (pause, eye-shift, a held breath releasing) rather than reached for as a plot event; the piece isn't over-plotted to manufacture motion.
- Every static stretch was caught and either given a true change or cut — no beat is allowed to stay and stall, even one with clean prose.
- Every surviving beat before the change is on the line to it; nothing wanders off the run-up, and the change lands as surprising + inevitable, not telegraphed or arbitrary.
- The change is felt, never announced — no line tells the reader "this is the moment everything changes." The architecture stays off the page.

## Common Pitfalls
- **No real change, just motion.** Things happen, nothing fundamentally shifts; the cable car was never clamped. Recovery: return to the premise-sentence and dig until a true X→Y surfaces; a topic with incident is not a story.
- **Reaching for a bigger change instead of a truer one.** Adding a plot event to "raise stakes" when the real turn is a held breath releasing. Recovery: lower the threshold — ask what the smallest true change is, and trust that a pause can clamp harder than an explosion.
- **Sparing a beloved static beat.** A gorgeous passage where nothing changes survives the cut on craft alone. Recovery: run it through the static audit honestly — if it can't carry even an eye-shift of change, cut it regardless of how good the prose is.
- **The run-up that over-explains the turn.** Foreshadowing so heavy the change is telegraphed and the surprise dies. Recovery: underwrite the run-up; trust the reader; let inevitability be felt only in retrospect, not signposted on the way in.
