# Dialogue Aloud (Andrew Stanton)
On the page, clever and true look identical; you only find out which one you wrote when a great actor has to say it out loud. Woody works because the line is what Tom Hanks would say with Stanton's cynical-softy underneath — not because it read well in the script. This workflow voices every line in the speaker's mouth, flags the ones they'd never say, harvests the idioms only they own, and rewrites to what only they could say.

## Input Required
- The draft lines, copy, captions, or script with dialogue or first-person voice
- Who is speaking — character, brand, founder, or persona — and their fixed inner wiring (the Weston spine + one-liner; pull from a prior `/stanton-spine` run if it exists)
- Any real source for the voice: transcripts, voice memos, past posts, an actor or person it's modeled on, recorded idioms
- The medium it ships in (film, essay, post, email, ad) and how the reader will encounter the rhythm

## Workflow

### Step 1: Read Every Line Aloud in Their Mouth
Do not read for sense. Read each line out loud *as the speaker*, and feel the body's response. The test is the fear: would you be afraid of a really good actor having to deliver this — afraid because it's wrong, hollow, or "writer talking through a mask"? A line that survives the actor is alive. A line that makes you wince at the thought of someone *performing* it is the writer, not the character.

Go line by line. For each, log the felt verdict before you reason about it.

| Line (verbatim) | Read aloud as the speaker — felt verdict | Why (one phrase) |
|---|---|---|
| (line 1) | ALIVE / WRITER-TALKING / UNSURE | |
| (line 2) | | |
| (line 3) | | |

UNSURE counts as a fail you haven't named yet. Read it again slower; if the fear is there, it's WRITER-TALKING.

### Step 2: Flag What They'd Never Say
Now go offensive. For every flagged line, name *why* this specific speaker would never say it — and the tell is almost always that it serves the writer, not the wiring. A line betrays the spine when it's smarter, cleaner, more on-brand, or more clever than the person actually is in that moment. Corleone never explains he wants to please his father; he just does. The dead lines are the ones that say the spine out loud instead of acting from it.

| Flagged line | What it serves (writer's wit / brand polish / exposition / theme stated) | Violates spine because… |
|---|---|---|
| (line) | | |

Anything that explains the character's own motivation, lands a joke the character isn't trying to land, or sounds like the brand's deck rather than a person — flag it.

### Step 3: Harvest What's Only Theirs
Before rewriting, mine the real voice. Performance generates dialogue, it doesn't just interpret it — the speaker's idioms, hesitations, and quirks are free material to lift straight into the line. Go to the source: transcripts, voice memos, the way this person actually talks, the actor they're modeled on. Pull the verbal fingerprints.

| Fingerprint | Example from source | Where it can land |
|---|---|---|
| Signature idiom / phrase | | |
| Hesitation / filler / rhythm | | |
| What they say *instead* of the obvious | | |
| Quirk / tic / mispronunciation / under-statement | | |

If there's no source, generate the fingerprints from the spine: what would *this wiring* repeat, dodge, undersell, or get wrong?

### Step 4: Rewrite to What Only They Would Say
Rewrite each dead line so the speaker, and only the speaker, could have said it — built from the harvested fingerprints, governed by the spine, never explaining itself. Aim for the line a great actor would *want* to say. Then read it aloud again. The fear should be gone; what's left should sound inevitable in their mouth and impossible in anyone else's.

| Original (dead) | Rewrite (only-they) | Aloud re-test: fear gone? |
|---|---|---|
| (line) | | yes / no — redo |

Withhold where the obvious line wanted to over-explain — the telling is the content, and a beat unsaid in their voice often beats a beat spelled out.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | Read in the actual actor's cadence; harvest from table reads and recording-booth takes. Woody's lines are Tom Hanks plus the cynical-softy spine — let the performer's idioms rewrite the page, not the other way around. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The "speaker" is the narrating voice. Read paragraphs aloud; flag any sentence that's the writer being clever at the reader's expense rather than the persona thinking on the page. Harvest the author's real verbal tics from past pieces. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | Read the hook and the close aloud first — those carry the most writer-voice. Cut any line that sounds like "a LinkedIn post" instead of this person talking. One harvested idiom in the hook beats a polished generic opener. |
| Sales / marketing copy | Read it as the *customer's* trusted voice, not the brand's deck. Flag every line that serves the brand's ego over the reader's ear. Harvest the customer's own words from reviews, calls, and DMs and put them in the speaker's mouth. |
| Brand / campaign | The brand has a spine and a one-liner; the voice is a character. Read campaign lines aloud as that character — kill the tagline-that-no-human-says, keep the line a real spokesperson would be proud to deliver. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against genius.md § Anti-Patterns + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- Every line was actually read aloud in the speaker's voice and carries a felt verdict — not skimmed silently for sense.
- No surviving line explains the speaker's own motivation or states the spine out loud; the wiring is *acted*, not narrated.
- At least one harvested fingerprint from the real source (or generated from the spine) made it into the rewrites — the voice is sourced, not invented from generic "brand tone."
- Each rewrite is something only this speaker could say; swap the name in and it should sound wrong in anyone else's mouth.
- The rewritten lines pass the re-test: the fear of a great actor saying them is gone.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Reading for sense, not for sound.** Silent reading hides the wince; clever and true look identical on the page. Recovery: actually say it out loud, in their voice, and trust the body's flinch over the eye's approval.
- **Polishing toward "good writing."** The smoothest, wittiest version is often the writer talking through a mask. Recovery: ask whose wit it is — if it's yours and not the character's, it's a flag, however nice it reads.
- **Rewriting from imagination instead of source.** Inventing idioms produces generic voice. Recovery: go harvest the real transcripts, reviews, or recordings first; lift fingerprints before you write a single new line.
- **Stating the spine in dialogue.** Having the speaker announce what they want kills it. Recovery: cut the explanation; let the line *do* the wiring the way Corleone never says he's pleasing his father.
