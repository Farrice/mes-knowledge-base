# The Brand Origin (Andrew Stanton)
A brand origin is not a timeline of how the company got founded — it is a character with a fixed inner wiring, pushed against its opposite, that exists to make one specific change in the world. WALL·E started as a feeling (a robot too lonely to know its job was futile) and only became a story when it found its opposite. A brand origin works the same way: find the spine, find the opposite that creates the drama, name the change, and land one beat of wonder.

## Input Required
- The brand or founder, and what it actually sells (product, service, listing, party, drop)
- Who it's for — the customer in one specific sentence, not a demographic
- The real beginning: the feeling, frustration, or moment the brand came from (raw is fine — a feeling beats a fact here)
- What the world looks like *without* this brand (the status quo it exists against)
- Where you'll deploy this (about page, founder LinkedIn post, pitch, listing bio, brand book)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Give the Brand a Spine + One-Liner
Before any timeline, decide the one thing this brand is solving in everything it does — the way it's "wired up no matter what's going on." WALL·E's spine was *to find the beauty*; his one-liner was *irrational love defeats life's programming*. A brand without a spine is a list of products and dates. A brand with one is alive, and every choice — pricing, tone, what it refuses to sell — traces back to it.

Write the spine as a fixed inner drive (a verb the brand can't stop doing), then a one-liner that compresses the whole worldview into a single charged sentence.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| **Spine** (the verb it can't stop doing) | *to ___* (e.g. "to make the overlooked feel chosen") |
| **One-liner** (the worldview, charged) | *___* (e.g. "the people who do the work deserve the spotlight") |
| **Proof it's the real spine** | name 2 brand choices (a product, a refusal) that both trace back to it |

If two of the brand's real choices don't both trace to the spine, the spine is forced. Keep digging — a forced spine is a false one.

### Step 2: Find the Central Complication
The spine alone is inert until something denies it. State the brand's origin as a one-sentence premise: character + conflict + conclusion, the way *Nemo* landed on "fear denies a good father from being one." The conflict is the verb in the middle — the thing standing between the brand's spine and the world it wants.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| **Character** | the brand's spine in one word (its drive) |
| **Conflict** (the *denies* verb) | what stands in its way — the market lie, the friction, the thing customers settle for |
| **Conclusion** | what the brand proves by the end |
| **Premise sentence** | "___ denies / defeats / breaks ___ from / into ___" |

Use this as a litmus on the origin you're about to write, not as an outline. If the sentence won't ring true, the origin isn't found yet.

### Step 3: Set the Opposites Conflict
Opposing agendas create conflict, conflict creates drama. WALL·E only worked when Stanton uncovered the opposite — another robot from somewhere else, an opposite in agenda and even in shape ("he was a box, she was a circle"). A brand origin needs the same charge: the brand pressed against its clean opposite, so the reader feels a real tension, not a list of features.

Name both poles, then make the opposition concrete and even visual:

| Pole | Fill in |
|---|---|
| **The brand** | what it is, in its purest form |
| **The opposite it exists against** | the status quo / the way everyone else does it / what the customer settles for |
| **Why they can't coexist** | the agenda that puts them in conflict |
| **The visual or felt contrast** | the box-vs-circle of it — one image that holds the opposition |

The opposite is not a competitor's name. It's the worldview the brand was born to push against. If there's no real opposite, there's no drama, and the origin reads as a résumé.

### Step 4: Name the Change the Brand Exists to Create
No change, no story — and that holds for brands. Stanton anchors on Sean Puri's "5-second moment of change": a real before-and-after for one person. Name the precise change this brand makes in a single customer's life. Not "we help people" — the active, felt shift from one state to its opposite.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| **Before** (the customer's state without the brand) | ___ |
| **After** (the state the brand creates) | ___ |
| **The 5-second moment** | the single beat where the change becomes visible/felt for one real person |

If "before" and "after" aren't genuine opposites, the change is cosmetic. The origin exists to make this change land — everything else is in service of it.

### Step 5: Place One Wonder Beat
End on wonder — the involuntary "oh" a puppy or a lonely robot produces, the human-condition moment that makes someone humble and appreciative of just existing. This is what started WALL·E: *"I want some of that juice."* One beat. Not a slogan, not a CTA — a single image or line where the reader feels the larger thing the brand is really about, told well enough that the *how* outclasses the *what*.

Draft three candidate wonder beats, then keep the one that produces an actual "oh" — and let the telling carry it (rhythm, restraint, one image worth savoring), never an explanation of the feeling.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | Open on the wonder beat as a cold image (the lonely robot, the empty room before the change); withhold the founder narration. Let the opposites play as visual contrast before a single word of "our story." The spine is the through-line; never have a character state it. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The origin is the premise sentence answered slowly. Spine in the first movement, the opposite it pushes against in the second, the change made concrete through one real customer in the third, the wonder beat as the close that deepens rather than wraps. Cut the neat "and that's why we exist" takeaway. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | One opposite, one change, one wonder beat. Lead with the before-state (the customer settling), turn on the moment of change, close on the wonder image. The spine governs the post but is never stated. No "here's our origin story" framing — drop straight into the moment. |
| Sales / marketing copy | The opposite IS the positioning — the brand against the thing the market settles for. The change is the promise, stated as a felt before/after, not a feature list. The wonder beat is the proof of stakes: what it means for one person when the change lands. |
| Brand / campaign | Spine + one-liner become the brand's fixed wiring across every asset; the opposite is the brand's real conflict (not its tagline); the change is the through-line each asset turns toward; the wonder beat is the campaign's hero moment. Any asset that doesn't trace to the spine is decoration — cut it. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against genius.md § Anti-Patterns + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- The spine is a single fixed inner drive, and at least two real brand choices both trace back to it — not a list of values (Spine Integrity).
- There is a genuine **opposite** the brand pushes against, concrete enough to hold one image; "no real opposite" means no drama — go back and find it (opposites create drama).
- The change names a true before→after for one specific person, and before/after are real opposites, not "we help people" (the 5-second moment).
- Exactly one wonder beat, and it produces an involuntary "oh" when read aloud; it's shown, not explained (Wonder; Telling Quality).
- The spine, premise, and machinery are nowhere stated on the page — the reader feels transported, never instructed (Naming the machinery).

## Common Pitfalls
- **Timeline-as-origin.** Founding dates and milestones with no spine read as a résumé, not a story. Recovery: throw out the chronology, find the one verb the brand can't stop doing, and rebuild the origin around the spine.
- **No real opposite.** A brand described only by what it is (not what it's against) has no conflict and therefore no drama. Recovery: name the worldview the brand was born to push against — the box to its circle — and write the origin as that tension.
- **Cosmetic change.** "Before" and "after" that aren't true opposites give a change the reader doesn't feel. Recovery: anchor on one real person and the single 5-second beat where their state flips; if you can't picture it, the change isn't found yet.
- **Wonder as slogan.** A motivational line bolted on the end instead of an earned image kills the "oh." Recovery: draft three candidate beats, keep only the one that lands shown-not-told, and let the telling (rhythm, restraint, one savorable image) carry it.
