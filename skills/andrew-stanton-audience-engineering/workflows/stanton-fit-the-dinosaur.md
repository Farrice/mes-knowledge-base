# Fit the Dinosaur (Andrew Stanton)
You promised a T-Rex and three bones in you realize you dug up a Stegosaurus. This is the rescue for that moment: the work has already revealed what it actually is, the revelation contradicts the plan you sold, and most teams blink or shrink rather than rebuild. This workflow gives you the nerve and the surgical map to fix the story to fit the dinosaur you actually found.

## Input Required
- The committed plan: the premise, ending, or structure you promised (to a client, an editor, an exec, yourself)
- The discovery: the thing the work surfaced that contradicts that plan — the bone that's the wrong shape
- The artifact as it stands: draft, reel, outline, deck, sequence — whatever is built so far
- What's locked vs. movable: deadlines, approvals, assets already produced, anything genuinely immovable
- The through-line you must protect: the one premise-sentence or spine the rescue cannot break

## Workflow

### Step 1: Name Both Dinosaurs
Put the promise and the discovery side by side, in plain language, before you touch the structure. The promised dinosaur is what everyone signed off on; the uncovered one is what the bones actually spell. Most teams never name the second out loud because saying it makes the rebuild real — so say it.

| | Promised dinosaur (what you sold) | Uncovered dinosaur (what you dug up) |
|---|---|---|
| Premise-sentence | (character + conflict + conclusion you pitched) | (the truer one the work keeps reaching for) |
| The change it drives | (the arc the plan promised) | (the arc the real material wants) |
| Where they collide | (the exact beat/section/claim where promise and discovery contradict) | |

If the two columns are nearly identical, you don't have a dinosaur problem — you have a polish problem, and this is the wrong workflow. The collision row must name a real contradiction, not a refinement.

### Step 2: Confirm the Truth Is Real, Not Fatigue
A late pivot driven by exhaustion wrecks good work; a late pivot driven by truth saves it. Your own resistance — or your own restless urge to blow it up — is an unreliable signal at this stage. Test the discovery against evidence, not feeling. On *Nemo*, the real note wasn't "the father is annoying" (the symptom everyone voiced) — it was that the audience hadn't been given the emotional ground to forgive him. Trust what the work *does* to people, not what you or anyone says about it.

| Test | Question | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior, not opinion | What does the artifact actually *do* — where do readers/viewers lean in, drop off, push back? | The discovery explains observed behavior, not just a stated note |
| Root, not symptom | Is this the upstream cause, or am I refitting to a surface complaint? | It's the cause a stack of notes all point back to |
| Survives a night | Does the discovery still ring true after sleeping on it / re-reading cold? | It holds when the fatigue and the deadline panic drain off |
| Not just novelty | Am I drawn to the new dinosaur because it's truer, or because I'm bored of the old one? | Truer, with evidence — not relief from the work |

If it fails these, stop. A forced pivot is a forced premise wearing a rescue costume. Keep digging or keep the original.

### Step 3: Lower the Stakes — "It's Just a File"
Stanton resisted the *Nemo* prologue hard until Lee Unkrich reframed it: *"it's just a file, we don't have to show anybody."* The dread of a big rebuild is mostly the dread of the *commitment*, not the work. Strip the commitment. You are running an experiment on a copy, accountable to no one, reversible in one keystroke.

- Duplicate the artifact. The refit happens on the copy. The original stays untouched and recoverable.
- Frame it as a throwaway test: "I'll spend [bounded time] refitting to the real dinosaur. If it's worse, I delete the file and we lost nothing."
- Write the bad version fast. The first refit will be rough — that's the point. You can't judge the new dinosaur until it stands up, even badly.
- Do not loop anyone in yet. Approval pressure is what makes teams shrink. Prove it to yourself on the file first, then bring the result, not the proposal.

### Step 4: List the Surgical Changes to Refit
Now map exactly what moves so the work fits the uncovered dinosaur. The discipline is surgical, not demolition: change only what the new truth requires, and let the downstream problems fall away rather than fixing each one. The *Nemo* prologue was a single upstream move that dissolved a dozen scattered notes untouched — find that one move before you start rewriting everything.

| Change | What it is now | What it becomes | Why the real dinosaur requires it | Downstream notes it dissolves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The upstream move | (the one structural change closest to the root) | | | (the surface problems this alone fixes) |
| Secondary refit | (beat/section that must follow from the upstream move) | | | |
| Cut | (what the old dinosaur needed but the real one doesn't) | — | | |
| Salvage | (asset/scene/line built for the old plan, repurposed) | | | |

Rank by leverage: do the single upstream move first, then re-read. Often half your planned secondary changes are no longer needed — they were symptoms of the wrong dinosaur, and the root move already cured them.

### Step 5: Protect the Through-Line
A refit is not a rewrite. Something made the original worth doing — the spine, the premise-sentence, the core change. Name it, and make the rescue serve it rather than replace it. You're changing the dinosaur, not the reason anyone cared.

- State the one thing that must survive intact: the premise-sentence or the spine from Pre-Flight.
- Walk the refitted artifact and confirm that thing is *stronger*, not just preserved — the right pivot deepens the through-line, it doesn't dilute it.
- Confirm the ending still lands surprising *and* inevitable. The refit should make the new ending feel like the only one that was ever possible, not a swerve.
- Re-run the clamp: does every beat still pull forward, or did the surgery leave a slack patch where the old structure used to carry the momentum?

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | The dinosaur is usually a character's true want or the real engine of the conflict. Refit by relocating the upstream beat (often a prologue/early reveal), as with the *Nemo* prologue — one move that re-grounds empathy and dissolves act-two and act-three notes. Protect the spine; let the production assets you can salvage (storyboards, VO) be repurposed, not scrapped. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The promised dinosaur is your thesis; the uncovered one is the truer argument the research kept surfacing. Refit by rewriting the framing claim and the lede, then let the body sections re-sort under the real thesis — most paragraphs survive, re-pointed. Kill the neat takeaway built for the abandoned thesis. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | Cheap to refit, so do it ruthlessly. The dinosaur is usually the hook's promise vs. the actual insight in the body. Rewrite the hook to the real payoff rather than bending the payoff to a hook that no longer fits. One post, one true claim — discard the draft and reshoot from the uncovered dinosaur. |
| Sales / marketing copy | The dinosaur is the real buyer belief or mechanism the testing revealed, contradicting the angle you committed to. Refit the lead and the core promise to the true mechanism; salvage proof, bullets, and CTA that still serve it. Don't patch a dead angle — the upstream move is a new lead, not a stack of tweaks. |
| Brand / campaign | The dinosaur is the brand's actual conflict or audience truth, surfaced mid-build against a locked positioning. Refit the central narrative and protect the brand spine; re-point each asset as an angle on the real conflict. Salvage produced assets by recontextualizing, not reshooting, wherever the new dinosaur allows. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against genius.md § Anti-Patterns + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- The uncovered dinosaur is a genuine contradiction of the promise, not a refinement — the collision row names a real conflict, or this was the wrong workflow.
- The pivot passed the truth check on *evidence* (behavior, root, survives-a-night), not on fatigue or boredom — a forced pivot is a forced premise and fails Truth vs. Forced.
- The refit found a single upstream move that dissolves downstream notes rather than patching each symptom — surgical, not demolition (Pattern 7).
- The through-line (premise-sentence / spine) survives *stronger*, and the new ending reads surprising + inevitable — the refit deepened the work, didn't dilute it.
- The clamp holds across the refitted artifact: no slack patch where the old structure used to carry forward pull.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Refitting on fatigue, not truth.** Late in a build, the urge to blow it up can be exhaustion in disguise; your own resistance and your own restlessness are both unreliable here. Recovery: run Step 2's evidence tests; if the discovery only "feels" true after midnight, sleep on it before you cut anything.
- **Demolition instead of surgery.** Treating the pivot as license to rewrite everything wastes the salvageable work and usually breaks the through-line. Recovery: find the one upstream move (Pattern 7), make only that, re-read — most of your planned changes were symptoms of the wrong dinosaur.
- **Skipping "it's just a file."** Refitting in place, under approval pressure, makes teams shrink the pivot to something safe and wrong. Recovery: duplicate the artifact, run the rebuild as a throwaway experiment, bring the *result* to stakeholders, not the proposal.
- **Losing the reason it mattered.** Chasing the new dinosaur so hard you abandon the spine or premise-sentence that made the work worth doing. Recovery: name the through-line before you cut, and confirm at the end that the refit made it stronger, not merely intact.
