# The Opposites Engine (Andrew Stanton)
*Trash Planet* was a feeling — a robot alone with a futile job — until the unlock arrived as an opposite: a second robot from somewhere else, wired the other way, a box meeting a circle. This workflow takes a flat premise (one force, one mood, one note held) and finds the opposing force that makes it move. Two agendas that can't simply get along generate conflict, conflict generates drama, drama generates will-they-won't-they, and the audience stays clamped waiting to find out.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Name Both Forces and Make Them Oppose
Start with the force you already have, then build its opposite from the spine outward — not its mirror image. The trap is making the opposite 100% opposite, which gives you a cartoon and no friction. *Conflicting* agendas beat *contrary* ones: WALL·E is wired to find the beauty and keep what others discard; EVE is wired to complete the directive and move on. They want different things badly enough to collide, not so totally that no relationship is possible.

| | Force A (what you have) | Force B (the opposite to build) |
|---|---|---|
| Spine (wired to…) | | |
| Wants, right now | | |
| Method / how it moves through the world | | |
| What it can't stand about the other | | |
| The thing they secretly share | (the bridge that makes them care) | (same) |

Pressure-test: if A and B could comfortably agree, the opposition is too soft — sharpen the agendas. If A and B could never occupy the same scene without one destroying the other, it's too hard — find the shared thing that keeps them in the room.

### Step 2: Build the Obstacle to Them Simply Getting Along
Opposition without an obstacle is two people who argue once and then sort it out off-screen. Drama lives in the wall that keeps the resolution out of reach. Name the structural reason these two can't just talk it through — incompatible jobs, mismatched information, a directive one of them is sworn to, a world that punishes the connection. The obstacle is what converts a single disagreement into a sustained current the audience gets pulled along.

- The collision (the moment their agendas first hit): __________
- Why they can't resolve it in one conversation: __________
- The stakes if the opposition wins (no connection) vs. if it's bridged: __________
- What each has to give up to cross toward the other: __________

### Step 3: Render It Visually and Concretely
Don't leave the opposition in the abstract — give it a shape, a silhouette, a prop, a color, a rhythm. WALL·E was a box; EVE was a circle. The contrast read before a single line of dialogue. Find the physical, on-the-page equivalent so the reader/viewer *feels* the opposition without being told it exists. The telling carries the content here: the box-versus-circle did more work than any line explaining that they were different.

| Force | Concrete render |
|---|---|
| A | (shape / texture / sound / pace / image — e.g. "a box, rusted, slow, hoarding") |
| B | (its visible counter — e.g. "a circle, clean, fast, scanning") |

Translate the same contrast into the piece's actual medium: short clipped sentences vs. long flowing ones, a hard color vs. a soft one, a grounded shot vs. a floating one, a buyer's plain language vs. a brand's polished line.

### Step 4: Ride the Will-They-Won't-They Tension
Opposition is the engine; the clamp is the ride. Anticipation mingled with uncertainty means the audience must keep wanting to know whether the two forces will cross — and never feel safe that they will. Map the swing: a beat of approach, a beat of recoil, a beat that raises the cost of crossing. Hold the answer until it's surprising in the moment and inevitable in retrospect. The second the outcome feels settled, the beach ball drops and they check their watch.

| Beat | Approach or recoil? | What makes the audience still uncertain |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | | |
| 2 | | |
| 3 | | |
| (resolution) | the cross — or the refusal | surprising + inevitable? |

If any beat lets the audience relax into "of course they'll end up together," re-introduce doubt — raise the obstacle, cost one of them something, or have the closer force pull back.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | Cast the two forces as protagonist and counter-force; render the opposition in silhouette/blocking/sound design (box vs. circle) so it reads pre-dialogue; structure the reels as the approach-recoil swing toward a surprising-inevitable cross. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The two forces are opposing ideas or worldviews, not characters. Hold both honestly through the piece; the reader rides the tension of which one wins. Resolve only at the end, and make the resolution feel earned, not pre-announced in the thesis. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | One sharp opposition, rendered fast. Open on the collision (the two agendas hitting), withhold which side wins until the last line. The contrast itself is the hook; the unresolved cross is the reason they finish. |
| Sales / marketing copy | The opposition is the customer's two competing pulls — the desire vs. the fear, the old identity vs. the wanted one. Dramatize the tug; the offer is the bridge that lets them cross. Render it concretely (before-state vs. after-state as two visible shapes), never as a feature list. |
| Brand / campaign | Find the brand's real internal tension — what it stands for vs. what the market pulls it toward — and stage it as opposing forces across the campaign. Each asset is one swing of the approach-recoil; the campaign resolves the tension only at the payoff. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- The two forces *conflict* without being 100% opposite — they could not comfortably agree, but they share something that keeps them caring (Pattern 12). A perfectly inverted twin fails.
- A real obstacle keeps the resolution out of reach; the opposition is a sustained current, not a single argument that resolves off-screen.
- The opposition is *rendered* concretely — a shape, color, pace, or image carries it before any explanation does (box vs. circle). If the only proof of opposition is stated in the text, it isn't built yet.
- Every swing beat keeps the audience uncertain whether the forces will cross; nowhere can they relax into the obvious outcome (Pattern 3 — the clamp).
- The resolution is surprising in the moment and inevitable in retrospect, and traces back to the spine — not bolted on to be tidy.

## Common Pitfalls
- **100% opposite = no drama.** Perfect mirror-image forces have nothing to negotiate and nothing to share, so the audience can't care. Recovery: rebuild B from A's spine so the agendas *conflict* but a bridge remains.
- **Opposition stated, not rendered.** Telling the reader the two are different does nothing; WALL·E's box and EVE's circle did the work silently. Recovery: assign each force a shape/color/pace/sound and let the contrast read before a single line explains it.
- **The obstacle is missing.** Two forces that resolve their conflict in one scene give you a moment, not an engine. Recovery: install a structural wall (jobs, directive, information gap) so getting along stays out of reach.
- **The cross feels safe too early.** Once the audience is sure they'll end up together, the beach ball drops. Recovery: cost one side something, raise the obstacle, or have the closer force recoil — keep anticipation mingled with uncertainty to the last beat.
