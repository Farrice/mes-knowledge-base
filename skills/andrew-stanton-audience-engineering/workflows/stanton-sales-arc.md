# The Sales Arc (Andrew Stanton)
A sales page is a story the reader is free to abandon at every scroll, so it is the purest test of the clamp: the cable car is connected to the chain or it isn't, and the second the reader's eye drifts to another tab the beach ball has dropped. This workflow builds the page as a continuous arc — premise-sentence first, a spine for the customer, a clamp on every section, one fundamental change, and a close that surprises in the moment and feels inevitable in retrospect.

## Input Required
- The offer — what it is, what it costs, what it concretely delivers
- The customer — who buys, in their words; their current situation and the moment they're in
- The transformation — the before-state and the after-state, stated as a state of being, not a feature list
- The mechanism — why this works (the thing they haven't tried, or have tried wrong)
- Proof on hand — results, testimonials, demonstrations, credentials
- The format and length it ships in (long-form sales page, VSL script, short landing page)
- The single action at the end (buy, book, apply, opt in) and its price/terms

## Workflow

### Step 1: Write the Premise-Sentence of the Offer
Distill the entire offer to one true sentence: **customer + conflict + transformation**. This is the *Nemo* "fear denies a good father from being one" move applied to a sale — one line that tells you the structure of the whole page. It is a litmus test, not a headline; you audit the page against it, you don't print it. If you can't write a true one, you don't understand the offer yet — keep digging before you write a word of copy.

Write the sentence several times and let the wrong ones be wrong. "That's not the right word, that's not the right thing." Stop when it rings.

| Element | Fill in |
|---|---|
| Customer (who, in their actual life) | |
| Conflict (the force denying them the after-state) | |
| Transformation (the state they reach when the conflict resolves) | |
| **Premise-sentence** (one line: *[conflict] denies [customer] from [after-state]*) | |

The conflict is the verb that does the work. "Fear denies." "Confusion denies." "The thing they were sold instead denies." Name the force, not the symptom — root, not symptom. If the page is really about *being talked out of the thing they already wanted*, the premise is about that, not about your feature set.

### Step 2: Give the Customer a Spine
The hero of this page is the customer, and a customer without a spine is a list of pain points. Give them a fixed inner wiring (Weston) plus a one-liner — the one thing they are solving in everything they do, the thing that governs how they "get up in the morning." WALL·E's spine was "to find the beauty"; his one-liner was "irrational love defeats life's programming." Find the customer's.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| Spine (the fixed wiring — what they are always, underneath everything, trying to be or protect) | |
| One-liner (the governing sentence) | |
| What this spine makes them *want* (the surface goal they'll admit to) | |
| What this spine makes them *need* (the real thing, which they may resist) | |

Want vs. need is the engine of the arc. The page opens on the want — the thing they came in searching for — and the close delivers the need, the thing the spine was actually reaching for the whole time. The transformation lands hardest when it satisfies the need they didn't come in able to name.

### Step 3: Clamp Every Section
Drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty. Walk the page section by section as one continuous ride and audit each for forward pull: does this section make the reader need to know what the *next line* says, not just what the next section is about? The low-hanging fruit is plot mystery ("keep reading to find out"); the real clamp makes them unable to look away from the sentence in front of them.

For each section, name what is being anticipated and what stays uncertain. Where neither exists, the beach ball has dropped — re-clamp or cut.

| Section | What the reader anticipates here | What stays uncertain (the open loop) | Clamped? (Y / dropped) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook / first screen | | | |
| Problem / current state | | | |
| Mechanism (why it works) | | | |
| Proof | | | |
| Offer / stack | | | |
| Close / CTA | | | |

Anticipation and uncertainty are conjurable from nothing — a tapping at the window that grows louder is tension manufactured out of air. A section that only *informs* has unclamped. Open a loop at the top of each section that the next section pays off, then opens its own. The reader should be pulled down the page the way the cable car is pulled up the hill, never once choosing to keep going.

And mind the telling, not only the content: a true claim delivered flat unclamps as fast as a weak one. Control the rhythm — short lines for the moments worth savoring, restraint where you're tempted to over-explain. A good proof point told poorly loses to a modest one told well.

### Step 4: Stage the Fundamental Change
Every story is built around a moment of change; a sales page is the customer crossing from the before-state to the after-state, and the page exists to make that single crossing land. Locate it. Most pages diffuse the change across a feature list; find the one moment the reader *becomes* the person who buys — the realization, the reframe, the proof that makes the old way impossible to keep living in.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| The before-state (who they are at the top of the page) | |
| The change-moment (the single beat where they cross — a reframe, a mechanism reveal, a proof that recodes everything) | |
| The after-state (who they are once they cross) | |
| Where on the page the change lands | |

This is the spine of the arc, not a section. Everything before it builds the pressure that makes the crossing inevitable; everything after it lives in the new state. If you can't name one fundamental change, the page is static — informative but inert — and a static page is a disconnected cable car. No change, no sale.

### Step 5: Close Surprising + Inevitable
The close should be surprising in the moment and inevitable in retrospect — they didn't see exactly *this* coming, yet "of course it had to be like that." Resolve the want into the need the spine was reaching for. The surprise is usually a reframe of the price or the decision; the inevitability comes from the premise-sentence and the change having earned it the whole way down.

| | Fill in |
|---|---|
| The expected close (the obvious "buy now" they're bracing for) | |
| The surprising turn (the reframe that lands instead — what the purchase *really* is, the cost of *not* crossing, the spine satisfied) | |
| Why it's inevitable (which earlier beats make this the only possible ending) | |
| The single action + terms | |

Avoid the forced close — the manufactured-urgency stinger bolted on because patience ran out. If the inevitability isn't already built into the arc, no countdown timer rescues it; go back and earn it. The strongest close makes the reader feel they'd be betraying the spine you gave them *not* to act.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | N/A for the page itself; if this is a brand film *as* a sale, run the arc as scene beats and let the change be dramatized, never stated. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | Run the full five steps; the change-moment is the reframe the whole essay was reaching for, and the offer/close is a single earned ask at the end, not a pitch grafted on. Keep the premise invisible. |
| VSL script | Format the spoken lines like Stanton's Walter Hill trick — short, rhythm-broken lines at the pace you want them *heard*, so the watcher's heart rate matches the script. Clamp per ~30-second unit; the change-moment is the demonstration beat. Every retention dip is an unclamp — re-cut it. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | Compress to one clamp, one change, one surprising line. The post that states the offer up front has spent its tension; hold the want live and deliver the need in the last line. One open loop, paid off, not five. |
| Sales / marketing copy (page / landing) | The default. Section order serves the arc, not a template; every block names its clamp or gets cut. Headline carries the premise's *feeling*, never its words. |
| Brand / campaign | The premise-sentence is the campaign's real conflict; each asset is one angle of the same arc, and the change-moment is consistent across touchpoints. The "clever" asset that wins applause but breaks the spine is the beach ball — cut it. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against genius.md § Anti-Patterns + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- A true premise-sentence (customer + conflict + transformation) exists and silently organizes the page; it is audited against, never printed verbatim.
- The customer has a fixed spine and one-liner; want and need are distinct, and the close delivers the need.
- Every section names its clamp; no block survives whose only function is to inform. The drop points were re-clamped or cut.
- Exactly one fundamental change is staged and located on the page; the page is not a diffuse feature list.
- The close is surprising in the moment and inevitable in retrospect, earned by the arc — not a forced-urgency stinger bolted on.
- The machinery is invisible: no section is labeled "the clamp" or "the spine," and the reader feels pulled, never engineered.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Writing the page as a feature stack, not an arc.** A bulleted list of benefits has no spine and no change; the reader skims and leaves. Recovery: return to Steps 1, 2, and 4 — premise, spine, change — and rebuild the order around the single crossing.
- **Clamping only the headline.** The hook pulls them in, then the middle informs and the beach ball drops at "the problem" section. Recovery: open a loop at the top of *every* section and pay it off at the next; audit the slack section and re-clamp or cut.
- **A forced close.** Manufactured urgency grafted onto a page that never earned the change. Recovery: don't add a timer — go back and build the inevitability into the arc so the close lands as "of course," not "or else."
- **Printing the premise as the headline.** Stamping "fear denies you from X" on the page names the machinery and breaks the spell. Recovery: let the headline carry the *feeling* of the premise; keep the sentence in your notes as the litmus.
