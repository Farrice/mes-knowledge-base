# Telling Over Content (Andrew Stanton)
Stanton would rather have a bad joke told well than a good joke told poorly. This workflow takes a draft whose *substance* is already decided and re-engineers its *delivery* — where exposition can give way to silence or gesture, where to break the line so it reads at the pace you want it watched, which moments to let the reader savor and which to get past. The idea stays fixed; the telling gets crafted.

## Input Required
- The draft to elevate (prose, script, post, copy section — must already be substantively decided)
- The premise-sentence the draft is serving (so restraint doesn't sand off what matters)
- The format and where it ships (read on a page? watched? scrolled on a phone?)
- The reader/viewer heart rate you want at the *end* — racing, held, slowed, gut-punched, hushed
- Any line, beat, or image the author has marked as a moment to land (vs. throat-clearing to get past)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Find Where Exposition Is Doing Work Silence Could Do Better
Walk the draft and mark every place it *explains* something the reader could feel, infer, or watch happen. Stanton learned this from silent film — Chaplin and Keaton lost nothing without dialogue, and exposition added past a point only subtracted. The note that piles up around a clunky line is usually real; the fix of "rewrite the line" is usually the symptom. The root is that the line shouldn't be there at all.

For each flagged spot, decide what carries it instead: a gesture, an image, an unstated implication, or nothing (a cut).

| Line / passage that over-explains | What it's spelling out | What carries it better | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| (passage 1) | (the thing being said outright) | gesture / image / implication / cut | UNDERWRITE / CUT / KEEP |
| (passage 2) | | | |
| (passage 3) | | | |

Rule of thumb: if the reader would have understood it one line later from action alone, the explanation is theft. Trust them — they're better readers of gesture and tone than you fear. KEEP only the exposition the piece would collapse without.

### Step 2: Break the Delivery to the Rhythm You Want It Read
Now set the *pace of reading* to match the pace of experiencing. On WALL·E, Stanton stole Walter Hill's 1979 *Alien* trick: break description into short, left-justified lines so the eye can't skim it, broken at the exact rhythm he wanted it read. "The door opens" — break — "steam." It looks like dialogue, so no one skips it, and the line breaks slow the heart rate to the pace of the movie.

Take your highest-stakes passage and re-break it. A long unbroken sentence reads fast and flat; a broken one forces the beat. Set the breaks where you want the reader to inhale.

| Passage | Current rhythm | Re-broken for the pace you want | Why the break lands here |
|---|---|---|---|
| (the key passage) | (one run-on block / flat) | (line / break / line) | (savor / dread / snap) |

Match the break to the feeling: short stacked lines for held breath or dread; one long unbroken line to rush past something; a single word on its own line for the snap. The form is doing the work the content can't.

### Step 3: Mark the Moments Worth Savoring vs. Get to the Point
Not every beat deserves equal weight. Stanton's whole formatting trick was about which moments are "worth savoring versus get to the point." Go through and tag each beat: *savor* (slow down, give it room, let it breathe) or *transit* (get there fast, don't linger). Over-savoring transit beats bores; rushing a savor beat throws away the payoff.

| Beat | Savor or Transit | Current treatment | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|
| (beat 1) | SAVOR / TRANSIT | (rushed / lingered) | (give room / compress) |
| (beat 2) | | | |
| (beat 3) | | | |

The savor beats are usually the ones serving the premise-sentence and the moment of change. The transit beats are connective tissue — necessary, but they should disappear under the reader. If everything is savored, nothing is.

### Step 4: Control the Reader/Viewer Heart Rate (Clamp Audit on Delivery)
Read the re-tuned draft as a continuous experience and track the felt heart rate beat by beat. This is the clamp check applied to *telling*, not plot: drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty, and the audience should never want to unclamp from the line. Where did the beach ball drop — where did the reader's attention release because the delivery went slack, over-explained, or flat?

| Beat | Heart rate you want | Heart rate it delivers | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| (open) | (held / racing / hushed) | | |
| (middle) | | | |
| (the savor moment) | | | |
| (close) | | | |

Any row where wanted ≠ delivered is an unclamp. Re-break the line, restore the withheld thing, or cut the slack. Confirm the *ending* heart rate matches the one named in Input — the piece should leave the reader exactly where you intended, not merely informed.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | Use the literal Walter Hill break — short left-justified description lines at the read-pace, so no one skims to dialogue. Withhold exposition the image will carry; the silent stretch is a feature. Make the script read at the speed the film will play. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | Vary sentence length as the rhythm instrument: stack short lines for the held beat, one long line to rush a transit. Cut the explanatory clause the next paragraph already implies. Mark 2-3 lines to savor; let the rest disappear under the reader. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | The line break *is* the medium — every break is a beat the scroller must complete. One savor line, the rest transit. Strip exposition to the bone; the reader fills the gap, and the gap is the engagement. End on the heart rate, not a summary. |
| Sales / marketing copy | Withhold the explanation that kills desire; let the prospect lean in to fill it. Break the offer line so the value lands on its own line, not buried mid-sentence. Savor the transformation beat; transit the mechanics. Telling-well outsells over-explaining-well. |
| Brand / campaign | Set one delivery signature — a rhythm or restraint the brand owns across assets — so the *how* is recognizable before the *what* is read. Cut the on-the-nose tagline the image already says. Consistency of telling is the asset. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- At least one over-explained passage was underwritten or cut, with silence/gesture/implication carrying it instead — no exposition dump survived that the next beat already implies.
- The highest-stakes passage was re-broken so its read-pace matches the intended experience-pace; the breaks are deliberate, not decorative.
- Savor and transit beats are distinguished; not everything is lingered on, and no payoff beat was rushed past.
- The delivery clamp holds — every beat pulls forward on anticipation + uncertainty, no slack row where the beach ball dropped, and the ending heart rate matches the target named in Input.
- The substance (premise-sentence, the change) survived intact: restraint sharpened the telling without sanding off what the piece is about.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Cutting exposition the piece actually needs.** Underwriting is a scalpel, not a chainsaw; strip the load-bearing line and the reader is lost, not trusting. Recovery: re-read each cut against the premise-sentence — if comprehension breaks, restore it and trim elsewhere.
- **Line breaks as decoration.** Breaking text into short lines because it "looks like a poem" without matching the rhythm to the read-pace just fragments prose. Recovery: read it aloud at the speed you want it watched; put breaks only where you want the reader to inhale.
- **Savoring everything.** If every beat is slowed and given room, the real payoff has no contrast and nothing lands. Recovery: pick the 2-3 beats serving the change; compress the rest to transit.
- **Polishing delivery on an un-dug draft.** Crafting the telling of something whose premise-sentence is forced or absent is whistling on the steps of Carnegie Hall — beautiful delivery of nothing. Recovery: stop and run the Decision Framework; uncover what it's about, then come back and tell it well.
