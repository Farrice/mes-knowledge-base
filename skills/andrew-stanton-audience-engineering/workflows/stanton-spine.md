# The Spine (Andrew Stanton)
Michael Corleone's spine is *to please his father*, and it never leaves him even after the father is dead — that single fixed wiring is why every scene he's in coheres. This workflow takes a protagonist, brand, customer, or founder who currently reads as a list of behaviors and finds the one thing they are solving in everything they do, states it as a fixed inner wiring, and pins a character one-liner over it so every later choice traces back to one source.

## Input Required
- The subject: a protagonist, a brand, a real person (founder/customer), or an ICP
- Raw material — what they do, what they've said, the choices already on record (transcript, bio, brand history, three real decisions they've made)
- The format the spine has to govern (a film, an essay series, a content account, a campaign)
- Any spine, motto, or "core value" already claimed — so we can test it rather than trust it

## Workflow

### Step 1: Find the Central Complication
Stop listing what they do. Look for the one thing they are solving in *everything* they do — the complication that shows up in unrelated decisions because it is the wiring underneath all of them. Pull three to five of their actual choices and look for the constant that survives across all of them. The constant is not the topic; it is the unresolved thing the topic keeps circling.

Don't invent it. Dig for it. WALL·E wasn't assigned "to find the beauty" — that wiring was uncovered by watching what the character could not stop doing (collecting, curating, keeping the one thing that catches light). Read the behaviors for the thing they keep returning to without being told to.

Now name the constant in the right-hand column — the one complication that appears in every row. If three rows point one way and one points another, the outlier is either the spine showing its edge or noise; keep digging until the rows agree.

### Step 2: State the Spine as Fixed Wiring
A spine is "a way they're wired up no matter what's going on in life — the core of you." Write it as a fixed infinitive, short and unconditional: *to please his father*, *to find the beauty*. Not a goal they could achieve and retire (a goal ends; a spine never does). Not a value they espouse (values are stated; spines are obeyed even when unstated). The test of the phrasing: it should still be true on their worst day and their best one, in a domain that has nothing to do with their work.

| Candidate spine (infinitive) | Survives their worst day? | Still true off-domain? | A goal they could finish? (kill it) |
|---|---|---|---|
| to ______ | | | |
| to ______ | | | |
| to ______ | | | |

Keep the one that survives both tests and is *not* a finishable goal. If the strongest candidate is a goal, push one level down: behind *to win the championship* sits *to never be overlooked again* — that's the spine.

### Step 3: Write the Character One-Liner
Pin a one-liner over the spine — the line that names the conflict the spine is permanently in. WALL·E's is *"irrational love defeats life's programming."* It is not the spine restated; it is the spine *versus its opposition*, compressed. The form is a collision: [the spine's force] defeats / outruns / refuses [the thing that should win]. The opposite is what creates the drama — give the spine something built to beat it, and the one-liner writes itself.

```
ONE-LINER PATTERN: [the spine's irrational force] [verb of victory] [the rational thing that should have won]
  examples:  "irrational love defeats life's programming"
             "the need to be seen defeats the safety of silence"
Draft: "________ ________ ________"
Opposition named: __________ (what is built to beat the spine — the source of the drama)
```

### Step 4: Test — Does It Govern the Morning?
The real test of a spine is whether it governs *how they get up in the morning* — how they "open a book," answer an email, choose between two ordinary options. Run the spine against three mundane decisions that have nothing to do with their stated mission. If the spine predicts the choice, it's alive. If you have to reach, it's a label you bolted on, not the wiring you dug out.

Then split want from need, because a true spine usually produces a *want* that masks the *need*. Marlin *wants* control (never lose Nemo); he *needs* to let fear stop denying him fatherhood. The spine drives the want; the arc is the need finally overruling it. Name both — the want the spine produces on the surface, and the need it is actually protecting underneath.

```

NEED (underneath, the arc): __________
The gap between them is the story.
```

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / film / video | The spine is the actor's instruction — it governs blocking, line readings, the small choices in scenes with no plot function. The one-liner goes on the title card in your head, not in the dialogue; a character who states their own spine is dead on arrival. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The narrator (you, or your subject) carries one spine across the whole series, not a new lesson per post. Readers bond to the wiring, not the topic. The want/need gap is the recurring tension that keeps them subscribed. |
| Short-form social (LinkedIn / X / Reels) | One spine per account, stated never. Every post is the same wiring meeting a different opposition. The one-liner is your positioning, but it lives as a felt pattern across the feed — readers should be able to predict your take before reading it and still want to. |
| Sales / marketing copy | Give the *customer* the spine, not the brand. The reader is the protagonist; the product is the thing that lets their spine finally beat its opposition. Copy that hits the want and ignores the need converts shallow; name the need underneath and it lands. |
| Brand / campaign | The brand's spine is the fixed wiring behind every product and post — the thing it is solving in everything it does. The one-liner is the campaign's real conflict, not its tagline. Each asset is the spine meeting a fresh opposition; an asset that contradicts the spine for applause gets cut. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- The spine is a **fixed infinitive that never ends**, not a goal that could be completed or a value the subject states aloud — if a goal slipped in, push one level deeper.
- The spine was **uncovered from real choices on record**, not assigned to fit the brief. If you reached for evidence or invented behaviors, you forced it — go back to the dig.
- The one-liner names a genuine **opposition** (the spine versus what should beat it). A one-liner with nothing built to defeat it has no drama.
- The spine **predicted at least 2 of 3 mundane, off-domain decisions** — otherwise it's a label, not wiring; relabel and re-test.
- **Want and need are distinct**, and the gap between them is named — if they collapse into one thing, the arc has nowhere to go.
- The machinery is **invisible in any delivered output**: the subject never says their own spine, the page never labels it "spine" or "one-liner."

## Common Pitfalls
- **Naming a goal instead of a spine**: *to win* ends the moment they win; the wiring underneath does not. Recovery: ask what's still true after the goal is achieved, and after it's lost forever — that's the spine.
- **Forcing the spine to fit the brand you wish existed**: assigning *to empower people* because it sounds good, with no choice on record that proves it. Recovery: throw out the aspirational phrase and re-run Step 1 on real decisions only; a forced spine is a false one.
- **A one-liner with no opposition**: *"love is good"* states a value and dies. The line needs a force built to beat the spine, or there's no drama to track. Recovery: name the rational thing that *should* win, then let the spine defeat it.
- **Collapsing want into need**: writing the surface want as if it were the whole character leaves the arc flat. Recovery: name the want the spine produces, then ask what it's protecting against — the answer is the need, and the distance between them is the story.
