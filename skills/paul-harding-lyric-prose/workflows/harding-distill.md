# Systole & Diastole — Clutter, Then Distill to Dense, Lucid Elegance (Paul Harding)

Harding writes "easily a thousand pages" for a 150-page book. He lets everything he is interested in onto the page — paintings, music, false starts, cul-de-sacs, the link he can only say "I have no idea how you belong, but I think you do" to — because "it's easier to have too much and pull back." Then he distills, builds back, distills again: the systole and diastole of the whole manuscript, the heart's contraction and expansion run across the draft. *Tinkers* is two hundred pages he tried to make "800 pages deep, because it's not 800 pages wide" — maximalism taken "as far as you can go," but "I hate clutter," carved until the prose is "so utterly lucid the reader forgets they're reading prose on the page" and yet "as rich as possible." This workflow runs the diastole on a draft that is already over-full: it does not add, it carves. The terror it has to dissolve first is that revision is punishment — proof you failed the first time. It is the opposite. The thousand pages were the *instrument*. "The twenty pages I wrote to get that one paragraph were not wasted pages." Cutting is not loss. Cutting is the only way the depth was ever going to surface.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this pass is dangerous *because* the urge under a cluttered draft is to add more, not carve):
- **Pattern 13 — Clutter Then Distill (Systole and Diastole)** — the core cycle: overwrite first, pour in everything that pulls at you even when you can't see how it belongs; then carve back to elegance until "there it is," nothing ornamental, all depths of field working. The pages written to reach one perfect paragraph "were not wasted pages."
- **Pattern 9 — Depth, Not Width (Density of Lived Experience)** — the target the carve aims at: compress meaning *down*, not *out*; pack a short passage with felt experience while keeping it lucid; "800 pages deep because it's not 800 pages wide"; no sentence "a mountain you have to climb."
- **Hidden Knowledge — Get It Wrong in a Particular Way** — the reframe that kills the punishment story: you can't reason your way to the great sentence; the cluttered draft is the *specific* wrong version that lets you see what the right one wants to be. Revision is insurance, not failure — the wrong-on-the-page was the only available path to the right.
- **Paul Harding Would Never... AN-4 (Ornamental "Racoo Baloney" / Showing Off)** — the thing the carve is hunting: prose written "in service of the writer" to win "the applause of other writers," the "mere virtuoso" with every word in the dictionary and nothing to say — *and clutter that never got distilled.* This is the anti-pattern this workflow exists to eliminate.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before touching the draft — but this workflow's gate is **Q5 specifically** (*Would precision serve this better than ornament? — if you're slathering adjectives to make it "beautiful," describe it more exactly and let it move instead*), plus the AN-4 reframe. Confirm three things. (1) **The draft is genuinely over-full, not under-built** — there is real material here to carve, density buried under clutter, *not* a thin draft that needs the *other* engine (`/harding-improv-draft` to generate, `/harding-then-what` to extend a stalled chain). If the draft is thin, do not run the diastole — you'll carve a small thing into nothing. The systole (overwrite) comes before this; this is the diastole. (2) **You can name what the draft is *for* — the depth it's trying to reach** — what felt experience must survive the carve. If you can't, you'll cut by length and lose the spine. (3) **You accept revision as insurance, not punishment** (Hidden Knowledge) — the clutter was the instrument that got you to the buried paragraph; cutting it now is the *point*, not penance. If you are revising because you feel you failed, stop and reread the reframe below — that story will make you protect the wrong sentences (the ones you suffered over) and cut the live ones.

> **The insurance reframe (read before carving):** the draft you are about to cut is not evidence you got it wrong the first time. It is the systole — the over-full contraction that *had* to happen so the diastole could find the dense, lucid thing inside it. "The twenty pages I wrote to get that one paragraph were not wasted pages." You did not waste the clutter; you *used* it. The carve is not undoing the draft — it is the second half of the same heartbeat. Hold the cut words gently and gratefully; many of them did their job (got you here) and are now free to go.

## Input Required

- **The draft as it stands (required)** — the actual overworked / cluttered / sprawling text, whole, not a summary. We are carving specific sentences, so we need the real ones. If it's a long piece, the section that's bloated.
- **The depth it's reaching for (required)** — what is this draft *for*? Not its topic — the felt experience it must deliver, the thing that has to survive the carve. ("A short scene that carries a whole marriage." "A post that makes the reader recognize their own avoidance.") This is the spine; everything that doesn't serve it is a candidate for the cut. Without it you'll carve by word-count and lose the heart.
- **The form & a rough density target** — Substack section / LinkedIn post / essay / scene / brand passage / email, and roughly how tight it needs to land. Drives how aggressive the diastole is. (Harding: depth, not width — short forms that need to *feel* large.)
- **Voice / grain (if not Harding's own)** — whose consciousness is this? For ghostwriting, the carve preserves the *client's* grain and repertoire — never flattens it into a generic "clean" voice, never pastiches a master onto them (Hidden Knowledge — Brain Is a Fingerprint).
- **Honesty spine (required for commercial/nonfiction)** — the carve must never cut a *true, load-bearing* particular to make the prose prettier. Density is achieved by removing ornament and received language, never by removing the real detail the recognition depends on. Flag which details are real-and-load-bearing so they're protected from the knife.
- **Protected lines (optional but recommended)** — the lines you already know are alive, the buried paragraphs the clutter was written to reach. Name them so the carve builds *around* them instead of over them.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Find the buried paragraph; name the spine (Pattern 9, Pattern 13)

Before you cut a single word, read the whole draft once, slowly, and find the **live core** — the paragraph (or two sentences, or single image) the rest of the draft was secretly written to reach. Harding's thousand pages exist to surface *one* perfect paragraph; in any over-full draft there is a buried density the clutter is circling. Mark it. This is the depth the carve protects and serves; everything else is now provisional.

Then name the **spine** explicitly — the felt experience the piece must deliver (from Input). The spine is the test every other sentence faces: *does this serve the buried core and the felt experience, or is it the writer circling, performing, or hedging?*

```
THE BURIED CORE (the live paragraph/image the draft was written to reach): __________
THE SPINE (the felt experience that must survive the carve): __________
DEPTH TARGET (what "800 deep in 200 wide" means for this specific piece): __________
```

> **Why this comes first:** if you carve before naming the core, you cut by *length* — you trim the long sentences and keep the short ornamental ones, and you can lose the one paragraph that mattered. The core is the keel. You build the lean boat around it.

### Step 2 — The clutter pass: diagnose, don't yet cut (AN-4, AN-2)

Now walk the draft and *tag* clutter without removing it yet — diagnosis before surgery, so you can see the shape of what's bloated before you change it. Every sentence gets a tag. Most clutter falls into the table's categories; tagging by category tells you *why* it goes, which prevents the panic-cut that takes a live line with it.

| Clutter type | What it is | Tell | The genius behind the cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ornamental "racoo baloney"** | A sentence whose primary job is to be *admired* — stacked adjectives, virtuoso flourish, beauty *applied to* the thing | "Look what I can do." You'd quote it to impress another writer, not because it's true | AN-4 — "in service of the writer," "the applause of other writers." Cut to depth, not decoration |
| **Received / off-the-rack language** | Pre-fabricated phrases, the language "you use to get through your day," clichés | You could have written it without looking at the actual thing | AN-2 — "will not work in good writing." Cut it; the real thing goes back in (Step 4) |
| **Slathered beauty** | Adjectives piled to make a thing "feel important," pathetic-fallacy decoration | Three adjectives where precision wanted one exact noun + a moving verb | Pattern 8 — precision releases wonder; ornament suffocates it |
| **Circling / restatement** | The same beat said three ways because you weren't sure which landed | You wrote variations and kept all of them | Pattern 13 — you overwrote (correct, systole); now keep the one that's truest, cut the other two |
| **The clanking gear** | "She looked out and thought…", announced transitions between world and mind | A seam you can hear between description and interiority | AN-5 — dissolve into co-extensivity, often by *cutting* the announcement |
| **The stray eighth note** | A word the sentence doesn't need — an extra beat the cadence trips on | Read aloud, the rhythm stumbles there | Pattern 11 — Harding revises "to remove one extra eighth note" |
| **The smuggled lesson** | A sentence telling the reader how to feel or what to conclude | "And that's why…", "reminding us all that…" | AN-3 — "the most violent thing"; the carve deletes it, recognition does the work |

**Do not cut yet.** Tag, then step back and look at the proportions: how much of the draft is core (Step 1), how much is clutter, how much is connective tissue carrying the chain. The shape of the clutter is the map for the diastole.

> **The AN-4 honesty check, run here:** before you tag a line "ornamental," confirm it isn't actually a *real, load-bearing particular* that just happens to be vivid. Harding's prose is dense with precise detail — density is not the enemy; *ornament* is. The test: does this sentence make a *true thing land harder* (keep — that's depth), or does it make *the writer look good* (cut — that's AN-4)? Never carve a real detail the recognition depends on (honesty spine). Cut the slather around it instead.

### Step 3 — Distill: carve back to "there it is" (Pattern 13, the diastole)

Now the contraction. Carve in passes, not one pass — the systole/diastole is *iterative*: distill, look at what's left, distill again. Each pass has one job; running them separately is what keeps you from over-cutting in a panic.

**Pass A — Remove the pure ornament (AN-4).** Delete every "racoo baloney" line, every slathered-adjective stack, every flourish written to be admired. These leave no hole; the draft only gets clearer. This is the easiest cut and the biggest density gain.

**Pass B — Collapse the circling (Pattern 13).** Where you said one beat three ways, keep the single truest version — usually the most *literal*, least decorated one — and cut the variations. You overwrote on purpose (systole); now you choose. If you can't tell which is truest, read all three aloud; the one with no stray eighth note and no received phrase is the keeper.

**Pass C — Dissolve the gears and the lesson (AN-5, AN-3).** Cut announced transitions ("she looked out and thought") so perception carries interiority (co-extensivity). Cut every smuggled lesson and takeaway; let the precise thing + the felt seeing stand, and let recognition do what explanation was trying to do. The draft should now end by *opening*, not closing.

**Pass D — Tune the cadence (Pattern 11).** Read the carved draft *aloud, cold*. Remove the stray eighth notes — the words the sentence doesn't need, the beats the rhythm trips on. Keep it angular and funky where the voice wants it, fluent where momentum wants it; kill sing-song and cutesy internal rhyme. This is the final-polish layer.

After each pass, apply the **"there it is" test**: pull back and look. Is the buried core (Step 1) now *exposed*, all depths of field working, nothing ornamental in front of it? If a passage still has clutter, carve again. If a passage has gone *thin* — if you cut a real load-bearing particular and lost density — that's the over-carve failure; restore the particular (Step 4 handles deliberate build-back). Harding distills *and builds back* — the diastole isn't only subtraction; sometimes the carve reveals a place that needs one more precise true detail to reach depth.

> **Maximum density with maximum readability — the dual gate.** After carving, every surviving sentence must pass *both*: **dense** (it carries felt experience, it earns its space, nothing in it is decoration) *and* **lucid** (a first-time reader parses it on one pass; it is "not a mountain you have to climb"). A sentence that is dense but opaque failed half the test — Harding's maximalism is "as far as you can go" *and* "so utterly lucid the reader forgets they're reading prose." If the carve made a line cryptic, you compressed *out* legibility instead of *down* into depth. Re-open it just enough to read clean.

### Step 4 — Build back where the carve revealed a hole (Pattern 13, the systole returning)

The diastole sometimes exposes a place that was *propped up by clutter* — when you cut the ornament, you find the underlying thing was never actually rendered, only gestured at. This is not a carving failure; it's the cycle working. Where a carved passage now reads thin, build back — but build back with *precision*, not the ornament you just removed:

- Add the *real, true* particular the spine needs (honesty spine — a real detail, never an invented vivid one).
- Render it with botanist's precision *plus motion* (Pattern 8) — the exact thing, and how it changes — not with adjectives.
- Layer the felt experience through it (the two things, Pattern 6) if the spine is emotional, kept co-extensive so no gear clanks.

Then carve *that* — the build-back is a mini-systole, and it gets its own small diastole. Systole, diastole, systole, diastole, until "it's all there" and nothing more wants to move.

### Step 5 — The cut-but-not-wasted ledger (the insurance reframe, made concrete)

Revision is insurance, not punishment — and the ledger is where you *prove* that to yourself, so the next draft starts brave instead of cramped. For the significant cuts, log not just *what* you cut but *what it bought you* — what reaching the core required passing through. This does two things: it dissolves the "I wasted those words" story (you didn't — they got you here), and it preserves any genuinely live thread you cut that belongs in a *different* piece ("I have no idea how you belong, but I think you do" — file it, don't grieve it).

```
CUT-BUT-NOT-WASTED LEDGER:
  • Cut: "[the line/passage]"  →  What it bought: [got me to the buried core / told me the beat didn't land / revealed the thin spot] — not wasted.
  • Cut: "[the line]"  →  Belongs elsewhere: [filed as a seed for ____] — live, just not here.
```

Then state the heartbeat metric: **before → after**. Word count down, *density up*. The piece is shorter and carries *more*. That is the systole/diastole working: depth, not width.

### Step 6 — Final "there it is" read (Pattern 9 + AN-4 gate)

Read the distilled piece once more, whole, aloud, as a first-time reader. The questions:

- **Is it all there?** Does the buried core now stand exposed, with every surviving sentence working, all depths of field active? (Harding: pull back until "there it is.")
- **Is anything still ornamental?** One last AN-4 sweep — is there a single sentence whose job is to be admired rather than to be true? Cut it now; it's the last clutter.
- **Dense *and* lucid?** Every sentence carries weight *and* reads clean on first pass. Neither soden-and-belabored nor cryptic.
- **Does it open, not close?** Recognition, not lesson — the reader is dilated, wanting to return, not handed a takeaway.
- **Did the grain survive?** (Ghostwriting especially.) It reads denser and cleaner but unmistakably as *this voice*, not flattened into generic "clean prose."

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social media** (LinkedIn / X / Substack note) | The native test of "depth, not width" — a short post that feels substantive and re-readable. Carve hardest here: every social draft is full of received "thought-leader" phrasing and a smuggled lesson at the close. Pass A (ornament) and Pass C (kill the takeaway) do the most work. The buried core is usually one precisely-seen real moment; expose it and let the post *dilate* the reader. A 90-word post that lands like 900 is the win — dense, lucid, opening a door, not stacking points. |
| **Marketing / brand** (origin, manifesto, about) | Carve the mission-statement slather and the "we knew we were building something special" lesson. The buried core is a *true* concrete particular from the actual founding; protect it (honesty spine) and cut the abstraction around it. Brand character emerges from what the carved-clean world *notices*, not from adjectives — so Pass A and the build-back (Step 4, a real particular rendered precisely) matter most. Density makes a brand line feel inevitable rather than written. |
| **Copywriting** (ad / landing / email) | The carve removes the ornamental flourish and the soft Latinate float, but **never** the real proof point or the load-bearing true detail — recognition (and conversion) depends on those. Distill toward the literal product detail + the felt experience of it (the two things), cut the slather, keep the spine. Honesty spine is load-bearing: density here is *removing decoration*, never *removing substantiated claims*. Hand proof/offer architecture to copy-engine / luke-iha; this pass tightens the prose, not the funnel. Pairs with `compression-sprint` for the hard length squeeze. |
| **Ghostwriting** | Carve to *their* density, not a generic clean voice. Preserve the client's grain and repertoire — their colors, verbs, depth of field — while cutting the clutter; the danger is flattening a real voice into "tidy" prose that's clearer but *no longer theirs*. The buried core is *their* live moment; protect it. Read aloud in their cadence, not Harding's. The output must read as the client, denser. |
| **Content / essay** | The full systole/diastole habitat. Overwrite is assumed done (or run `/harding-improv-draft` first); this is the diastole. Carve the circling and the ornament, dissolve the clanking gears, kill the essayist's reflex to *conclude*. Build back any thin spot with one precise true detail. The goal is the re-readable short piece — substantive, lucid, opening a door — that the reader returns to "on their birthday" and goes deeper into each time. |

### Worked example A — a cluttered paragraph, distilled in passes (Substack)

**Spine:** the dread of a familiar phone call you already know how it ends. · **Buried core:** the moment before answering, when you know. · **Form:** Substack section.

> **THE DRAFT (cluttered, over-full — the systole):**
> The phone, that ancient and ceaseless harbinger of both joy and sorrow, rang out shrilly into the heavy, oppressive silence of the late afternoon, and I knew — I simply knew, with that bone-deep, ineffable certainty that we all come to recognize over the long course of our lives — exactly who it was and precisely what they were going to say, because some calls announce themselves before you ever lift the receiver, reminding us that we are never truly ready for the conversations we have rehearsed a thousand times. The light was beautiful. It was a gorgeous, melancholy, golden, fading kind of light, the sad and lovely light of an afternoon slipping away. I looked at the phone and I thought about how tired I was.

**Clutter pass diagnosis:** *Ornamental (AN-4):* "ancient and ceaseless harbinger of both joy and sorrow." *Received (AN-2):* "bone-deep, ineffable certainty," "we are never truly ready." *Slathered (Pattern 8):* "gorgeous, melancholy, golden, fading… sad and lovely light." *Smuggled lesson (AN-3):* "reminding us that we are never truly ready for the conversations we have rehearsed a thousand times." *Clanking gear (AN-5):* "I looked at the phone and I thought about how tired I was."

> **Pass A (cut ornament):** drop "ancient and ceaseless harbinger…" — the phone is a phone.
> **Pass B (collapse circling):** the light is described four adjectives' worth; keep one *precise, moving* rendering, cut the rest.
> **Pass C (dissolve gear + kill lesson):** delete "reminding us…"; delete "I looked at the phone and I thought about how tired I was" — let the seeing carry the tiredness.
> **Pass D (tune cadence):** read aloud, remove the stray beats.

> **THE DISTILLED PIECE (the diastole — "there it is"):**
> The phone rang into the late-afternoon quiet and I knew who it was before I crossed the room, the way you know a call that has already happened a dozen times and is only happening again. The light had gone to that low gold that doesn't hold; it was already leaving the wall where it lay. I let it ring once more. I was very tired, and the receiver was cold, and I picked it up.

*Why it runs the engine:* 118 words → 71. The ornamental harbinger is gone; the four-adjective light became one precise *moving* rendering ("doesn't hold… already leaving the wall"); the smuggled lesson is deleted and the dread is now *refracted* through the literal cold receiver and the one-more-ring (the two things), never stated; the clanking "I thought about how tired I was" became the felt, co-extensive "I was very tired, and the receiver was cold." It opens a door — we never hear the call — instead of concluding. Denser, more lucid, the buried core (the moment before answering, when you know) now exposed.

### Worked example B — brand passage, over-built then carved (honesty spine load-bearing)

**Spine:** a small shop that ships flawed and honest. · **Buried core:** the wrong box, taped twice, shipped anyway. · **Honesty spine:** the shop, the bulb, the wrong box, the first ship date are real. · **Form:** brand origin, tighten to ~55 words.

> **THE DRAFT (over-built — the systole):**
> In the humble, hardworking heart of our very first workshop, beneath the warm and honest glow of a single hardworking bulb that seemed to symbolize our scrappy determination, we packed our very first unit into a box — and here's the beautiful thing, the thing that taught us everything — it was the wrong box, a box we had to tape not once but twice, and in that imperfect moment we understood that perfection was never the point, that authenticity beats polish every single time, and we shipped it with pride.

**Clutter pass diagnosis:** *Ornamental (AN-4):* "the humble, hardworking heart," "warm and honest glow… that seemed to symbolize our scrappy determination." *Received (AN-2):* "here's the beautiful thing," "authenticity beats polish every single time." *Smuggled lesson (AN-3):* "we understood that perfection was never the point… authenticity beats polish… shipped it with pride." The real load-bearing particular (the wrong box, taped twice) is *buried* under all of it — protect it.

> **THE DISTILLED PIECE:**
> One bulb over the bench. The first unit went into the wrong box — the only one we had — and we taped it twice because the first tape didn't hold. Then we shipped it. The wrong box, taped twice, out the door before the light changed.

*Why it runs the engine:* 92 words → 48. Every ornament and every lesson is cut; the *real* particular (wrong box, taped twice, the single bulb, shipped) is protected and now *carries* the brand's character — scrappy, honest, ships anyway — through what the world notices, never asserted. "Authenticity beats polish" (received, smuggled lesson) is gone; the reader recognizes the value without being told it. Honesty spine intact: nothing true was cut, only the slather around it.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Carving by length instead of by spine.** Trimming the long sentences and keeping the short ornamental ones because "shorter is tighter" — and accidentally cutting the buried core while a virtuoso flourish survives. **Recovery:** Step 1 first, every time. Find and name the live core and the spine *before* cutting; build the lean piece *around* the keel. Length is an output of the carve, never its target — "depth, not width" means you can keep a long sentence that earns its space and cut a short one that doesn't.
- **Revising as punishment (protecting the wrong sentences).** Treating revision as proof you failed, so you cling hardest to the lines you *suffered over* (the ornamental ones you're proud of) and cut the easy live ones. The suffering becomes the selection criterion — exactly backwards. **Recovery:** reread the insurance reframe in Pre-Flight. The clutter was the *instrument* — "the twenty pages to get that paragraph were not wasted." Cut by *what serves the spine*, not by what cost you. Run the cut-but-not-wasted ledger (Step 5) to feel, concretely, that the cuts bought something. Most pride-of-craft lines are AN-4.
- **Over-carving into thinness (compressing out instead of down).** Cutting so hard the prose goes bare and cryptic — you removed legibility and load-bearing particulars, not ornament, and the piece is now short and *empty* instead of short and *dense*. **Recovery:** apply the dual gate after every pass — dense *and* lucid. Where a passage went thin, you likely cut a real particular that the clutter was propping up; Step 4 — build it back with *precision* (the exact true thing, in motion), not the ornament you removed. Systole returns; carve *that* in turn. Density is meaning packed *down*, never the page stripped *bare*.
- **Adding instead of carving (the wrong heartbeat).** Faced with a cluttered draft, the reflex is often to *fix it by writing more* — another clarifying sentence, another transition, another adjective to "make it land." This is systole when the draft needs diastole; it makes the clutter worse. **Recovery:** confirm in Pre-Flight that the draft is *over-full, not under-built*. If over-full, the move is the carve — Steps 2–3, remove until "there it is."
