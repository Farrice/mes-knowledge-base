# The Perception Engine — Slow, Pre-Linguistic Attention to Luminous Description (Paul Harding)

Harding's grandfather, dying, looked up and asked where the cracks in the ceiling came from. Most writers would have written "cracks in the ceiling" and moved on to what it *meant*. Harding slowed down, stayed inside the seeing, and asked the only question that matters: *then what would he actually see?* — and the ceiling caved into wires and plumbing, then sky, then stars, then the black. The luminous passage was never invented; it was *uncovered* by refusing to rush past the thing toward its significance. That is this workflow. You take any subject — a swan boat on the Boston Common, a chipped mug, the light on a wall at 4pm — sift down beneath the first habituated label into raw sensation, strip every off-the-rack phrase, render the literal thing exactly (and *moving*), then give the felt experience of it. The world, rendered precisely, already means itself. Your job is reverent attention and accurate transcription, not the manufacture of meaning.

This is **not** the hot-take engine and it is **not** the ornament engine. It is the patient one. The single most common failure is rushing — toward the symbol, toward the takeaway, toward the "beautiful" adjective. Slow down. That is the headstone line.

## Input Required

- **The subject** — the one object, place, or moment to render. Specific, not categorical: *the swan boat I rode on the Common when I was seven*, not "boats"; *the coffee cup on my desk right now*, not "a mug." If it's general, narrow it to a single instance you can actually perceive or remember.
- **Point of contact** — how you can get into real sensory contact with it: it's in front of you now / a vivid memory / an observed scene you can re-enter. (This is the honesty-spine anchor; the description must come from contact, not invention.)
- **The whose-eyes** (optional but powerful) — if a character/persona/brand is perceiving this, name them. *What* gets noticed and *how* it's named will refract through them (description-becomes-character). If none, it's the writer's own clear attention.
- **Format / where it lands** — Substack/essay line, social post, marketing/brand prose, copy with a real product detail, ghostwritten passage. Drives length and how much the felt-layer can carry.
- **Felt charge** (optional) — the experience the subject holds for the perceiver (dread, tenderness, the creek that "means death," nostalgia). Layered in Step 4 *through* the thing, never stated. If you don't know it yet, leave it blank — the literal chain may surface it.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Get into contact, then refuse the first label (Pattern 2, AN-2 gate)

Do not write a sentence yet. Put yourself in front of the subject — physically, or by re-entering the memory until it's vivid. Then catch the first word your mind hands you for it, and *distrust it*. The first label is almost always habituated — "the swan boat is pretty," "the light is golden," "the cup is old." Those are the phrases you use to get through your day; they "will not work in good writing." Write the first labels down expressly so you can see them and reject them.

```
SUBJECT: __________
FIRST (HABITUATED) LABELS — to be rejected: __________ , __________ , __________
```

These are now banned for the rest of the workflow. You are going to go *underneath* them.

> The reflex to defeat here is "yeah, I got it, I know that already." You don't. You've stopped looking because you recognized the category. Sift below recognition.

### Step 2 — Observe sense by sense, pre-linguistically (Pattern 2 — the heart of the engine)

Now *look*, as if you had never seen this thing and have no word for it. Go sense by sense and record only what is *literally, physically there* — not what it's like, not what it means, not how it makes you feel. Stay longer than is comfortable; the line that lands usually comes from the observation you'd have skipped on autopilot.

| Sense | The literal question (answer with no metaphor, no meaning yet) | What is actually there |
|---|---|---|
| **Sight** | What exact shapes, colors, edges, light? What is the depth of field — what's sharp, what's blurred? | __________ |
| **Sound** | What is actually audible — pitch, rhythm, the small ones under the obvious one? | __________ |
| **Touch / temperature** | Texture, weight, heat, the air on skin, what the body registers? | __________ |
| **Smell / taste** | What's literally in the air or the mouth, however faint? | __________ |
| **Motion / change over time** | What is *moving* or *changing* — the light shifting, a surface trembling, the slow drift? (This row is non-negotiable — see Step 3.) | __________ |

Two discipline rules while filling this table:
- **No similes or meaning yet.** "Like a..." or "which reminds me of..." or "symbolizing..." are all forbidden in this step. You are transcribing sensation, not interpreting it.
- **Hunt the overlooked.** Force at least one observation per row that you would *not* have noticed if you'd written from memory of the category. The fresh detail is the proof you actually slowed down.

### Step 3 — Render with botanist's precision, then add the calculus (Pattern 8, AN-4 gate)

Take the rawest, truest observations from Step 2 and write them *exactly* — not beautifully, exactly. Name the literal thing with a botanist's precision. The fear is that this will sound clinical, surgical. The opposite happens: precision *releases* the wonder that ornament can only fake. "Have faith that the world means itself."

Then do the move that turns precise into luminous: **add the calculus** — describe how the thing *changes over time*. Static precision is a photograph; precision-in-motion is alive. The light doesn't just fall on the wall — it climbs, reddens, lengthens, withdraws. The swan boat's wake doesn't just exist — it widens, smooths, vanishes into the pond's skin.

| Move | What you do | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Precise, not pretty** | Replace every adjective doing "beauty duty" with a more *exact* noun, verb, or measured observation. | If you cut the word, do you lose information or only decoration? Decoration = cut it. |
| **Add the calculus (motion)** | Take the most precise image and describe its change over even a few seconds. Mine the **verbs** — that's where the life is. | Is anything moving/changing? If the passage is frozen, it's a photo, not perception. |
| **Counterpoint, sparingly** (optional, from Pattern 7) | If a precise image has gone habitual, borrow the verbs of the thing's *opposite category* — vapor in stone's verbs, silence in noise's — to snap it awake while staying literal. | Does the reader feel "I don't quite know what it means, but..." while the line is still pure description? |

> **The AN-4 guard:** if a sentence's primary job is to be *admired* — if it's "look what I can do" — cut it to depth instead of decoration. Harding takes maximalism "as far as you can go, but I hate clutter." The maximalism is in the *depth* of attention, never in the count of adjectives. No pathetic-fallacy slather ("the willow wept its silver tears"), no "ancient, gnarled, sorrowful" adjective-stacking.

### Step 4 — Layer the felt experience THROUGH the thing (Pattern 6 — The Two Things)

You now have the literal thing, rendered precisely and moving. If the subject carries a charge for the perceiver, give the reader **both things**: the world (neutrally, accurately true) *and* the perceiver's experience of it. The creek is objectively a fine creek; the character feels it "means death" — and now we know the character, with nothing explained. The gap between the two is a whole narrative dimension.

Rules for the felt layer:
- **The literal thing comes first and stays true.** The reader trusts the feeling *because* they were shown the real thing before being asked to feel anything.
- **Refract, do not state.** Never write the emotion's name ("it was so nostalgic," "I felt grief"). Let the feeling live in *what gets noticed and how it's named* — the depth of field, the verbs, the colors the perceiver registers. (If a character is perceiving, this is where their private repertoire shows — their light, their kind of verb.)
- **Erase the seam (co-extensivity).** Do not announce the shift from outside to inside ("she looked at the boat and remembered..."). Let the perception *be* the interiority. No clanking gears.

> If you find yourself writing a sentence whose only job is to tell the reader how to feel, you owe them the precise thing instead, with the feeling refracted through it. Stating the feeling is, in Harding's terms, "the most violent thing you could do to the reader."

### Step 5 — Tune by ear, then distill; open the door, don't close it (Patterns 11 + 10/13)

Read the whole passage **aloud, cold.** Two passes:

1. **Cadence pass (Pattern 11).** Feel the beats. Harding revised to remove "one extra eighth note." Match the rhythm to the mood — monosyllabic and angular for stop-and-lurch weight, fluent and polysyllabic to move the reader fast. Kill sing-song, kill cutesy internal rhyme. Keep it "angular, a little choppy, but funky." If a line doesn't *play* aloud, re-tune it.
2. **Distill pass (Pattern 13 + the ending).** Carve back any clutter until "there it is" — every word apt, all depths of field working, the prose "so lucid the reader forgets they're reading prose on a page." Then check the *ending*: it must **open a door, not close one**. No takeaway, no "and that's when I realized," no moral. Harding persuades by *recognition* — "that's absolutely true; I've always known it and never seen anybody put it into words" — never by lesson. End on the precise thing or the felt charge, and let the reader's recognition do the rest.

> **Final gut check (the calibration test):** would Paul Harding recognize this as a mind paying real attention — or as someone filling lyric-prose slots? If you're unsure, it's the second. Slow down and rewrite one more time.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social (LinkedIn / X / Substack)** | Open on **one precisely-seen real moment**, never an abstract claim or a "here's what I learned" lesson. Run Steps 1–3 hard, keep the felt layer (Step 4) light and the close *open* (no takeaway). Depth, not width — a short post that feels substantive and re-readable. At this length the description IS the post; one luminous image beats five competent ones. |
| **Marketing / brand prose** | The brand's "character" emerges from what its world *notices* and how it names things (description-becomes-character), not an adjectives-on-the-about-page list. Render the **real** product/experience with Step 3 precision *and motion* so it turns luminous — never slather. Honesty spine is load-bearing: the particular must be true. |
| **Copywriting (ad / landing / VSL)** | Give the reader the **literal product detail (true)** via Steps 2–3, then the **felt experience of it** via Step 4 — let them infer the benefit through recognition, which they trust more than a stated claim. Counterpoint (Step 3 optional) makes a familiar product feel newly seen. **The detail must be real** — route any unsubstantiated claim back to copy-engine / luke-iha; this workflow renders, it does not invent proof. |
| **Ghostwriting** | Surrender to the **client's** grain, not Harding's. Find *their* natural perceptual register — what they'd notice, their kind of verb — and never pastiche a master onto them. Erase the seam between their world and their worldview (Step 4 co-extensivity). The passage must read as *theirs*. |
| **Content / essay (long-form)** | Full engine. Steps 1–5 in order; let the literal chain run (`stanton`/Pattern 3 territory if it wants to extend). Overwrite then distill (Step 5). End by opening a door so the reader returns to the piece rather than filing it. This is the native habitat — the most room for depth. |

### Worked example A — Substack / essay (the swan boat on the Boston Common; the transcript's own example, re-seen from pre-linguistic observation)

**Subject:** the swan boat I rode on the Common pond as a boy. · **Whose eyes:** the writer, remembering. · **Format:** essay opening. · **Contact:** vivid childhood memory. · **Rejected labels:** *the pretty swan boats*, *a peaceful pond*, *a magical summer day*.

> *Pre-linguistic observation (not delivered):* Sight — flat pond skin, a green so dark it read black where the willows hung; the swan's neck a painted plaster S, the seam where the two halves of the mold met running down its breast. Sound — the slap of the paddle behind us, a metronome; a child two seats up scuffing the bench. Touch — the bench warm, then cool where shade crossed it. Motion — the wake spreading, then smoothing, then gone into the skin again. *The detail I'd have skipped:* the mold-seam down the plaster swan's breast.

**The passage:**
> The pond held the willows upside down on its skin and didn't let them move. We slid out over them. Behind us the foot-paddle slapped its slow metronome and the wake opened in two long arms, widened, thinned, and was taken back into the water without a mark, again, and again, so that the pond kept closing over where we had just been. The swan up front was plaster, white, its neck a painted S, and down its breast ran the seam where the two halves of the mold had been pressed together and would never quite come apart. The shade of a willow crossed the bench and the wood went from warm to cool under my legs and then warm again on the other side, and I understood, the way a boy understands a thing in his skin before he has the words to lose it, that the day was a thing that closed over you and took you back without a mark.

*Why it works (Harding's standard):* The plaster swan is rendered with botanist's precision — not "magical," but the *mold-seam that would never quite come apart*, the one detail slowing past habituated seeing (Pattern 2, 8). The calculus is everywhere in the verbs: the wake *opens, widens, thins, is taken back*; the shade *crosses*; the wood goes *warm to cool to warm* (Pattern 8). The felt charge — time closing over you, the foreknowledge of loss — is refracted entirely *through* the literal wake "taken back without a mark," never named (Pattern 6, AN-3). No seam between world and mind (Pattern 4). It ends on recognition, not a lesson — an open door (Pattern 10). The rejected labels ("pretty," "peaceful," "magical") never appear.

### Worked example B — Copywriting / brand (a real product detail: a hand-thrown ceramic mug)

**Subject:** the glaze on a hand-thrown mug we sell. · **Whose eyes:** the brand's attention. · **Format:** landing-page product paragraph. · **Contact:** the mug in hand on the desk. · **Rejected labels:** *beautiful artisan craftsmanship*, *premium quality*, *a touch of elegance*. · **Honesty spine:** every detail below is true of the actual object.

**The passage:**
> Hold it to the window and the glaze isn't one blue. It pools dark in the throwing-rings the potter's fingers left climbing the wall, then thins to the color of skim milk over the high ridges, so the whole cup reads like weather seen from above. Where the kiln ran hottest, near the rim, the surface broke into a fine map of cracks you feel before you see — a faint catch under the lip, like frost. No two come off the wheel the same. The one on your desk in a year will hold the print of a thousand mornings and still surprise you in the four o'clock light.

*Why it works:* No "beautiful," no "premium," no "elegance" (AN-2, AN-4 — all rejected). The luminous quality is *released* by precision — "pools dark in the throwing-rings," "thins to the color of skim milk," "a fine map of cracks you feel before you see" (Pattern 8). The calculus arrives twice: the glaze read across the surface like *weather*, and the projection forward — the mug *changing over a year* in the "four o'clock light" (Pattern 8 motion). The felt benefit — uniqueness, daily intimacy, a thing that rewards return — is refracted through real particulars, never claimed (Pattern 6, recognition over assertion). And the close opens forward ("still surprise you") rather than landing a CTA-lesson. Honesty spine intact: these are true properties of the object.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Paul Harding Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Pre-Linguistic Attention**, **The Two Things**, **Co-extensivity**, **Precision-as-Wonder**, **Cadence**, **Recognition over Lesson**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Slowed down past recognition (Pattern 2, Rubric: Pre-Linguistic Attention):** at least one delivered image is a thing you'd have skipped on autopilot — freshly *seen*, not recalled from the category. If every image is first-label, you didn't look; return to Step 2.
- **No received language (AN-2):** scan every phrase — could you have written it without looking at the actual thing? If yes, cut it and re-see. The rejected-labels list from Step 1 must appear *nowhere* in the passage.
- **Precision, not ornament (Pattern 8, AN-4, Rubric: Precision-as-Wonder):** the ordinary turned luminous *through* exact, moving description — no decorative adjective is doing the lifting. No pathetic-fallacy slather, no adjective stacks. Every word, when cut, must lose *information*, not just prettiness.
- **The calculus is present (Pattern 8):** something *moves or changes over time* — the light shifts, a surface trembles, the wake closes. A frozen passage is a photograph, not perception. Mine the verbs.
- **Both things, refracted not stated (Pattern 6, AN-3, Rubric: The Two Things):** if the subject carries a charge, the literal thing came first (true), and the felt experience is refracted *through* it — no emotion is named, no lesson landed.
- **No clanking gears (Pattern 4, Rubric: Co-extensivity):** a reader can't locate where description ends and the perceiving mind begins. No "she looked out and thought..." seams.
- **It plays aloud (Pattern 11, Rubric: Cadence):** read cold, the accents fall where the meaning wants them — angular, lucid, no sing-song, no stray eighth note.
- **Opens a door (Pattern 10, AN-3, Rubric: Recognition over Lesson):** the ending invites recognition ("somebody finally put that into words"), it does not deliver a takeaway. The reader wants to return, not to file it.
- **Honesty spine intact:** every rendered particular is *true* of the real subject. No vivid detail was invented to fake authenticity. (In commercial work, route any unsubstantiated claim back to the copywriter; this workflow renders truth, it never manufactures it.)
- **The calibration test:** would Harding recognize a mind paying real attention, or someone filling lyric-prose slots? If the second — rewrite.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Rushing past the thing toward its meaning (AN-1).** The instant you reach "it's symbolic" or "and that's when I understood," you've stopped looking — you skipped the observation and installed a meaning instead of letting it accrue. The passage *announces* significance instead of earning it. **Recovery:** return to Step 2, the pre-linguistic table. Go back to the last *literal* observation and ask "then what is actually there?" — the meaning will accrue from the precise chain. Harding's whole faith: the world means itself; you don't add the significance.

- **Slathering beauty instead of releasing it (AN-4).** Reaching for "beautiful" adjectives — *ancient, golden, magical, ethereal, sorrowful* — to make the thing *feel* important, stacking three modifiers where one exact noun would do. This is ornamental "racoo baloney," prose written to be admired. **Recovery:** Step 3. Replace every beauty-duty adjective with a more *exact* observation, then add the calculus (motion). Precision releases the wonder that ornament can only counterfeit — describe the mold-seam, not the magic.

- **Stating the feeling instead of refracting it through the thing (AN-3).** Writing "it was so peaceful and nostalgic, and I felt such longing" — telling the reader how to feel, "the most violent thing you could do." **Recovery:** Step 4. Cut every named emotion. Give the precise literal thing first (Steps 2–3), then let the feeling live in *what gets noticed and how it's named* — the wake taken back without a mark, the four o'clock light. The reader will feel it harder because they were shown the real thing and trusted to recognize the charge.

- **Inventing the sensory data (honesty-spine breach — the most dangerous, because the craft makes the lie convincing).** Reaching for "vivid" details you didn't actually observe, especially in commercial work, because they sound good. The better the precision, the more believable the fabrication. **Recovery:** stop and re-establish the **point of contact** from Input. Describe only what you have genuinely perceived or can truthfully recall; if the detail isn't real, either go observe it or describe a different, true particular. In copy, route unprovable claims back to copy-engine / luke-iha — Harding's craft makes a *true* thing land harder; it never fakes one.
