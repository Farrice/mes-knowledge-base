# Perceptual Copy — Make the Reader FEEL the Product (Honesty Spine)

A feature list tells the reader the product is good. It never lets them *feel* it, so they don't believe it — they file it. Harding's whole faith is that "the world means itself": render a real thing precisely enough, plus the experience of it, and you do not have to argue the benefit — the reader recognizes it. "Oh my god, that's absolutely true." This workflow ports that engine onto copy. You do not add claims and you do not slather adjectives. You find the *real, true* particulars of the product, give the reader each literal thing and the felt charge of it, and let the benefit arrive by recognition instead of assertion. **Because the craft makes a thing land harder, the detail must be real — a false "vivid" detail is the most dangerous failure here, because the better the prose, the more convincing the lie.** Harding supplies the perception; copy-engine and luke-iha supply the proof, the offer, and the mechanism. He is the man making the true line *felt*, not the man inventing the claim.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this workflow is dangerous *precisely because* perceptual craft can make an unsubstantiated detail feel true):

- **Pattern 6 — The Two Things (Literal Thing + Felt Thing)** — the core engine: write the precise literal thing first (neutral, true), then layer the character's/reader's charged experience of it. The gap between them is where the benefit lives.
- **Pattern 8 — Botanist's Precision Releases Wonder** — describe the thing *more exactly* (and in motion) instead of slathering adjectives; precision is what makes the ordinary luminous. The anti-slop discipline for copy.
- **Pattern 10 — Persuade by Recognition, Not Argument** — the reader is moved by recognizing a felt truth they couldn't articulate, not by being told the benefit. Never state the takeaway; open the door, don't close it.
- **Cross-Domain Applications — Copywriting row + the honesty-spine paragraph** — "Give the reader the literal product detail (true) *and* the felt experience of it — let them infer the benefit through recognition. The detail must be real." The non-negotiable spine.
- **Paul Harding Would Never... AN-3 (Explaining the Feeling / Landing a Lesson)** — "the most violent thing you could do." In copy this is the stated benefit and the bossy CTA. Replace the told feeling with the precise thing + the experience of it.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before writing a single line. Specifically confirm:
> - **Q1 (slow down to what's literally there):** you have the *actual* product in front of you — the real material, the real motion, the real moment of use — not a marketing summary of it. If all you have is the brief's benefit bullets, you cannot do this pass yet; go get the real particulars.
> - **Q3 (literal thing AND felt experience, not the explained feeling):** you will render the real detail and the reader's experience of it, and you will *not* state the benefit. If a line's job is to tell the reader how to feel, it fails AN-3.
> - **Q5 (precision over ornament):** the lift comes from exact, moving description, not from "beautiful" adjectives.
>
> **Honesty spine (the hard gate — do not pass without it):** every particular you are about to render must be **real and verifiable** about *this* product. The craft amplifies a true claim; it never manufactures an unsubstantiated one. If a detail is aspirational, generic, or unconfirmed, it does not get the perceptual treatment — kick it back to copy-engine / luke-iha for a real proof point or a real mechanism. **A perceptually-rendered false detail is the failure mode to kill on sight, because the rendering makes the lie believable.**

## Input Required

- **The product / service itself** — and access to the *real* particulars: what it's actually made of, how it actually feels/sounds/looks/works in the hand, the real moment someone uses it. Not the brief's adjectives — the thing.
- **The copy as it stands** (if rescuing) — the actual block, email, or page that reads as a feature/benefit list. Whole thing, so we tune the real lines.
- **The format** — ad / landing section / email / social / product-page block. Drives length and how much rendering runway you get.
- **The single benefit each block is supposed to deliver** — stated plainly, for *your* reference only. (This is the thing the reader must *infer by recognition* — it is the one thing you are forbidden to write.)
- **Proof / honesty status** — for each particular you intend to render: is it true and substantiated about this product? Mark each REAL / UNCONFIRMED before you write. UNCONFIRMED details do not get rendered.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Inventory the REAL particulars (the honesty gate, before any prose)

Before you make anyone *feel* anything, list the literal, true things about the product — sense by sense, as if you'd never seen it (Pattern 2: slow down to what's literally there). This is the honesty gate and the raw material at once. You are mining for the specific real detail that, rendered precisely, will carry the benefit — not the benefit, and not an off-the-rack descriptor.

For each particular, capture what is *literally* there and confirm it's real. Then mark which sense it lives in — copy that only ever describes how a thing *looks* is half-blind.

| Sense / register | What is literally, verifiably there (no adjectives yet) | REAL or UNCONFIRMED | Which benefit it can carry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sight | (the exact material, the exact motion, the exact color *as it changes*) | | |
| Sound | (the click, the silence, the hum — the real one) | | |
| Touch / weight | (the heft, the give, the temperature, the grain) | | |
| The moment of use | (the literal small action: the thumb, the first sip, the open) | | |

> **The honesty cut, here:** strike every UNCONFIRMED row now. You cannot render what isn't real, because the rendering is exactly what would make it believed. If the strongest "particular" you have is an adjective from the brief ("premium," "buttery," "effortless"), that is not a particular — it's a stated feeling (AN-3) wearing a sensory costume. Go find the real thing under it, or route the claim back to the copywriter. **What survives this table is the only material you're allowed to render.**

### Step 2 — Find the one detail that means the benefit (Pattern 6, the literal half)

Not every real particular earns a place. Harding doesn't render the whole creek — he finds the one feature of it that, for *this* person, carries the charge. Pick, per block, the single real detail that the target reader's *felt benefit lives inside*. The test: if you rendered this one thing precisely, would the reader feel the benefit without you naming it?

| Block / moment in the copy | The benefit it must deliver (your eyes only — never written) | Candidate real details (from Step 1) | The ONE detail that carries it |
|---|---|---|---|
| (e.g. hero) | (e.g. "this knife is sharp and precise") | (the edge, the weight, the cut) | (the tomato skin parting *before* you feel pressure) |

Choose the detail that is most *specific* and most *picture-able in the body* — the reader has to be able to feel it, not decode it. A detail you can point at or feel beats a detail you have to interpret. Write the chosen detail down as a neutral, literal fact first — no charge yet. That neutral truth is the foundation; the felt layer goes on top of it, never instead of it.

### Step 3 — Render the literal thing with botanist's precision, in motion (Pattern 8)

Now describe the chosen detail *exactly* — and crucially, describe how it *changes over time*. Harding's discovery: clinical precision doesn't kill the wonder, it releases it, and adding "calculus" (the thing in motion) is what makes it astonishing. The reflex you must beat is slathering: reaching for "luxurious, premium, buttery-smooth" to make it *feel* important. Don't decorate the thing — render it more precisely and let it move.

| Reflex (cut it) | The precise-and-moving render (keep it) |
|---|---|
| "Buttery-smooth leather." | "The strap takes the shape of your shoulder by the third wearing; the crease that forms is yours, not the factory's." |
| "A powerful, premium blade." | "The skin of the tomato parts a half-second before you feel the blade — you finish the cut having pressed nothing." |
| "Rich, immersive sound." | "The bass arrives in your chest a beat before your ears name it." |

Rules for this render:
- **No adjective does the lifting.** If the line still works with the praise-adjective deleted, the adjective was decoration (AN-4); cut it. The verbs and the motion carry it. Mine the *verbs* hardest — that's where the narrative of the experience lives.
- **Drop received language (AN-2).** Strike "effortless," "seamless," "game-changing," "next-level," and any phrase you could have written without looking at the actual product. If it arrived pre-fabricated, distrust it and re-see the thing.
- **Add the calculus.** A still description is a photograph. Show the detail *across the moment of use* — the crease forming, the skin parting, the bass arriving — so the reader lives it forward.

### Step 4 — Layer the felt experience, co-extensively (Pattern 6 felt half + Pattern 4, no clanking gears)

The literal render is the world, neutrally true. Now give the reader's *experience* of it — but do not bolt it on with a clanking gear ("…and you'll feel confident," "which makes you feel"). Let the perception *be* the feeling. Harding refuses the seam between the outside thing and the inside experience; in copy the seam is the phrase "which means" and the stated emotion after it. Erase it.

| The clanking-gear version (cut) | The co-extensive version (keep) |
|---|---|
| "The crease that forms is yours — *which makes the bag feel personal and premium.*" | "The crease that forms is yours, not the factory's. A year in, no one else has one like it." |
| "The skin parts before you press — *so you'll feel like a pro in the kitchen.*" | "The skin parts before you press. You start cooking the way you always pictured yourself cooking." |

The felt half is still *shown*, not told — it renders what the experience *is*, not a label for it. The gap between the literal thing (Step 3) and the felt experience (Step 4) is a real dimension the reader steps into; that gap is where the benefit becomes felt rather than asserted. Keep both halves. Do not collapse them into a stated emotion.

### Step 5 — Close by recognition, not by lesson (Pattern 10, AN-3)

The CTA and the closing line are where copy most reflexively explains the feeling and lands the takeaway — "Don't miss out on the confidence you deserve." That is AN-3: the told feeling, the closed door. Harding's close *opens* — it leaves the reader with the felt truth still ringing, so they move toward the product because they recognized something, not because they were instructed to.

| The lesson-landed close (cut) | The recognition close (keep) |
|---|---|
| "Get the bag that makes you feel premium and confident." | "The crease will be yours by spring." |
| "Don't settle for dull knives — upgrade to precision today." | "Cut one tomato. You'll get it." |
| "Experience sound the way it was meant to be heard." | "Play the song you know by heart. Listen to where the bass lands." |

The recognition close ends on the *thing*, or on the reader's small true future action with it — never on the named emotion, never on the bossy verb. It can still carry the do-it instruction (Harding's lane is the felt render, not the offer mechanics), but it lands on a real, picture-able particular, not on "transform / unlock / deserve." If you need a hard Saxon punch on the button itself, that's the **/ward-saxon-punch** handoff — this workflow makes the body *felt*; Ward lands the button.

### Step 6 — Clutter-then-distill, then read it cold (Pattern 13 + the honesty re-check)

Harding overwrites then carves — "easier to have too much and pull back." Write the block fuller than it needs to be (multiple renders of the detail, several felt layers), then distill to the one render and the one felt layer that do the most work. Then read the whole block *cold, aloud,* as a first-time skeptic who has never heard of this product.

- **The feel test (the whole point):** does the reader *feel* the product, or are they being *told about* it? If any line's job is to inform them how good it is, it failed; replace it with the precise thing + the experience.
- **The recognition test (Pattern 10):** would the reader think "yes — that's exactly it," or "good point, noted"? The first is recognition; the second means you stated the benefit. Rebuild toward recognition.
- **The clank test (Pattern 4):** scan for "which means," "so you'll feel," "making you" — the gear-shift seams. Dissolve them.
- **The ornament test (AN-4):** delete every praise-adjective and read again. If the line still lands, leave them out. If it collapses, the adjective was load-bearing decoration — re-render with precision and motion instead.
- **The honesty re-check (the spine, non-negotiable):** for every rendered particular, confirm it is still *true and verifiable* about this product. The craft has made these lines land harder than the original feature list — which is exactly why a false one is now more dangerous. If the precision made an unconfirmed detail *feel* real, that is the failure to kill on sight. A device rings a true line; it must never be the reason an empty one sounds true.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Ad (paid social / display)** | One detail, one render, one felt layer — you have no runway. Open *on* the precise real moment of use (the tomato skin parting), not on a claim or a "POV: you just…". Land on recognition, not on "shop now energy." The image and the line should make the same single thing felt. |
| **Landing-page section / product-page block** | Each section owns *one* real detail that carries *one* benefit (Step 2 table, one row per section). Render literal+felt, plain copy between. Do not stack three rendered details in a paragraph — that's clutter you didn't distill (Pattern 13). The eye stops on the rendered line; the connective copy stays plain so the rendered lines stay audible. |
| **Email** | Tightest — usually *one* rendered detail per email, built around the single felt moment this email exists to deliver. Open on the literal thing (a real scene of use), let the felt layer arrive co-extensively, close on recognition. Stacking renders in a 150-word email reads as trying too hard. |
| **Social (organic LinkedIn / X / Substack)** | Open on one precisely-seen *real* moment of using the product, not an abstract claim or a "here's what I learned" lesson. Let the post dilate the reader by recognition; depth, not width — a short post that feels substantive and re-readable. Honesty spine is *more* exposed here (no "ad" frame to excuse hype) — the detail must be real or the audience smells the manufacture. |
| **Ghostwriting (founder describing their own product)** | Render through the *founder's* grain, not yours (surrender to their fingerprint) — find the real detail *they* actually notice about their product and the way *they* name it. The felt layer must read as their lived experience of the thing, not a copywriter's. Dissolve the seam between their world and their worldview; never pastiche a "lyric" voice onto them. |
| **Content / brand prose** | The brand's "character" emerges from what its world *notices* and how it names things (Pattern 5) — give the brand a consistent perceptual repertoire (its real colors, its real light, its kind of verb) and reserve it. Make the overlooked everyday detail of the product luminous through precise attention, not through hot-take or hype. |

### Worked example A — Product-page block (a leather weekender bag)

**Benefit this block must deliver (reference only):** *this bag ages into something personal and gets better with use.* · **Real detail that carries it:** the strap takes the owner's shoulder over time (confirmed: full-grain vegetable-tanned leather, which genuinely creases and patinas with use — REAL).

> **BEFORE (feature/benefit list):**
> "Crafted from premium full-grain leather, this luxurious weekender ages beautifully over time, developing a rich patina that's uniquely yours. Effortlessly stylish and built to last, it's the bag that makes a statement."

> **AFTER (perceptual copy):**
> *Literal render (precise + in motion):* "Full-grain, vegetable-tanned. By the third trip, the strap has stopped being a strap the factory made and started taking the exact set of your shoulder — a soft crease where the weight rides, deepening every time you sling it on."
> *Felt layer (co-extensive):* "A year in, the leather has gone the warm brown of a thing that's been somewhere. No one else has one like it, because no one else carried it."
> *Recognition close:* "Pack it Friday. By Sunday it already knows you."

*Why it converts:* the reader *feels* the bag becoming theirs — the crease forming, the color deepening, "it already knows you" — and infers "personal, gets better, lasts" without the copy ever asserting "premium," "luxurious," "uniquely yours," or "makes a statement." Every rendered particular is true of vegetable-tanned full-grain leather (it does crease and patina). No praise-adjective does the lifting; the verbs do (*stopped being, taking the set, deepening, gone, knows*). The honesty spine holds: nothing is claimed that the material doesn't actually do.

### Worked example B — Email (a precision chef's knife)

**Benefit this block must deliver (reference only):** *this knife is sharp and precise — it makes you cook better.* · **Real detail that carries it:** a properly sharp thin-bladed knife parts skin before the cook registers pressure (REAL — true of a thin, sharp blade).

> **BEFORE (feature/benefit list):**
> "Experience unmatched precision with our razor-sharp, professional-grade chef's knife. Effortlessly slice through anything and feel like a pro. Upgrade your kitchen today — you deserve it."

> **AFTER (perceptual copy):**
> *Literal render (precise + in motion):* "Set the edge on a tomato and the skin parts a half-second before you've decided to press. You finish the cut having leaned on nothing."
> *Felt layer (co-extensive):* "The first few dinners after, you keep noticing it — the small surprise of food just *opening* under your hand. Then you stop noticing, because that's how cooking is supposed to feel."
> *Recognition close:* "Cut one tomato. You'll get it."

*Why it converts:* the reader *feels* the precision — skin parting before pressure, food opening under the hand — and infers "sharp, precise, makes me better in the kitchen" without "razor-sharp," "professional-grade," "effortlessly," "like a pro," or "you deserve it." The close opens a door ("you'll get it") instead of landing the lesson ("upgrade today"). Honesty spine: the rendered behavior is what a genuinely sharp thin blade does — true, not manufactured. No adjective lifts; the verbs and the motion do (*parts, decided, leaned, opening, get it*).

## Common Pitfalls

- **Slathering adjectives to manufacture the feeling (AN-4).** Reaching for "premium, luxurious, buttery-smooth, razor-sharp" to make the product *seem* good — beauty applied *to* the thing instead of released *from* it. This is the exact failure of the original feature list, dressed up. **Recovery:** Step 3 — delete every praise-adjective and instead describe the real detail *more precisely and in motion* (the crease forming, the skin parting). If you can't render it precisely, you don't actually have the real particular yet (Step 1) — go get it.
- **Bolting the benefit on with a clanking gear (AN-3 / AN-5).** Rendering a nice literal detail and then ruining it with "…which means you'll feel confident" or "…so you look like a pro." The seam announces the manufacture and the stated emotion kills the recognition. **Recovery:** Step 4 — cut the "which means / so you'll feel" and let the felt layer be *shown* (the experience itself), co-extensive with the perception. The gap between the literal thing and the felt thing does the work; don't fill it with a label.
- **Rendering a detail that isn't real (the honesty-spine breach — the most dangerous).** The perceptual craft makes a vivid claim feel true, so it's tempting to render an aspirational or generic "detail" the product doesn't actually deliver — and now it's *more* believable than an honest feature list would be. **Recovery:** Step 1's honesty gate is non-negotiable — every particular is marked REAL or struck. If the strongest material you have is an adjective from the brief, that's a stated feeling in a sensory costume, not a real detail; route the claim back to copy-engine / luke-iha for a true proof point or mechanism. A perceptually-rendered false detail is the failure mode to kill on sight.
- **Landing the lesson at the close (AN-3).** Ending on "Don't miss out — upgrade to the confidence you deserve today," closing the door with a told feeling and a bossy verb. **Recovery:** Step 5 — close on the real thing or the reader's true small future action ("Cut one tomato. You'll get it." / "By Sunday it already knows you."). Open the door; let recognition, not instruction, move them. If the button itself needs a hard punch, hand off to /ward-saxon-punch — but the body lands on a felt particular, not a stated emotion.
