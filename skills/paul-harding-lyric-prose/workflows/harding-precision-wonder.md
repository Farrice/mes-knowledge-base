# Botanist's Precision — Plus Calculus, So the Light Moves (Paul Harding)

Harding's terror was that describing a thing with "a botanist's precision" — exact, clinical, the way you'd key out a leaf in a field guide — would turn the prose "surgical-sounding," would *kill* the beauty. He did it anyway, on faith. And the opposite happened: the passage "turned into much more beautiful, astonishing." Then he found the second half of the move. A botanist's precision renders the thing at one frozen instant; but light doesn't hold still, surfaces don't hold still, the world is always *changing.* So he "adds calculus to it" — the rate of change, the motion over time — and the light "moves and changes" on the page. Precision is the lens. Calculus is the breath that makes the lens come alive. Together they release the wonder that was already in the thing, because — the faith under all of it — **the world means itself.** You do not have to add significance with adjectives. You have to look harder, then watch it move.

This is the rescue instrument. Its trigger is specific and physical: you catch your own hand reaching for adjectives — "beautiful," "stunning," "haunting," "magical" — to *make* a thing feel important, OR a nature/object/everyday subject has come out flat (under-seen) or over-decorated (slathered). Either failure has the same cure: stop slathering, describe *more exactly*, then make it move. This is **not** the broad front-door engine (that's `harding-perception-engine` — build a whole passage from raw observation up). This is the narrow surgical pass: one subject, one description, the slather stripped and the calculus added.

## Input Required

- **The subject** — the one thing the description is about. Specific, single, perceivable: *the light on the kitchen wall at 4:15 in November*, not "afternoon light"; *the rust on the bottom hinge of the back gate*, not "old metal." If it's a category, narrow it to one instance you can actually see or remember.
- **The failing draft** (if rescuing) — the flat or over-decorated version as it stands. Paste the real sentence(s); we are operating on the actual words, including (especially) the slathered adjectives.
- **The slather list** — the adjectives/phrases currently doing the "make it feel important" work: *beautiful, golden, haunting, stunning, peaceful, melancholy.* Name them so you can see them and strip them. (If drafting fresh, write down the adjectives you're *tempted* to use — those are the slather you'll refuse.)
- **Point of contact** — how you can get into real sensory contact with the subject: it's in front of you now / a vivid memory / an observed scene you can re-enter. This is the honesty-spine anchor; precision must come from contact, not invention.
- **The change-over-time you can observe** — what actually *moves* in this subject across seconds or minutes (the light reddening and climbing the wall, the rust bleeding a stain wider after rain, the steam thinning). This is the raw material for the calculus pass. If you can't name a real change, you may not have looked long enough.
- **Format / where it lands** — Substack/essay line, social post, brand/marketing prose, copy with a real product detail, ghostwritten passage. Drives length and how much the felt-layer (Step 5) can carry.

## Workflow

The shape of the work is three artifacts, in order: a **precise static render** (precision, no slather) → an **"add calculus" motion pass** (the same thing, now changing over time) → the **final** (the two fused, cadence tuned, felt charge optionally layered). Never skip from slather straight to "final" — the discipline is in producing the static render first so you can *see* that precision alone already beat the adjectives.

### Step 1 — Strip the slather and name what it was hiding (AN-4 gate, "world means itself")

Do not improve the description yet. First, demolish it. Take the failing draft (or your tempted adjectives) and strike every word whose job is to *tell the reader it's beautiful/important* rather than to *show what's there.* Then ask the diagnostic question under each one: what was that adjective covering for?

| The slather word | What it claims | What it was hiding (the real thing it skipped looking at) |
|---|---|---|
| "beautiful," "stunning," "gorgeous" | "feel something" | the actual color, shape, and quality of the thing — never specified |
| "golden," "warm" (of light) | "this light is nice" | the *exact* hue, where it falls, what it does to the surface it lands on |
| "haunting," "melancholy," "lonely" | "feel sad" | the felt charge that should be refracted *through* the thing (Step 5), not stapled to it |
| "peaceful," "serene," "still" | "calm scene" | the small motions that are actually happening — the thing is never truly still |
| "ancient," "old," "timeless" | "this has weight" | the specific evidence of age — the rust pattern, the wear, the particular crack |

The faith that makes this safe: **the world means itself.** You are not removing the beauty — you are removing the *claim* of beauty so the real beauty has room to be released by precision. If stripping the adjectives leaves the sentence dead, that's not because the adjectives were holding it up; it's because nobody ever looked at the thing. Steps 2–4 are the looking.

```
SUBJECT: __________
SLATHER STRIPPED (the adjectives that were doing the lifting): __________
WHAT THEY WERE HIDING (the unlooked-at particulars): __________
```

### Step 2 — Render it with a botanist's precision (the static frame)

Now describe the thing as exactly as a field guide would describe a specimen — almost clinical, frozen at one instant. Specify what the slather refused to: the *exact* color (not "golden" — what gold? the gold of what?), the *exact* geometry, the *exact* texture and where light meets it. Use nouns and verbs that point, not adjectives that gush. Trust that exactness, not decoration, is what astonishes.

The terror you must override here: that this will read "clinical, surgical-sounding," that precision will kill the feeling. Override it on faith — Harding did, and "it turned into much more beautiful, astonishing." Precision is the *engine* of wonder, not its enemy.

| Precision move | The slather version (kill it) | The botanist version (build it) |
|---|---|---|
| **Exact color, not category** | "golden light" | "light the color of weak tea, then of cider" |
| **Exact placement, not vague glow** | "light filled the room" | "a band of light lay across the lower third of the wall, just above the baseboard" |
| **Verbs that locate, not adjectives that praise** | "the beautiful, peaceful room" | "the light pooled in the chair's seat and ran thin along its arm" |
| **The specific physical evidence** | "the old, weathered gate" | "rust had eaten through the lower hinge and bled a stain the shape of a fern down the post" |
| **Texture/edge, not mood-word** | "soft, gentle shadows" | "the shadow of the mullion fell across the floor in four hard-edged bars" |

Write the static render in full. Read it. It should already, frozen and motionless, beat the slathered version. If it doesn't yet, you haven't looked closely enough — return to contact (Step 1 of the perception engine: refuse the first label) and re-see.

```
PRECISE STATIC RENDER (no slather, exact, one frozen instant):
"__________"
```

### Step 3 — Add calculus: make it move and change over time

This is the move that separates this workflow from "just be precise." A botanist's render is a single frozen frame. But the real subject is never still — light reddens and climbs, rust spreads after rain, steam thins, frost retreats off a pane. **Add the rate of change.** Describe not just what the thing *is* but what it is *doing* and *becoming* across the seconds or minutes you watch it. Calculus is the mathematics of change over time; that's the metaphor exactly — render the derivative of the thing, the motion, and the light starts to live.

Take your static render and find every place a real change can be layered in. The richest material is almost always in the **verbs** and in **time-words** (as, by, until, slowly, then, already).

| Static (frozen) | + Calculus (moving over time) |
|---|---|
| "the light lay across the lower wall" | "the light climbed the wall a hand's-width every quarter hour, reddening as it rose" |
| "the rust bled a fern-shaped stain down the post" | "after each rain the stain crept a little wider, the fern unfurling another frond into the grain" |
| "steam stood over the cup" | "the steam thinned as I watched, lifting and unraveling until only the faintest waver hung above the rim" |
| "frost edged the windowpane" | "the frost was already retreating from the center of the pane outward, the clear circle widening as the sun found it" |

Two guardrails:
- **The change must be real and observed**, not invented to sound dynamic (honesty spine). If you didn't watch the light climb, don't say it climbed. Watch it, or render a change you actually saw.
- **Motion in the verbs, not in new adjectives.** Adding "swirling, shimmering, dancing" is just slather wearing a motion costume. The calculus is in *what the thing does over time*, rendered with precise verbs — climbed, reddened, crept, thinned, unraveled.

```
"ADD CALCULUS" MOTION PASS (the same precise thing, now changing over time):
"__________"
```

### Step 4 — Fuse, and tune the cadence by ear

Merge the static precision and the motion into one rendering. Often the static frame opens (here is the thing, exactly) and the calculus completes it (and here is what it did) — but let the fusion be seamless, not two stitched halves. Then read it aloud, cold, and tune: cut the stray eighth note, kill any sing-song, keep it angular and lucid. The sentence should *play* — the accents falling where the meaning wants them. (Pattern 11; deploy lightly here — full cadence work is its own pass.)

Run the AN-4 re-check on the fused version: is any word here in service of being *admired* rather than in service of the *thing*? If a phrase is performing its own beauty, cut it. The reader should forget they're reading prose; they should just *see the light move.*

### Step 5 — (Optional) Layer the felt charge through the thing, never on it

If the subject carries a felt charge for the perceiver — the kitchen light that means a specific lost afternoon, the rust that means neglect, the steam that means the last quiet minute before a hard day — layer it now, *through* the precisely-moving thing, not stated on top of it (Pattern 6, The Two Things). Give the literal thing exactly (Steps 2–4), then let one move reveal the charge — what the perceiver *does* with the seeing, or one figurative turn that arrives *after* the precision has earned it. Never replace the precision with the feeling; the feeling is trustworthy only because the real thing was shown first. And never close on a stated emotion ("it made me sad") — that's the slather of Step 1 returning at the end. Open the door; let recognition do the rest.

### Worked example A — Essay line (the 4:15 light under bare trees)

**Subject:** the November light on the kitchen wall at 4:15, coming low through the bare trees. · **Point of contact:** observed, this week, real. · **Slather to strip:** *beautiful, golden, melancholy, peaceful.*

**The slather version (anti — what to kill):**
> "The beautiful golden afternoon light streamed peacefully through the bare branches, filling the kitchen with a haunting, melancholy glow that made the whole room feel timeless and serene."

*Why it fails Harding's standard:* every load-bearing word is an adjective *claiming* a feeling — *beautiful, golden, peacefully, haunting, melancholy, timeless, serene* — and not one of them shows what's actually on the wall. It's AN-4 ornament: written to be admired, not to render the thing. The reader is *told* to feel something and shown nothing to feel it about.

**— PRECISE STATIC RENDER —**
> "The light came in low and lay across the lower third of the wall in a single band, the color of weak tea, and where the bare branches crossed in front of the window they laid four thin grey bars over it."

*Why it beats the slather:* "the color of weak tea," "the lower third of the wall," "four thin grey bars" — exact placement, exact color, exact geometry. Frozen, clinical, and already more alive than the adjective pile, because now there is something real to see.

**— "ADD CALCULUS" (it moves) —**
> "As I stood there the band climbed the wall, reddening as it rose — tea to cider to a thin rust — and the four grey bars stretched and slid sideways with it, until the light reached the cabinets and went out all at once."

*The real change rendered:* the light *climbs and reddens* (the rate of change across the minutes), the branch-shadows *stretch and slide* with the sun's descent, then it *goes out all at once.* The motion is all in the verbs — climbed, reddening, stretched, slid, went out — no motion-costume adjectives.

**— FINAL (fused, cadence-tuned) —**
> "The light came in low and lay across the lower third of the wall, the color of weak tea, four thin bars of branch-shadow laid over it. As I watched it climbed and reddened — tea, then cider, then a thin rust — the shadows stretching and sliding sideways with it, until it reached the cabinets and went out all at once, and the room was just a room again."

*Wonder released, not slathered:* not one "beautiful" or "golden"; the ordinary 4:15 light turns luminous purely through the exact color-sequence and the watched motion of its climbing and going out. The faint charge (something ending) is *refracted* through "went out all at once, and the room was just a room again" — never stated as melancholy. The world meant itself.

### Worked example B — Marketing / brand prose (a true product detail)

**Subject:** the brass key-tag on a leather goods maker's keychain, after a year of daily use. · **Point of contact:** the actual aged sample. · **Slather to strip:** *beautiful, premium, timeless, rich.*

**The slather version (anti):**
> "Our beautiful, premium brass tag develops a rich, timeless patina that's simply stunning."

*Why it fails:* four adjectives, zero seeing. *Beautiful, premium, rich, timeless, stunning* — all claim, no thing. The reader has no idea what the tag actually looks like after a year, only that they're supposed to be impressed.

**— PRECISE STATIC RENDER —**
> "After a year the brass tag is no longer the flat yellow it shipped as. It's darkened to the brown of an old penny, except along the edge and across the maker's stamp, where a thumb passes a dozen times a day and keeps it bright."

**— "ADD CALCULUS" —**
> "The change keeps going: the dark creeps in from the corners month by month, the bright worn patches shifting as your grip shifts, so that a year from now the tag will carry a map of exactly how your hand holds it."

**— FINAL (fused) —**
> "The brass tag ships flat yellow and doesn't stay that way. Within a year it darkens to the brown of an old penny — except along the edge and across the stamp, where your thumb passes a dozen times a day and keeps it bright. The dark keeps creeping in from the corners, the bright patches shifting with your grip, until the tag carries a map of exactly how your hand holds it."

*Wonder released, not slathered:* no "premium," no "timeless"; the patina is made desirable through the *exact* color-change (flat yellow → old-penny brown) and the *motion over time* (the dark creeping, the bright patches shifting into a "map of how your hand holds it"). The honesty spine holds because every detail is true of the real aged sample — the precision sells *because* it's real.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Adding a precise word but keeping the slather (the half-measure).** Writing "the beautiful light, the color of weak tea, streamed gorgeously" — bolting precision *onto* the adjectives instead of *replacing* them. The slather is still doing the claiming and now the precision just decorates it. **Recovery:** re-run Step 1 — strike every "beautiful/gorgeous/stunning" *first,* render the precision into the gap they leave, and confirm not one survivor is doing the "feel something" job. Precision *replaces* ornament; it doesn't accompany it.
- **Precision without calculus — a frozen still-life (the missing half).** Rendering an exact, clinical static frame and stopping there, so it reads accurate but inert — a museum label, not living light. **Recovery:** run Step 3 in full. Find what the subject *does over time* (climbs, reddens, creeps, thins) and put the change in the verbs. A botanist's render is the lens; the calculus is the breath. Both, or it doesn't come alive.
- **Calculus as motion-costume adjectives.** Reaching for "swirling, shimmering, dancing, glittering" and calling it motion — which is just a second wave of slather pretending to be dynamic. **Recovery:** delete the -ing adjectives; render the *actual physical change over time* with precise verbs and time-words (as, by, until, then) — the light *climbed,* the rust *crept,* the steam *thinned.* Real change observed, not a sparkle word.
- **Inventing the precision to sound convincing (honesty-spine breach).** Manufacturing an exact color or a watched motion you never actually observed, because precise prose is persuasive — the most dangerous failure, since the better the craft the more convincing the lie. **Recovery:** before delivering, confirm contact for every particular and every change. If you'd be inventing the sensory data, go look, recall harder, or render a thing you have genuinely seen. Harding's precision makes a *true* thing land harder; it must never fake one.
