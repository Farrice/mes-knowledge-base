# Counterpoint Description — Render a Thing in the Verbs of Its Opposite (Paul Harding)

Clouds are vaporous and silent. So Harding asked the only question that breaks habituated seeing: what would they be if they were *stone*? A mountain range. A herd of buffalo. And then the verbs arrived — the clouds *stampeded* across the sky, their *roar* rolling ahead of them, the horizon *shaking* under hooves of vapor. The cloud was never lied about. It was still a cloud, still moving, still being described — but borrowed into the muscle and noise of its exact opposite, it snapped the reader awake: *I don't quite know what it means, but...* That collision — gas rendered in solid's verbs, liquid in stone's, silence in a roar's — is this workflow. You take a description that has gone habitual or generic, name what category the thing essentially *is* (vapor, silence, stillness, weightlessness), deliberately borrow the verb and noun repertoire of the opposite category, and test the collision on the page. "So much happens in the verbs." This is the rescue move when a passage has gone dead — and the guard, always, is that you stay *literal*. You are not announcing a metaphor. You are describing the thing, harder, through a lens that makes it impossible to skim.

This is a **scalpel, not the whole engine.** It is for the line that has gone limp — not for carpet-bombing a passage. Counterpoint everywhere is louder than counterpoint nowhere; if everything is startling, nothing is. One or two collisions, well-buried, in a passage of precise plain description. That is the dosage.

## Input Required

- **The subject** — the one thing whose description has gone limp: a specific object, element, phenomenon, or moment. *The fog rolling off the bay this morning*, not "weather." *The silence in the house after she left*, not "quiet." Narrow it to a single instance you can perceive or truly recall.
- **The dead line (if there is one)** — the habitual/generic sentence you're trying to rescue, verbatim. *"The clouds drifted peacefully across the blue sky."* Seeing the off-the-rack version is how you find what to break. If you're writing fresh, skip this — but confirm the line genuinely needs snapping (Pre-Flight gate), don't manufacture a problem.
- **Format / where it lands** — Substack/essay line, social post opener, marketing/brand prose, copy with a real product detail, ghostwritten passage. Drives length and how much weight one collision can carry.
- **Felt charge** (optional) — the experience the subject holds, if any (menace, tenderness, awe, dread). The choice of *which* opposite category to borrow can be tuned to the charge — clouds-as-buffalo carries menace and scale; clouds-as-spilled-milk carries softness. Leave blank if you don't know yet; Step 2 may surface it.
- **Honesty anchor** (commercial work only) — confirm the thing's real behavior is being revealed, not invented. The borrowed verbs dramatize a *true* motion of the subject; they don't lie about it.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Name the thing's essential category (the pole you'll invert)

Before you can borrow the verbs of the *opposite*, you have to name what the thing essentially *is* — its native category along some sensory or physical axis. Harding's clouds are, essentially, **vapor** (formless, weightless, silent, slow). That single naming is what makes the inversion possible: you can't borrow from the opposite until you know which pole you're standing on.

Pin the subject's essence on the axis that matters most for this description. A thing has several; pick the one your dead line was *failing* to convey.

| Axis | One pole | The opposite pole |
|---|---|---|
| **State of matter** | gas / vapor (cloud, steam, breath, fog) | solid / stone (mountain, rock, bone, iron) |
| **State of matter** | liquid (river, milk, blood, rain) | solid (glass, ice, brick) — *or* gas (smoke, mist) |
| **Sound** | silence / stillness (a held breath, an empty room) | roar / clamor (a stampede, an engine, surf) |
| **Motion** | stillness (a stalled afternoon, a frozen pond) | violent motion (a charge, an avalanche, a riot) |
| **Weight** | weightless (a feather, a thought, light) | massive (an anvil, a continent, a freight train) |
| **Temperature / energy** | inert / cold (ash, dusk, stone) | live / hot (a furnace, an animal, lightning) |
| **Scale** | small / domestic (a teacup, a sparrow) | vast / geologic (an ocean, a mountain range) |

```
SUBJECT: __________
ITS ESSENTIAL CATEGORY (the pole, on the most relevant axis): __________
THE OPPOSITE CATEGORY I'll borrow from: __________
```

> The transcript's logic exactly: *clouds are vaporous and silent* (the pole) → so render them as *stone, a mountain range, stampeding buffalo* (the opposite). Liquid-as-solid; gas-as-liquid. The bigger the true gap between the poles, the harder the collision snaps.

### Step 2 — Mine the opposite category for its VERBS first (Pattern 7 — "so much happens in the verbs")

This is the engine room, and the discipline is: **verbs before nouns.** Most writers attempting counterpoint reach for a noun ("the clouds were like mountains") and produce a tired simile. Harding mines the *verbs* of the opposite category — that's where the magic and the narrative live. "The stampeding clouds *roaring* across the sky." The noun (buffalo/mountain) may not even appear; the borrowed verbs carry the whole inversion.

Fill the opposite category's repertoire, verbs hardest:

| Repertoire | List what belongs to the OPPOSITE category (not the subject) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbs (mine these hardest)** | What does the opposite category *do*? (stone: looms, juts, grinds, holds; stampede: charges, thunders, roars, tramples; liquid: pours, seeps, pools, floods) | This is 80% of the device. Get 6–10. |
| **Nouns / things** | What things belong to it? (mountain range, herd, anvil, surf, avalanche) | Optional in the final line; often best left implied. |
| **Adjectives** (use sparingly) | Its qualities (granitic, thunderous, molten, leaden) | Lowest priority — an adjective collision is weaker than a verb collision. |

Now apply **Pattern 12 (etymological depth)** to your verb shortlist: prefer the borrowed verb you understand *below* its surface — the one whose buried sense adds a faint, productive slowdown. *To loom* once meant to come into view indistinctly and threateningly; borrowed onto fog, it does double duty. *To stampede* carries panic, not just speed. Choose verbs with buried contours over flat ones — but never a verb the reader has to decode. The goal is a beat of saturation, never a puzzle.

### Step 3 — Run the collision through the Category-Inversion Table (the core mechanic)

Now test the borrowed verbs *on the subject* and watch what happens. You are looking for the collision where the borrowed verb reveals a **true behavior** of the subject the habitual line was hiding — fog really does *pour* and *settle*; a charged silence really does *press* and *gather*. The best counterpoint is not a lie that sounds pretty; it's a truth you could only see through the opposite category's eyes.

| Subject (its essence) | Opposite category | Borrowed verbs tested on the subject | The collision (does it reveal a TRUE behavior?) |
|---|---|---|---|
| clouds (vapor, silent) | stone / stampede | stampede, roar, thunder, shake (the horizon) | YES — fast storm clouds really do mass and surge like a herd; the verbs reveal it |
| fog (gas) | liquid | pour, brim, spill, settle, drown (the road) | YES — fog genuinely behaves like a fluid; borrowed liquid verbs are *more* accurate, not less |
| a silence (no sound) | a roar / surf | gather, press, build, break (over the room) | YES — a charged silence really does accrue pressure and "break" |
| afternoon light (still, soft) | a blade / fire | slant, cut, lay (a stripe), kindle, climb | test it — does the room's light actually do this over time? |
| [your subject] | [your opposite] | [your borrowed verbs] | YES / NO — and *why* |

Two failure modes this table catches:
- **The cliché collision (AN-2):** if your tested pairing is one the language already wore out — *silence screams, flames dance, time marches* — discard it. The reader has met it; it can't snap them awake. Go back to Step 2 and find the *unused* verb in the opposite category.
- **The lie that sounds good (honesty spine):** if the borrowed verb makes the subject do something it *doesn't actually do* — purely for effect — you've left description and entered fabrication. The verb must dramatize a real motion of the real thing. Counterpoint reveals; it does not invent.

### Step 4 — Write the line LITERALLY — describe the thing, never announce the metaphor (the literalness guard, AN-4)

Now write it. And here is the move that keeps this in Harding's lane instead of a creative-writing exercise: **write it as plain description of the subject, with the borrowed verbs simply doing the work** — do *not* signal "this is a comparison."

The difference is everything:

| Mode | Example | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Announced metaphor (AN-4 — kill it)** | "The clouds were *like* a herd of buffalo, *as if* stampeding, *a metaphor for* nature's power." | Flaunted. "Look at my metaphor." The simile machinery (*like, as if, as though*) and the stated meaning both have to die. |
| **Counterpoint (Harding — keep it)** | "The clouds came stampeding across the sky, their roar rolling ahead of them, the whole horizon shaking under hooves of vapor." | Literal description. The borrowed verbs carry the inversion *silently*; no "like," no announced meaning. The reader is snapped awake, the line stays descriptive. |

Rules for the literal write:
- **Strip the simile scaffolding.** *Like, as, as if, as though, resembling, a kind of* — these announce a comparison and break the spell. Let the borrowed verb attach *directly* to the subject. Not "the fog poured like water"; "the fog poured down the hill and pooled in the low road."
- **Keep one foot in the subject's real nature.** "Hooves of vapor" — the *hooves* are borrowed (stampede), the *vapor* keeps it literally a cloud. That tension (solid verb, vapor noun) is the snap. Don't let the subject fully become the other thing; it must stay recognizably itself, described.
- **Do not state what it means.** No "...reminding us of nature's force," no "...as if the sky were angry." The collision does the work; the meaning accrues. The reader should feel "I don't quite know what it means, but..." and that *is* the success state (Pattern 7 success metric).
- **Verbs lead.** If the line's power is in a borrowed *adjective* or *noun* rather than a *verb*, re-mine — you've taken the weaker version of the device.

### Step 5 — Dose, tune by ear, and confirm it still reads as description (Pattern 11 + the dosage guard)

Read the line — and the lines around it — **aloud, cold.** Three checks:

1. **Dosage.** Is this the *only* counterpoint collision in the passage, or close to it? One or two startling inversions inside precise plain description is the dose. Three or four in a paragraph and the device announces itself; the reader stops trusting that you're describing and starts watching you perform. If you've stacked them, keep the strongest, return the rest to plain precise description.
2. **Cadence (Pattern 11).** Borrowed verbs change the rhythm — stone verbs are heavy and stop-and-lurch, liquid verbs flow. Match the cadence to the collision: "stampeding... roaring... shaking" tumbles with momentum; "the fog brimmed, settled, and held" lands heavy and still. Cut the stray eighth note. Keep it angular and funky, never sing-song.
3. **The literalness re-check (the headline test for this workflow).** Read the finished line as a first-time reader. Do they receive it as **a description of the thing** (snapped awake, but reading literal) — or as **a writer showing off a metaphor**? If the latter, you failed the device. Strip more simile scaffolding, cut any stated meaning, or pull the collision back toward the subject's real behavior until it reads as description again.

> **Final gut check (the calibration test):** would Paul Harding recognize this as a mind that *re-saw* the thing through its opposite and rendered it true — or as someone reaching for a clever comparison to be admired? If unsure, it's the second. The reader should forget there is a device. Slow down and rewrite.

### Worked example A — Essay / Substack (the transcript's own clouds-as-buffalo, marked illustrative)

**Subject:** storm clouds massing over the bay before rain. · **Format:** essay line. · **Dead line:** *"The clouds drifted across the sky as a storm gathered."* · **Essential category:** vapor — formless, weightless, silent. · **Opposite borrowed:** stone / stampede — massive, kinetic, roaring. · **Borrowed verbs:** stampede, roar (rolling ahead), thunder, shake (the horizon), break.

**The counterpoint line:**
> The clouds came stampeding across the sky, their roar rolling ahead of them, the whole horizon shaking under hooves of vapor — and then they broke against the hills and the first rain came down like dropped stones.

*Why it works (Harding's standard):* The dead line's verb was *drifted* — habituated, weightless, the off-the-rack motion of clouds (AN-2). The rescue borrows the verbs of the exact opposite category (stone/stampede): the clouds *stampede*, the roar *rolls*, the horizon *shakes* — "so much happens in the verbs" (Pattern 7). Crucially it stays **literal**: there is no "like a herd of buffalo," no "as if angry," no stated meaning — the borrowed verbs attach directly to *clouds*, and "hooves of *vapor*" keeps it recognizably a cloud while the hooves snap it awake (Step 4 literalness guard, AN-4). It reveals a *true* behavior — fast storm cloud really does mass and surge — so it's description, not a lie. The reader feels "I don't quite know what it means, but..." (Pattern 7 success metric). *(This line is built from Harding's own stated transcript example — clouds rendered as stampeding stone — and is marked illustrative, not a verbatim quotation. The one simile at the close — "like dropped stones" — is deliberately the lone announced comparison and could be cut for stricter counterpoint: "and the first rain came down in dropped stones.")*

### Worked example B — Copywriting / brand (a real product behavior: fog-grey wool, the second inversion — liquid borrowed onto a gas / settling, onto a textile)

**Subject:** the way a loosely-woven grey wool throw settles when you drop it over a chair (a real product). · **Format:** landing-page product line. · **Dead line:** *"This soft, cozy wool throw drapes beautifully over any chair."* · **Essential category:** light, airy, soft (a textile, near-weightless when loose). · **Opposite borrowed:** liquid / fog — pouring, pooling, settling, brimming. · **Borrowed verbs:** pour, pool, settle, fill, brim. · **Honesty anchor:** the throw genuinely behaves this way — loose open-weave wool really does flow and pool rather than fold.

**The counterpoint line:**
> Drop it over the arm of a chair and it doesn't fold — it pours, finding the low places, pooling in the seat, brimming over the edge and settling there grey as fog that won't lift.

*Why it works:* The dead line is pure received language — *soft, cozy, drapes beautifully* (AN-2, AN-4); you could have written it without ever touching the wool. The rescue names the textile's essence (light, airy) and borrows the verbs of liquid/fog — it *pours, finds the low places, pools, brims, settles* (Pattern 7, verbs mined hardest). It stays **literal**: no "like water," no "as if liquid" — the verbs attach straight to the throw, and the one held image "grey as fog that won't lift" doubles the inversion (a gas borrowed onto the now-liquid wool) while staying descriptive. And it reveals a **true behavior** — loose open-weave wool genuinely flows and pools rather than creasing — so the honesty spine holds: this is a real product property dramatized through borrowed verbs, not a fabricated one (Pre-Flight honesty gate). The felt benefit (luxurious heaviness, generous drape) is *inferred* through the re-seeing, never claimed. No stated meaning; ends on an open image.

- **Verbs carry the inversion (Pattern 7):** the device's power lives in *borrowed verbs*, not borrowed nouns or adjectives. - **Reveals a true behavior (honesty spine):** the borrowed verbs dramatize a *real* motion of the real subject (fog really pours; storm cloud really surges) — counterpoint reveals a truth the habitual line hid; it never invents a behavior the thing doesn't have. ## Common Pitfalls

- **Announcing the metaphor instead of describing the thing (AN-4 — the signature failure of this device).** Writing "the clouds were *like* a stampede of buffalo, *as if* charging across the sky" — the simile scaffolding (*like, as if*) and any stated meaning flag it as a performance, and the spell breaks. The reader admires your cleverness instead of seeing the cloud. **Recovery:** Step 4. Strip every *like / as / as if / as though*, attach the borrowed verb *directly* to the subject ("the clouds came stampeding..."), and delete any sentence telling the reader what it means. The collision must read as literal description; the meaning accrues on its own.

- **Reaching for a noun comparison instead of mining the verbs (weak-version failure).** Producing "the fog was a grey ocean" (a tired noun metaphor) instead of borrowing the *verbs* of liquid ("the fog poured down the hill and pooled in the low road"). Noun collisions are flat; verb collisions move. **Recovery:** Step 2. Mine the opposite category's *verbs* first — get 6–10 — and prefer the ones with buried etymological weight (Pattern 12). "So much happens in the verbs"; the noun can usually stay implied.

- **The cliché collision (AN-2).** Reaching for the inversion the language already exhausted — *silence screams, flames dance, the wind howled, time marched on*. These feel like counterpoint but they're received language; the reader has met them a thousand times and skims right past. **Recovery:** Step 3's cliché check. Discard any pairing you've encountered before, return to the opposite category, and find the *unused* verb — the one no one has worn out on this subject yet. The freshness of the collision is the whole snap.

- **Counterpoint everywhere (dosage failure / AN-4 by accumulation).** Loving the device so much that every line gets a startling inversion, until the passage reads as a performance and the reader stops trusting any of it is real description. If everything is loud, nothing is. **Recovery:** Step 5 dosage check. Keep the single strongest collision (two at most in a long passage), return every other line to precise plain description. Counterpoint snaps *against a quiet ground*; remove the ground and there's nothing to snap against.

- **Lying about the thing to make the collision land (honesty-spine breach — most dangerous in commercial work, because the craft makes the lie convincing).** Borrowing a verb that makes the subject do something it doesn't actually do, purely for the startle. The better the line, the more believable the fabrication. **Recovery:** Step 3's true-behavior test. Confirm the borrowed verb dramatizes a *real* motion of the real subject — fog really does pour, loose wool really does pool. If it doesn't, the line is fantasy, not description; pick a different opposite category whose verbs reveal a truth, or in copy route the claim back to copy-engine / luke-iha. Counterpoint reveals what was always there; it never invents.
