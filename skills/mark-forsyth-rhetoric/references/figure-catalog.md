# The Figure Catalog — working cheat sheet

Forsyth's own recommendation, verbatim in method: *"I came up with little names for myself, and then I have
a cheat sheet with a few examples and some bullet points… the technical names do not matter at all, and
indeed the ancient Greeks couldn't even agree on half of these technical names."*

So this is a **cheat sheet, not a syllabus**. Own-name first, Greek in parentheses, two examples, one
trigger. Consult it while drafting the lines that matter. Never march through it in order, and never print
the Greek name in delivered prose.

Full mechanism and sourcing for each figure lives in [`../genius.md`](../genius.md). This page is for speed.

## Group 1 — Repetition (the same word, moved around)

| Own-name | Greek | Shape | Examples | Fires when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Sandwich** | diacope | X … short wedge … X | "Bond. James Bond." · "Free at last, free at last" | A name, identity, or single key word must become iconic |
| **The Drumbeat** | anaphora | Same opening, 3–6 units | "We shall fight…" · "I have a dream" | Building to a claim the reader must accept — their defences drop |
| **The Landing** | epistrophe | Same closing, 3–6 units | "Yes, we can" · "of the people, by the people, for the people" | One word must be inescapable; or the shape must enact stuckness |
| **The Closed Circle** | epanalepsis | Unit opens and ends on the same word | "Yesterday… yesterday" | A section must feel sealed, not merely stopped |
| **The Staircase** | anadiplosis | Last word of each clause opens the next | "Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate." | A causal chain where no step may be disputed |
| **The Shape-Shift** | polyptoton | One word, two grammatical forms | "Love is not love that alters when it alteration finds" · "the haters gonna hate" | A key word can be twisted; **titles especially** |
| **The Triple Hit** | epizeuxis | Same word ×3, bare | "Location, location, location" · "Howl, howl, howl" | Pure emphasis, zero argument. Once per piece, maximum |

## Group 2 — Symmetry & Balance (weighing one thing against another)

| Own-name | Greek | Shape | Examples | Fires when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Mirror** | chiasmus | Say it, then say it reversed | "Ask not what your country can do for you…" · "my mind on my money and my money on my mind" | A reversal, a reciprocal, or an objection — manufactures inevitability |
| **The Even Scales** | isocolon | Two halves, same length | "not because it's easy, but because it's hard" · "To err is human, to forgive divine" | You need to sound *reasonable* rather than clever |
| **The Rule of Three** | tricolon | Three items, third longest, usually a twist | "the good, the bad, and the ugly" · "Lies, damned lies, and statistics" | Supporting items need a click of closure |
| **The Full Sweep** | progressio | 3–6 paired opposites, in series | "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times" · "for richer, for poorer" | The point is **totality** — and "everybody" is too concise to stop the mind |

> Forsyth's own grouping note: chiasmus, isocolon and polyptoton "are all setting one thing against another
> in contrast." Same family, three tensions.

## Group 3 — Image & Sense (making the abstract perceivable)

| Own-name | Greek | Shape | Examples | Fires when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Sense-Jump** | synesthesia | Describe one sense using another | "She smelled the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight" | A smell, taste or feeling resists direct description |
| **The Dressed Abstraction** | personification | Give the concept a body and clothes | "the green-eyed monster which doth mock the meat it feeds on" | An abstract noun is the subject and stays inert |
| **The Overshoot** | hyperbole | Go far too far, unmistakably | "I feel like I'm on the surface of the sun" | Magnitude must be *felt*. Half-measures read as false claims |
| **The Impossible Thing** | adynaton | Name what would have to happen first | "till China and Africa meet, and the salmon sing in the street" | Devotion, skill or duration needs a picture, not a superlative |

## Group 4 — Structure & Punch (what you do to the sentence itself)

| Own-name | Greek | Shape | Examples | Fires when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Fist** | schesis onomaton | Kill the main verb | "London." · "Space, the final frontier." · "In victory, magnanimity" | A first line, a title, or a proclamation that must not negotiate |
| **The Long Hold** | periodic sentence | Withhold the main verb to the end | Kipling's "If" | A climax must feel earned; the reader must find nowhere to stop |
| **The Sound Upgrade** | alliteration + metre | Same meaning, better form | "Full fathom five thy father lies" vs "your father is 9.8 metres underwater" | **The content is fixed** and cannot change — so change the form |

## Group 5 — Contradiction (the koan family)

| Own-name | Greek | Shape | Examples | Fires when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Koan** | paradox | Make a sensible thing sound like nonsense | "The only thing worse than being talked about is not being talked about" | A truth must be carried away and chewed on. Do not overuse |
| **The Double Meaning** | pun-paradox | Two senses of one word collide | "Labour isn't working" · "Don't it make my brown eyes blue" | A single word does double duty and the collision is the point |
| **The Counterintuitive Proof** | veridical paradox | A puzzle with an inevitable answer | The Monty Hall problem | The audience will resist a claim — make them derive it instead |

## Group 6 — Voice & Rhythm (before any figure fires)

| Own-name | Shape | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| **The Establishing Shot** | First-paragraph diction that locates you geographically, socially, tonally | The prose is voiceless — the reader defaults to hearing "please stand clear of the closing doors" |
| **The Scales** | Nonsense iambic pentameter, improvised, daily | The writer's problem is chronic, not local |
| **The All-Senses Sweep** | Every section carries something touched, smelled, tasted (Jilly Cooper's rule) | The writing is all-visual and flat |

## The two rules that outrank every entry above

1. **The announcer rule.** Figures go where the stakes are — the claim, the turn, the close. Surrounding
   prose stays plain so the pattern registers as a rising voice. Ornament spread evenly is ornament wasted:
   a compilation of bass drops with no buildups is horrible to listen to.
2. **Classify the job first.** Dishwasher manual, or something meant to be kept? Concision is correct for
   the first and fatal to the second. Forsyth's own carve-out: "if you're just writing a quarterly company
   report, then you probably want to tone it down a little bit."

## Calibration

One or two flakes of chili in a paragraph puts you far past how most people write. The ceiling exists —
"too many chilies and now you're ill" — but almost nobody is near it. When in doubt in *this* harness, the
error to fear is the flat line, not the rich one.
