# Mark Forsyth — Rhetoric, Memorable Language & the Enchantment OS

Mark Forsyth approaches great writing the way a scientist approaches an experiment: "Fly, my pretties, fly!"
is not in *The Wizard of Oz*, yet everyone remembers it — because diacope is so powerful a figure that our
brains rewrite history to fit it. Churchill said "blood, toil, tears and sweat," and the world corrected him
to "blood, sweat and tears." **We will change what we heard, and change history, to get a nice memorable
phrase.** That is not a curiosity. It is a mechanism you can operate.

The 2026 interview adds the thesis the catalog was always serving:

> **"Don't write just to be efficient and get the meaning across. Write to enchant."**

Writing for efficiency is like dressing for efficiency — everyone in high-visibility waterproof trousers. We
decorate rooms, cook meals instead of drinking Soylent, buy clothes we like. There is no reason writing alone
should be stripped bare. And the etymology closes the argument: **a *grammar* was something written down; a
*glamour* was a spell you cast to enchant someone and carry them off.** Same word. *Spelling*, *spell*.
Written language was always a technology of enchantment; we are the generation that repurposed it for
compliance.

## Quick Reference

- **Load [genius.md](genius.md) before any workflow.** The catalog, the memory-receptor theory, the
  enchantment thesis and the operating principles live there. The working cheat sheet is
  [references/figure-catalog.md](references/figure-catalog.md).
- **The core claim**: memorable language is formula. The mind has memory receptors in certain shapes;
  rhetoric shapes words to fit the slot. Proof: we falsify memories to obey the strong ones.
- **The governor is the announcer rule.** You cannot grip a reader continuously. Figures go at the stakes
  moments; the surrounding prose stays plain so the pattern registers as a rising voice. A compilation of
  bass drops with no buildups is horrible to listen to.
- **Classify the job first.** Concision is correct for a dishwasher manual and fatal to anything meant to be
  remembered. Ecclesiastes compressed to "there is a time for everything" says the same thing and would have
  moved no one for three thousand years.
- **Names don't matter.** The ancient Greeks couldn't agree on them either. Keep a cheat sheet with your own
  names; deploy formulas, not vocabulary. Never print the Greek in delivered prose.
- **Calibration**: the sources disagree. 2024 — "I've never seen somebody overusing the figures of rhetoric."
  2026 — "there's always going to be a limit where that was too many chilies." The ceiling is real and almost
  nobody is near it; in this harness the live failure mode is the flat line, not the rich one.
- **Process spine**: prepare completely (walk, talk it aloud to an imaginary non-expert) → write as fast as
  you can (if it flows out of you, it flows into the reader) → edit by run-up (delete the run and rewrite
  whole; tinkering kills rhythm).
- **Sourcing discipline**: both transcripts are auto-captions — substance-accurate, not word-perfect. Read
  `extractions/mark-forsyth/SOURCE-MANIFEST.md` before quoting anything as verbatim Forsyth, and never
  reproduce the four UNCONFIRMED items.
