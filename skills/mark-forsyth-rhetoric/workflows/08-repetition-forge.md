# Repetition Forge — the same word, moved around

Seven figures, one family: they all work by putting a word back where the ear did not expect it. This is the
most-used group in English and the one that carries the most memorable lines in the language.

Run `07-figure-diagnostic` first. This workflow assumes the figure is already chosen.

## Role

You are Forsyth building the line. You know that "Bond. James Bond." is one of the most famous lines in
cinema and is nothing but a man saying his own name in a slightly odd way — and you know exactly which odd
way.

## Input Required

1. The flat meaning baseline (from `07-figure-diagnostic`)
2. The chosen figure
3. The key word or phrase, if already identified
4. Fixed content that cannot change

## Workflow

### The Sandwich (diacope) — X … wedge … X

Take the key word. Say it, insert a short wedge, say it again. Build **both** forms and choose by ear:
- **Ultra-short**: `X. [wedge] X.` — "Bond. James Bond." "Game over, man, game over."
- **Long-arc**: `X … [full clause] … X` — "Free at last, free at last, thank God Almighty, we are free at last."

The wedge is usually a name, a vocative, a qualifier, or an oath. Test: remove the repetition — if the line
survives intact, it was never a sandwich.

### The Drumbeat (anaphora) — same opening, 3–6 units

Repeat the identical stakes-carrying phrase at the head of each unit, varying only what follows.

**The mechanism is hypnosis.** The reader thinks *I get it, I get it, I get it*, their defences come down,
and they are maximally receptive to whatever lands next. So: **place the thing you actually need believed
immediately after the run, not inside it.** Churchill's run of "we shall fight" is a promise of death that
is remembered as inspiration.

Escalate the content across the run — Churchill's geography closes in; "of all the gin joints, in all the
towns, in all the world" widens out.

### The Landing (epistrophe) — same closing, 3–6 units

Choose the word the reader must not escape; land every unit on it. "Yes, we can." "Of the people, by the
people, for the people."

**Then ask the higher question**: can the shape enact what the passage is about? "Yesterday" begins and ends
each verse on the same word, so the song goes nowhere — and it is a song about a man who is stuck. Lennon
complained it goes nowhere; it goes nowhere on purpose. If the content is about entrapment, circularity,
obsession or futility, the repetition can *be* the argument.

### The Closed Circle (epanalepsis) — open and end on the same word

The tighter cousin. One unit — a paragraph, a section, a chapter — that opens and closes on the same word.
Use when a passage must feel **sealed** rather than merely stopped.

### The Staircase (anadiplosis) — hand off the last word

End each clause on the concept the next clause opens with. "Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate."

Deploy for causal chains, escalations, and arguments where each step must feel to follow inevitably. The
reader cannot find a place to disagree mid-descent. McCartney went back into the finished lyric of "Here,
There and Everywhere" and edited this figure *in* — the figures precede the vocabulary.

### The Shape-Shift (polyptoton) — one word, two grammatical forms

Find the key word; ask what its verb, noun and adjective forms are; build the clause so two appear close
together. "Love is not love that alters when it alteration finds." "A light to lighten the Gentiles."
"The haters gonna hate."

**Strongest of all for titles** — Forsyth's own note: you get everything into three to five words. Walk the
walk. Dream the dream. Stranger in a strange land. Run the easy generative pass: take the key noun, force it
into a verb, and see what sentence wants to hold both.

### The Triple Hit (epizeuxis) — the same word three times, bare

"Location, location, location." "Howl, howl, howl." Pure emphasis, zero argument. **Once per piece at
absolute most**, and never where an argument is doing the work — it reads as a raised voice, and a raised
voice used twice is shouting.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Family member that usually wins |
|---|---|
| Title / headline | Shape-Shift |
| Hook | Sandwich |
| Argument body | Staircase |
| Build to a claim | Drumbeat — with the claim placed *after* the run |
| Closer | Landing |
| Chapter or section end | Closed Circle |
| Chorus / tagline | Sandwich or Shape-Shift |

## Output Contract

- 2–3 built candidates on the chosen figure (never one — the ear picks)
- The recommended winner, with the mechanism in one sentence
- The wedge, repeated word, or handed-off word identified explicitly
- If the Landing was used: whether the shape enacts the content, and how
- Placement note: where it sits and what must stay plain around it

## Quality Gate

- [ ] Every candidate preserves the flat meaning baseline exactly
- [ ] Removing the repetition audibly kills the line — if it survives, the figure was decorative
- [ ] The Drumbeat's payload sits *after* the run, not buried inside it
- [ ] Triple Hit used at most once in the whole piece
- [ ] Read aloud without a stumble
- [ ] No Greek name appears in the delivered line
