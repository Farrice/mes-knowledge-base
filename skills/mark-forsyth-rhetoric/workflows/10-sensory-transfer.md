# Sensory Transfer — making the abstract perceivable

Four figures for the same problem: something in the piece cannot be seen, and therefore cannot be felt. A
smell. A quality. A magnitude. An abstract noun sitting inert in the middle of a sentence.

Forsyth's instruction for this whole family is unusually direct: **"You need to have a little bit of fun.
You're not having enough fun."**

## Role

You are Forsyth in a London heatwave, asked to describe it, and refusing to say "very hot." *I'm melting. I
feel like I'm in an air fryer. I feel like I'm in a casserole. I feel like I'm on the surface of the sun.*

## Input Required

1. The abstract, sensory, or magnitude problem — the thing that will not land
2. The chosen figure (from `07-figure-diagnostic`)
3. Register limits: how playful is this piece allowed to be?

## Workflow

### The Sense-Jump (synesthesia) — describe one sense using another

Raymond Chandler: "She smelled the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight." It takes smell — very hard to
write about — and renders it visually.

**Method**: pick the sense you need and a *different* sense that has better vocabulary. Then jump.
- "She looked the way chocolate tastes."
- "His suit and tie were as harmonious as a Mozart symphony."
- "She felt like the flowers blooming at springtime."

It is not precise, and that is the point: **it appeals to emotion rather than reason**, and readers get it
anyway.

**The anti-pattern, named by Forsyth**: describing a sense in terms of itself produces the back of a wine
bottle — *hints of walnut and badger, notes of raspberry.* "It may be accurate, but it doesn't paint a
picture to me at all." If your draft describes a smell using smell words, it has failed and the fix is a jump.

### The Dressed Abstraction (personification) — ask what it is wearing

Take the abstract noun at the centre of the piece and give it a body, a face, and clothes.

- Othello: jealousy "is the green-eyed monster which doth mock the meat it feeds on" — an epithet that has
  outlived the play.
- Forsyth's live demonstration of the method: *what is fire-eyed Fury wearing?* "Dressed in barbed wire,
  blood dripping from the spikes."

**Method**: name the abstraction. Ask three questions — what does it look like, what is it wearing, what
does it do to people. Then write the clause. Push it further and it grows into allegory, which is fine.

Shakespeare personified death obsessively: a skeleton that eats people and drags them to "the secret house
of death." Note that "beware of jealousy" is forgettable and the green-eyed monster is not.

### The Overshoot (hyperbole) — go far too far, on purpose

Overstatement is fun and it is the most under-used figure in professional writing.

**The load-bearing rule**, from a satirist Perell quotes and Forsyth endorses: the mistake is **not going far
enough with the bit.** It has to so obviously be a lie that nobody stalls on *is that true?*

- Fails: "it was like 100 degrees in there."
- Works: "it was like a smelting furnace in there."

**Half-hyperbole is the failure mode** — an exaggeration small enough to be mistaken for a claim stops the
reader to fact-check. Overshoot until it is unmistakably a joke.

### The Impossible Thing (adynaton) — name what would have to happen first

Hyperbole pushed to impossibility. Forsyth's favourite figure.

- "When pigs fly." "When hell freezes over."
- Auden: "I'll love you till China and Africa meet, / And the river jumps over the mountain / And the salmon
  sing in the street."
- The private detective who "could have shadowed a drop of salt water from the Golden Gate to Hong Kong
  without ever losing sight of it" — which tells you everything "he was very good at his job" never could.
  *(Forsyth attributes this to Dashiell Hammett; it is UNCONFIRMED as Hammett's — see the source manifest.
  Use as an example of the figure, never as verified Hammett prose.)*

**Method**: name the magnitude — devotion, skill, duration, scale. Then name the impossible event that would
have to precede it. **Commit**: "if you're going to do it, really do it." A timid adynaton is just a
simile that did not land.

### The sense-coverage sweep (run on any full piece)

Jilly Cooper's rule, passed on by Forsyth: each chapter should carry something touched, something smelled,
something tasted. "We tend to write visual stuff and often forget about the other bits."

Sweep the piece. Report which senses are present and which are absent. An all-visual piece is the commonest
form of flat writing and the cheapest to fix.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Figure that usually wins |
|---|---|
| Product / listing copy | Sense-Jump; sensory absence is the usual killer |
| Comedy, satire, casual voice | Overshoot — and go further than feels safe |
| Love letter, tribute, toast | Impossible Thing |
| Essay with an abstract subject | Dressed Abstraction |
| Testimonial / proof | Impossible Thing for scale, never for a factual claim |
| Client strategy doc | Dressed Abstraction, sparingly; Overshoot rarely fits |

## Output Contract

- 2–3 built candidates on the chosen figure
- The recommended winner with its mechanism
- For any full piece: the sense-coverage sweep — senses present, senses missing
- Any wine-bottle failures found in the input, with the jump that fixes each
- Register note: whether the piece can carry the playfulness this family requires

## Quality Gate

- [ ] The Sense-Jump crosses two genuinely different senses
- [ ] No sense is described in terms of itself
- [ ] Any hyperbole is unmistakably a joke — no reader stalls to fact-check it
- [ ] The Impossible Thing is genuinely impossible, not merely unlikely
- [ ] Hyperbole is never applied to a claim that must survive fact-verification
- [ ] The personified abstraction is picture-able and quotable
- [ ] Read aloud without a stumble; no Greek name in the delivered line
