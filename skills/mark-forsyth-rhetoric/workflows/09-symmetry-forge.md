# Symmetry Forge — weighing one thing against another

Four figures that all work by setting one thing against another in contrast. Forsyth groups them himself:
chiasmus, isocolon and polyptoton "are all setting one thing against another" — the difference is only what
is being weighed. Add progressio and you have the family that carries most political rhetoric in English.

Humans love symmetry. We build the Taj Mahal and St Paul's, and a symmetrical sentence makes the brain say
*of course that must be right* before the logic is checked. That is the mechanism, and it is worth stating
plainly: **these figures manufacture the feeling of inevitability.** Use them where you have earned it.

Run `07-figure-diagnostic` first.

## Role

You are Forsyth reading JFK's inaugural and noticing it is chiasmus after chiasmus after chiasmus — and that
presidents lean on the figure for a reason that has nothing to do with taste.

## Input Required

1. The flat meaning baseline
2. The chosen figure
3. What is being weighed against what
4. Fixed content that cannot change

## Workflow

### The Mirror (chiasmus) — say it, then say it reversed

Write the claim forward. Mirror the key terms in reverse order. Tune until both halves scan.

- "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country."
- "Mankind must put an end to war, or war will put an end to mankind."
- "Let us never negotiate out of fear, but let us never fear to negotiate."
- "You stood up for America; now America must stand up for you."
- "With my mind on my money and my money on my mind."

**Fires on**: a reversal of priorities, a reciprocal relationship, or an objection you are turning around.
The tell that a chiasmus is available: anytime what you mean is *and vice versa*.

**The ethical note, stated once**: this figure produces assent from shape rather than from argument. Use it
where the claim is true and the reader is resisting for the wrong reason — never to slide a weak claim past
someone's judgment.

### The Even Scales (isocolon) — two halves, same length

Two phrases of matched length and stress, weighed against each other.

- "We choose to go to the moon, not because it's easy, but because it's hard."
- "To err is human, to forgive divine."
- "Roses are red, violets are blue."

Forsyth's gloss: it is "like chiasmus but toned down" — a general weighing rather than absolute symmetry.
Kennedy could have said "because it's hard, not easy"; the balance is what makes it stately.

**Fires on**: needing to sound *reasonable* rather than clever. On the one hand this, on the other hand
that. Count syllables and match them — this figure lives or dies on the count.

### The Rule of Three (tricolon) — three items, third longest, usually a twist

Three is the shortest group that can have a conclusion: two to line things up, the third to land.

- Straight: "I came, I saw, I conquered." "Liberty, égalité, fraternité."
- **With a twist** (better): "the good, the bad, and the ugly" — two moral terms, then an aesthetic one.
  "Lies, damned lies, and statistics."
- Escalating: "of all the gin joints, in all the towns, in all the world."
- Cheating openly: "anytime, any place, anywhere" — the second and third mean the same thing and nobody
  notices or minds.

**Rules**: the third item runs slightly longer. The twist is optional but it is where the magic is — a
tricolon with no twist reads as a drumbeat, not a joke. When you only have one real item, echo it in
near-synonyms; the shape does the work.

### The Full Sweep (progressio) — 3–6 paired opposites in series

Replace an abstract totality word with paired opposites spanning the space.

- "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times."
- "For richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health."
- "A time to weep and a time to rejoice."
- "You're hot then you're cold, you're yes then you're no."
- "Black men and white men, Jews and Gentiles, Protestants and Catholics."

**Fires on**: the point being *totality*. "Everybody" is too concise — it does not stop the mind. Keep each
pair rhythmically parallel; the effect is measured and stately, and it is the figure that makes a reader
feel the full sweep instead of skimming past one abstraction.

**Length discipline**: 3–6 pairs. Two is not a sweep; more than six becomes a list and loses the music.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Usually wants |
|---|---|
| Thesis statement | Full Sweep |
| Objection-handler | Mirror |
| Closer / kicker | Mirror or Rule of Three |
| Balanced comparison, "reasonable" register | Even Scales |
| Feature or benefit run | Rule of Three, twist on the third |
| Vow, ceremony, manifesto | Full Sweep — the genre licences length |

## Output Contract

- 2–3 built candidates on the chosen figure
- The recommended winner with its mechanism in one sentence
- For the Mirror: both halves shown with the mirrored terms marked
- For the Even Scales: the syllable counts of both halves
- For the Rule of Three: what the third item twists
- Placement note

## Quality Gate

- [ ] Meaning baseline preserved exactly in every candidate
- [ ] Even Scales halves actually scan to matched length — counted, not eyeballed
- [ ] Tricolon's third item is longest and, where possible, twists
- [ ] Full Sweep runs 3–6 pairs, each rhythmically parallel
- [ ] The Mirror is not being used to smuggle a weak claim past judgment
- [ ] Read aloud without a stumble; no Greek name in the delivered line
