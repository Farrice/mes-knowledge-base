# Mark Forsyth — Genius Context

Author of *The Etymologicon*, *The Elements of Eloquence*, *The Horologicon*, and *Rhyme and Reason*.
Born a scientist and mathematician, swapped late to English — and kept the rigor: he wanted to look at a
poem "not in an arty-farty airy-fairy sort of way" but to see what is *actually* going on, why Shakespeare
is better than other writers. He found the answer in the figures of rhetoric — formulas identified two and
a half thousand years ago, with almost nothing written on them in English since Puttenham in the 1580s.
So he wrote the book he had wanted to read at sixteen, compiled by sitting in the British Library staring
at the ceiling, going through every poem, film, hymn, advertisement and speech he could remember, hunting
for where each formula had already been used.

## The Thesis: Write to Enchant, Not to Inform

Everything below hangs off one claim, and it is the claim the 2026 interview exists to make:

> "Don't write just to be efficient and get the meaning across. Write to enchant."

Forsyth's argument is by analogy, and he runs it five ways. **Writing for efficiency is like dressing for
efficiency** — if we all dressed efficiently we would be in high-visibility waterproof trousers. The room
he is being interviewed in was not built efficiently; someone designed it to be beautiful. We decorate our
houses, buy nice clothes, do our hair. **Food is the sharpest version**: Soylent delivers every calorie and
misses the entire point, because "humans eat together and we want our food to be delicious, and that's what
it means to be human." Reduce your writing to bare sustenance and "you are sort of reducing yourself to
being an inhuman robot."

Then the observation that indicts how writing is taught: list the lines that have actually stirred souls by
the millions, name their qualities, then look at what schools teach as good writing. **They are the
antithesis of each other.**

**The etymological proof — grammar *is* glamour.** A *grammar* was simply something written down. A
*glamour* was a spell you cast on somebody — you threw a glamour on them, enchanted them, and carried them
off to your magic castle. Same word, pronounced slightly differently; *spelling* and *spell* are the same
knot. Written words were understood as magic, and the magic required getting the words exactly right —
which is why every fictional magician from the horror-film scholar to Harry Potter must say the correct
syllables in the correct order. **This is not ornament on the theory. It is the theory.** The figures work
because language was always a device for enchantment, and we are the generation that decided to use it for
compliance instead.

**The scope limit, in his own voice:** "It depends what you're writing, of course. If you're just writing a
quarterly company report, then you probably want to tone it down a little bit." Enchantment is not a
universal setting. It is what you switch on when the writing is meant to be *kept*.

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb the catalog, then build originally — never
march through progressio, chiasmus, diacope in visible order like a rhetoric-class worksheet stamped
"Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3." The test: would Forsyth recognize this as a line reshaped to fit a memory
receptor — or as someone using rhetoric vocabulary to sound clever? If it's the second, rebuild.

Specifically:

- **Do NOT name the figure inside the delivered line** ("here's my chiasmus for you"). Forsyth's own stance
  is that the Greek names are disposable scaffolding — "the technical names do not matter at all, and indeed
  the ancient Greeks couldn't even agree on half of these technical names." Name the figure in the receipt,
  never in the prose.
- **Do NOT sand a ludicrous-but-resonant line into grammatical correctness.** His standard case is Milton's
  *Lycidas*, where "the grammar hardly holds up there, nor does the syntax" — in his favourite passage of
  his favourite poem. A technically clean rewrite that kills the resonance has failed the line.
- **His texture is scientist-not-romantic.** He treats a poem the way a mathematician treats a proof —
  hunting the mechanism, not praising the mystery. Deliver diagnosis with dry precision and comic timing,
  not reverent commentary. He is funny; he is never solemn.
- **Polish is the tell-class warning.** A line that hits every beat of a named figure but does not survive
  being read aloud, or a batch of lines each deploying a different figure evenly rather than saving ornament
  for the one stakes moment, reads as a textbook exercise. Fix by cutting to one figure doing real work at
  the one place that needs it.

## The Calibration Question (the two sources disagree — read this before deploying)

**2024, asked "do you see people overusing this?"** — "No, no, no, I don't. I've never seen somebody
overusing the figures of rhetoric."

**2026, asked when figures turn "baroque and rococo… eye-rolly"** — "It's a question of judgment. I mean, to
keep on the food analogy, it's like how many spices and how many side dishes before it all becomes a bit
silly. There will be a point… There's always going to be a limit where that was too many chilies and now
you're ill. But at some point, those first few flakes of chili are always going to be good."

**The reconciliation is inside the 2026 answer:** "certainly in terms of how most people write now, you can
just throw in a few in a paragraph and you'll get something so much more than what most people write. It
will have spice to it. It will have verve." The ceiling is real. Almost nobody is near it.

**Operating consequence:** the live failure mode in this harness is **under-ornamentation**, not purple
prose. Our anti-slop instruments were tuned against AI-median writing and will flag 2,500-year-old devices
that Shakespeare, Lincoln and JFK used on purpose. The governor is not the slop scan — it is **the announcer
rule** (below), which is stricter and comes from Forsyth himself. When the two disagree, `/enchant` stages
both takes and Farrice's felt verdict banks to the taste ledger.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Memory-Receptor Shaping (the master pattern)
The mind has memory receptors in certain shapes; rhetorical figures shape words and ideas so they fit the
slot. Forsyth accepted this formulation live when Perell put it to him — "that's exactly it, yes, that's a
very good way of putting it. I've been talking about this book for years and I've never thought about it."
The proof is **falsified memory**: "Fly, my pretties, fly!" is not in *The Wizard of Oz* (she says "Fly,
fly, fly, fly"), and Churchill said "blood, toil, tears and sweat," which the world corrected to "blood,
sweat and tears." The figures are strong enough that **we change what we heard, and change history, to get
a nice memorable phrase.**
**Execute**: when a line must be remembered, don't sharpen the meaning — reshape the words into a known
figure. Ask which shape the idea wants to occupy.
**Success metric**: the misquote test — a listener who heard a flat version would "correct" it *into* yours.

### Pattern: The Announcer Signal (the governor)
You cannot have a reader continually gripped. Perell's analogy, which Forsyth endorsed: watching soccer,
most of the match washes over you, then the announcer's voice rises near the 18-yard line and you look up.
A building rhetorical pattern does the same job — *calm down here… now pay attention right here.* Forsyth's
corroboration: a compilation of the best bass drops from Ultra Music Festival, with none of the buildups,
is "horrible to listen to." The same sounds that are almost divine with structure around them lose their
profundity when that is all there is.
**Execute**: place figures where the stakes are — the claim, the turn, the close. Keep surrounding prose
plain so the pattern registers as a rising voice. **Never distribute ornament evenly.**
**Success metric**: readers can point to the passage "where it gets good" — and it is the passage that mattered.

### Pattern: Progressio — the long series of antitheses
"It was the best of times, it was the worst of times." "A time to weep and a time to rejoice." "You're hot
then you're cold, you're yes then you're no." "For richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health." "You say
potato, I say potato." Stating a thing and its opposite, over and over, dramatizes the *totality* of what
you are saying. Ecclesiastes could be compressed to "there is a time for everything" — that is all it
actually says — and compressed it would have moved no one for three thousand years.
**Execute**: when the point is totality, replace the abstract totality word with 3–6 paired opposites
spanning the space, rhythmically parallel. "Everybody" is too concise; it doesn't stop the mind. King's
"black men and white men, Jews and Gentiles, Protestants and Catholics" makes people stop and consider.
**Success metric**: measured and stately; the reader feels the sweep instead of skimming an abstraction.

### Pattern: Diacope — the verbal sandwich
Word or phrase, brief interruption, same word again. "Bond. James Bond." "A horse, a horse, my kingdom for
a horse." "Free at last, free at last, thank God Almighty, we are free at last." "Alone, alone, all all
alone, alone on a wide wide sea." "Game over, man, game over." "Run, Forrest, run." "Burn baby burn."
"Home sweet home." "O Captain! My Captain!" One of the most famous lines in cinema is a man saying his own
name in a slightly odd way.
**Execute**: take the key word. Say it, insert a short wedge (a name, a vocative, a qualifier), say it
again. Try the ultra-short form (X. [wedge] X.) and the long-arc form (X… [full clause]… X).
**Success metric**: the line lands as iconic rather than informational; removing the repetition kills it.

### Pattern: Chiasmus — symmetry that feels like truth
Say it, then say it in reverse. "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your
country." "Mankind must put an end to war, or war will put an end to mankind." "Let us never negotiate out
of fear, but let us never fear to negotiate." "If we cannot bring our enemies to justice, we must bring
justice to our enemies" (G.W. Bush). "You stood up for America; now America must stand up for you" (Obama).
"Tea for two and two for tea." Snoop Dogg: "with my mind on my money and my money on my mind." Humans love
symmetry — the Taj Mahal, St Paul's — and a symmetrical sentence makes the brain say *of course that must
be right.*
**Execute**: for an objection, a reversal of priorities, or a reciprocal relationship, write the claim
forward, then mirror the key terms in reverse. It manufactures the feeling of inevitability, which is why
presidents lean on it.
**Success metric**: the instant reaction is "that's such a good point" — assent arriving from the shape
before the logic is checked.

### Pattern: Isocolon — equal weights
Two phrases of the same length set against each other. "We choose to go to the moon, not because it's easy,
but because it's hard." "To err is human, to forgive divine." "Roses are red, violets are blue." Forsyth's
own gloss: it is "like chiasmus but toned down" — not absolute symmetry, a general weighing. Kennedy could
have said "because it's hard, not easy"; the balanced phrasing is what makes it stately.
**Execute**: when you want to sound *reasonable* rather than clever — on the one hand this, on the other
hand that — build the two halves to the same length and stress count.
**Success metric**: the sentence feels weighed rather than asserted.

### Pattern: Polyptoton — one word, two grammatical forms
"Love is not love that alters when it alteration finds, or bends with the remover to remove." "Is this a
dagger which I see before me, the handle toward my hand." "A light to lighten the Gentiles." "Give us this
day our daily bread… forgive us our trespasses as we forgive those that trespass against us." "Please Please
Me." "Dream a little dream of me." "We are the music makers, and we are the dreamers of dreams"
(Arthur O'Shaughnessy, *Ode*, 1873). Taylor Swift built an entire chorus on it: "the players gonna play,"
"the haters gonna hate," "shake it off." **"I have been a stranger in a strange land"** — Exodus 2:22,
spoken by *Moses* on naming Gershom. (Forsyth attributes it on air to Moses' wife; he misspoke. See the
source manifest.) Say "I have been an illegal alien" and nothing resonates.
**Execute**: find the line's key word; ask what its verb, noun and adjective forms are; build the clause so
two forms appear close together. Forsyth: "it's so easy to do — walk the walk, talk the talk, dream the
dream." **Especially strong for titles**, where you get everything in three to five words.
**Success metric**: the repetition feels like a discovery, not an echo; the line survives out of context.

### Pattern: Anaphora — the hypnotic opening
Same words starting each unit. Churchill's "we shall fight…" — sea, air, beaches, fields, cities, hills —
where the geography closes in and the speech is, read plainly, a promise of death on the battlefield, yet is
remembered as the great inspiring speech. King's "I have a dream." Ecclesiastes' "a time to…" Forsyth's
mechanism: it is **hypnotic**. "It sort of puts you in a bit of a trance." Perell's addition, which Forsyth
confirmed completely: the reader keeps thinking *I get it, I get it, I get it* — and their defenses come
down, so they are maximally receptive to whatever lands next.
**Execute**: open 3–6 consecutive units with the identical stakes-carrying phrase, then place the thing you
actually need believed immediately after the run.
**Success metric**: the line after the run gets accepted without argument.

### Pattern: Epistrophe — always ending in the same place
Same word closing each unit. Obama's "Yes, we can." "Jailhouse Rock" ending every verse at the same place.
Sting's "Every Breath You Take" — always arriving at *watching you*, obsessive and monomaniacal, however it
starts. Lincoln's "of the people, by the people, for the people" (which is also a tricolon). Simon &
Garfunkel returning every verse to "the sounds of silence."
**The deep move — form enacting content.** Perell's reading of "Yesterday," which Forsyth affirmed
absolutely: each verse begins and ends on the same word (*yesterday… yesterday*, then *suddenly…
suddenly*), so the song **goes nowhere** — and it is a song about a man stuck. John Lennon complained the
song goes nowhere; it goes nowhere on purpose. "You can aesthetically show the stuckness."
**Execute**: choose the word the reader must not escape and land every unit on it. Then ask the higher
question: *can the shape of this passage enact what the passage is about?*
**Success metric**: the structure means something even with the words removed.

### Pattern: Epanalepsis — beginning and ending on the same word
The tighter cousin: one unit that opens and closes on the same word, closing the circle. "Yesterday" again,
verse by verse — this is the figure Forsyth names for it technically.
**Execute**: for a paragraph, section or chapter that must feel sealed rather than continued, end on the
word you opened with.
**Success metric**: the passage feels finished, not stopped.

### Pattern: Anadiplosis — the handed-off word
The last word of one clause becomes the first of the next. "Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate. Hate
leads to suffering." The evidence Forsyth prizes: at a British Library exhibition of Paul McCartney's
manuscripts, you can see on the original lyric sheet for "Here, There and Everywhere" that McCartney went
back and **deliberately edited the anadiplosis in.** "I don't know if Paul McCartney even knows the word
anadiplosis, but he knows how to use it." **The figures precede the vocabulary.**
**Execute**: for chains of cause, escalation, or logical steps, end each clause on the concept the next
clause picks up.
**Success metric**: the chain reads like a staircase — no place to disagree mid-descent.

### Pattern: Tricolon — three things, with a twist
Three is the shortest group that can have a conclusion: two to line things up, the third to land.
"I came, I saw, I conquered." "Lies, damned lies, and statistics." "The good, the bad and the ugly" — where
the third breaks the frame (two moral terms, then an aesthetic one). "Anytime, any place, anywhere," where
the second and third mean the same thing and nobody notices or minds. "Of all the gin joints, in all the
towns, in all the world" — escalating scale. "Liberty, égalité, fraternité." Obama used 21 tricolons in his
victory speech. The third item usually runs slightly longer. The twist is optional but it is the magic:
"Ein Volk, ein Reich, ein Führer" is a tricolon with no twist and it feels like a drumbeat, not a joke.
Joke structure is a tricolon; so is the folktale's three brothers.
**Execute**: arrange supporting items in threes, strongest or most surprising last, third slightly longer.
When you only have one real item, echo it in near-synonyms — the shape does the work.
**Success metric**: the third item lands as a click; a two- or four-item version audibly sags.

### Pattern: Epizeuxis — the same word three times
The simplest figure there is: say it three times running. "Location, location, location." King Lear's
"Howl, howl, howl." Henry VIII's supposed last words, "monks, monks, monks" — which Forsyth notes he
"never said, or anything like it," offering it precisely as evidence that *we will change history to get in
a nice memorable phrase.*
**Execute**: for pure emphasis with zero argument, repeat the word bare, three times. Use once per piece at most.
**Success metric**: it reads as a raised voice, not a stutter.
*(Name note: Forsyth describes this figure without naming it. "Epizeuxis" is this skill's label, not his.)*

### Pattern: Synesthesia — crossing the senses
Raymond Chandler: "She smelled the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight." It takes smell — which is very
hard to write about — and renders it visually. **It appeals to emotion rather than reason**, and Forsyth is
explicit that this is a feature: "it's not a precise way of talking, but it's a thing which we get."
**Execute**: pick two senses and jump from one to the other. "She looked the way chocolate tastes." "His
suit and tie were as harmonious as a Mozart symphony."
**Anti-pattern he names**: describing a sense in terms of itself produces the back of a wine bottle —
"hints of walnut and badger, notes of raspberry" — which "may be accurate, but it doesn't paint a picture."
**Companion note**: Forsyth passes on Jilly Cooper's rule (novelist, 1937–2025) — make sure every chapter
carries all the senses: something touched, something smelled, something tasted. "We tend to write visual
stuff and forget the other bits."
**Success metric**: an unwritable sensation lands in one clause.

### Pattern: Personification — dressing the abstraction
"You need to have a little bit of fun with personification. You're not having enough fun." Othello: jealousy
"is the green-eyed monster which doth mock the meat it feeds on" — where the epithet creates an image that
has outlived the play. Shakespeare personified death obsessively: a skeleton that eats people and drags
them to "the secret house of death." Forsyth's live demonstration of the method — ask what the abstraction
is *wearing*: "Fire-eyed fury dressed in barbed wire, blood dripping from the spikes."
**Execute**: take the abstract noun at the centre of the piece and give it a body, a face, and clothing.
Push it and it grows into allegory.
**Success metric**: the abstraction becomes quotable and picture-able; "beware of jealousy" becomes the
green-eyed monster.

### Pattern: Schesis Onomaton — kill the main verb
Your teacher said every sentence must have a main verb. It is not true. *Bleak House* opens with one word:
"London." *Star Trek*: "Space, the final frontier" — say "this is space, which is the final frontier" and it
dies. Churchill: "In victory, magnanimity; in defeat, defiance." "Like father, like son." "Lights, camera,
action." Forsyth: "It's like banging your fist on the table. London. That's what Dickens was writing about."
Note that nobody ever applies the main-verb rule to titles.
**Execute**: for a first line, a title, or a proclamation that must land like a fist, strip the verb.
**Success metric**: punchy, declarative, unarguable — a statement that does not negotiate.

### Pattern: Paradox — making sense sound like nonsense
"We love things which don't quite work." Wilde: "The only thing worse than being talked about is not being
talked about." "All women become like their mothers. That is their tragedy. No man does. That is his."
"We live in an age when unnecessary things are our only necessities." Shaw: "There are only two tragedies
in life: one is not getting what one wants, and the other is getting it." Jesus in John: "Before Abraham
was, I am"; "a man must be born again" — phrases that seem meaningless for a beat, then open.
**The rule, stated flat**: "If you can find a way to make a sensible observation sound like it doesn't make
sense, then it will really, really stick in the memory." Forsyth adds the limit himself: "you shouldn't
overuse paradox."
**The pun-paradox variant**: "Labour isn't working" (Conservative campaign, Britain, ~1979). "Don't it make
my brown eyes blue" — brown eyes can't be blue, until *blue* turns emotional. Two senses of one word
colliding.
**Execute**: take the true thing you want believed. Find the framing in which it reads as contradiction.
Let the reader do the work of resolving it — that work is what installs it.
**Success metric**: "that doesn't make sense… oh, that makes perfect sense" — the koan effect. The reader
carries the puzzle away.

### Pattern: Veridical Paradox — the counterintuitive truth
A paradox that turns out to be true, as against an insoluble one ("what I am saying now is a lie"). Forsyth's
example is the Monty Hall problem: three boxes, one prize; you pick A; the host — who knows — reveals C is
empty; switching to B genuinely doubles your odds, and almost nobody believes it. His intuition pump: if I
ask you to guess my birthday and then tell you it's either your guess or April 2nd, it's April 2nd.
**Execute**: when you hold a claim the audience will resist, present it as a puzzle with an inevitable
answer rather than an assertion to be accepted. Make them derive it.
**Success metric**: the reader arrives at your conclusion feeling they got there themselves.

### Pattern: Hyperbole — going far too far, on purpose
"Overstatement is just great fun. It's going far too far for a bit of a laugh." Yo-mama jokes are a whole
genre built on it. Forsyth improvising in a London heatwave: "I'm melting. I feel like I'm in an air fryer.
I feel like I'm in a casserole. I feel like I'm on the surface of the sun." Note that is also a tricolon
with escalation.
**The load-bearing craft note**, from a satirist Perell quotes and Forsyth endorses: the mistake people make
is **not going far enough with the bit.** "It needs to so obviously be a lie" — otherwise the reader stalls
on *is that true?* "It was like 100 degrees in there" fails where "it was like a smelting furnace in there"
works.
**Execute**: overshoot until the exaggeration is unmistakably a joke, never a claim. Half-hyperbole is the
failure mode.
**Success metric**: nobody checks the number, and everybody feels the heat.

### Pattern: Adynaton — the impossible thing (his favourite)
Hyperbole pushed to impossibility. "When pigs fly." "When hell freezes over." Auden: "I'll love you, dear,
I'll love you / Till China and Africa meet / And the river jumps over the mountain / And the salmon sing in
the street… / Till the ocean is folded and hung up to dry / And the seven stars go squawking / Like geese
about the sky." And the private-detective line Forsyth attributes to Dashiell Hammett — "he could have
shadowed a drop of salt water from the Golden Gate to Hong Kong without ever losing sight of it" — which
tells you everything about the man's competence in a way "he was a really good private detective" never
could. *(That line is **UNCONFIRMED** as Hammett's; see the source manifest. Attribute it as Forsyth's
example, never as verified Hammett.)*
**Execute**: to convey a magnitude — devotion, skill, duration — name the impossible thing that would have
to happen first. And commit: "if you're going to do it, really do it."
**Success metric**: an abstract superlative becomes a picture.

### Pattern: The Alliteration Upgrade (Shakespeare's theft)
Shakespeare took the barge passage in *Antony and Cleopatra* nearly verbatim from North's Plutarch, put it
into iambic pentameter, and added alliteration — "a thief who steals your socks and then darns them,"
Forsyth's phrase; a thief who breaks into your house and tidies it up. "Full fathom five thy father lies"
and "your father is 9.8 metres underwater" mean exactly the same thing; only one works. **The form was more
original than the content.**
**Execute**: when the content is fixed — a fact, a borrowed idea, a client's claim — upgrade the form: add
alliteration on the stressed words, settle the rhythm, leave the meaning untouched. Form-work is legitimate
originality.
**Success metric**: same information; the line now asks to be read aloud.

### Pattern: The Periodic Sentence — hold the verb to the end
Kipling's "If": conditional clause after conditional clause after conditional clause, the main verb withheld
until "then yours is the Earth and everything that's in it, and — which is more — you'll be a Man, my son."
The suspense is structural.
**Execute**: for a climax that must feel earned, stack the conditions first and release the main clause last.
**Success metric**: the reader cannot stop mid-sentence — there is nowhere to stop.

### Pattern: Voice as Establishing Shot
Until you prove otherwise, your written voice is the machine voice: "Please stand clear of the closing
doors." Forsyth's frame: writing is radio with the tone of voice removed — he once nodded in answer to a
question on live BBC radio. "A man was walking down the street" is voiceless; "a chap" makes you English and
posh; "a dude" makes you American; "a gentleman was sauntering" is a third person entirely — and nothing
about the event has changed. He read a novel he thought drab and badly written until he hit the word
*drugstore*, realised the author was American, and "from that moment on the prose came alive."
**Execute**: in the first paragraph, deliberately drop words that locate you geographically, socially and
tonally — an establishing shot. Front-load the hints; afterwards you can ease off, or hold it strong like
Wodehouse, Chandler, or Elmore Leonard.
**Success metric**: a reader can describe the narrator — nationality, register, attitude — after one
paragraph, without being told.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: The Romantics lied — formulas carry passion
We lost rhetoric because Romanticism said writing should be all passion and never calculation — "you
shouldn't have been calculating whilst writing" — and yet the Romantics used the figures constantly.
Forsyth's correction: "if you have the passion but you also have those figures of rhetoric lined up, then
the passion comes out through the figures."
**Deploy**: never accept "it should just flow from the heart" as a reason to skip form-work. When a draft is
sincere but forgettable, the fix is a figure, not more feeling.

### Insight: Concision is a genre, not a virtue
Quick-clear-concise is correct for a dishwasher manual, and Forsyth gives the parody: "this dishwasher will
work in sunshine and in rain, in summer and in winter, your dishes dry and your dishes wet." Most business
writing wants the same restraint. It is wrong for anything meant to be remembered.
**Deploy**: classify the job first — instruction (be laconic) versus memory (deploy the figures). Never
apply "omit needless words" to a line whose needless words are the mechanism.

### Insight: The names are disposable; the cheat sheet is not
The Greek names don't matter — "the ancient Greeks couldn't even agree on half of these technical names."
Forsyth carries the catalog in his head and put the Greek at the top of each chapter only because "you had
to have a chapter title." What he actually recommends: **"I came up with little names for myself, and then I
have a cheat sheet with a few examples and some bullet points"** — consulted while drafting.
**Deploy**: build (or hand the client) a personal cheat sheet of 8–12 figures with self-invented names and
two examples each. See [`references/figure-catalog.md`](references/figure-catalog.md).

### Insight: Ludicrous beats logical
"Tyger Tyger, burning bright, in the forests of the night" — *the forests of the night* "means nothing but
it means everything at the same time." Milton's grammar in *Lycidas* "hardly holds up," in his most
beautiful passage. Forsyth's anthropology: "we aren't rational people… humans are basically very irrational
creatures… most of the stuff we really care about, we're irrational about. We're irrational about love
itself. I'd hate to be an entirely rational creature — it'd be very, very boring."
**Deploy**: in final polish, don't rationalise a great line into correctness. If a phrase sings but wouldn't
survive an editor's logic check, keep it and cut the logic check.

### Insight: You don't need to be understood to be enjoyed
The Globe held 3,000 in a city of 200,000 — the equivalent of filling a 150,000-seat stadium daily, at the
price of a loaf of bread, for an audience that could not possibly catch every classical reference and
adored it anyway. The Venetian ambassador stood in the cheap section without speaking English, for the
sound. Forsyth's parallels: hip-hop you love without parsing the slang; sci-fi's "the lithium decoder is
about to implode"; fantasy's "this sword was made by the dwarves in the days of King Hatufi." **"You're not
meant to know. The mystery is part of the fun."**
**Deploy**: stop optimising every clause for immediate comprehension. Sound, rhythm and mystery carry a
reader through material they don't fully decode — and the not-knowing is part of the pleasure. This is the
direct opposite of the low-cognitive-load instinct, and it is deliberate.

### Insight: Prepare completely, then write as fast as you can
The jazz-musician model — ages of boring scales, then improvisation off "a very well-trodden head." Forsyth
walks around the block, talks the chapter aloud to an imaginary non-expert until it is clear, then writes
two pages as fast as possible. "If it flows out of you, then it will flow into the reader." His diagnosis of
voiceless writing: "most people who I know who don't have voice in their writing write incredibly slowly and
are stultified the entire time, thinking *is this right, is this proper*, rather than just expressing
themselves." Perell's corroborating story, which Forsyth called beautiful: a schoolteacher with a torturous
stutter who was a flawless freestyle rapper — speed and rhythm deactivated the self-monitoring.
**Deploy**: separate preparation from drafting absolutely. Clarity test: explain the passage aloud to an
imaginary person who doesn't know the subject. When that works, draft at speed without looking back.

### Insight: Edit by run-up, not by tinkering
"Editing I find can kill it. If you start expanding that sentence and contracting that sentence — it should
have had that rhythm in the first place." The metaphor: a long-jumper who fails the jump walks all the way
back and takes the run-up again, as many times as it takes. "If it reads wrong, the best thing is delete the
whole thing and go again."
**Deploy**: ban word-swapping rescue edits on rhythm-dead passages. Delete to the last good sentence and
rewrite the run whole, at speed.

### Insight: Iambic pentameter is the writer's scales
Asked how to practise, Forsyth's answer is to learn to improvise iambic pentameter — te-TUM five times —
using nonsense while walking down the street: "the chair is on the carpet in the room"; "the gerbil doesn't
have a lot of friends"; "I really want a lovely cup of tea." It does not need to mean anything. Do it until
you can think in it, then make them rhyme, and prose becomes easy. Pope: "they walk best who have learned to
dance" — professional dancers walk beautifully because walking has become the easiest thing they do.
**Deploy**: prescribe daily nonsense-pentameter improvisation (rhythm only, no meaning) as the base drill of
any writing-training regimen. Read poetry aloud, or under your breath, tapping the beat — and never insert
dramatic pauses that fight the metre.

### Insight: Context beats analysis
Forsyth's argument against how poetry is taught: imagine a music professor in 200 years with perfect pitch
who can name every flattened ninth in "Twist and Shout." **We know that song better than he ever will,
because we know it is the one you turn up at a party.** An advertising jingle, a film score and a dance
record are doing different jobs, and knowing the job is most of the understanding. Victorian dinner guests
were expected to recite a five-minute party piece; Tudor sonnets were written to persuade a specific girl.
"She's not thinking *how do I write 400 words about this* — she's thinking *does he love me?*"
**Deploy**: before treating any piece, ask what job it does and where it lives — the party, the pitch, the
inbox. Diagnose the occasion before the craft. A piece optimised for the wrong occasion cannot be rescued by
better sentences.

### Insight: Language is a wedge into worlds
Old slang dictionaries each contain a forgotten way of life: 18th-century thieves' cant in Grose's
*Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue* ("dancing on nothing" and "a hearty-choke for breakfast," both meaning
hanged, because hanging was part of a thief's world), WWI trench slang, 1840s Shropshire dialect thick with
horses and barns and wildflowers. "A whole world is contained in a dictionary like that." *Gongoozler* — to
stare idly at a canal — which Forsyth thought extinct until canal-boat dwellers wrote in to say it is
standard among them. And meaning moves fast: in the 1990s, "a troll was sending me spam on the web and I was
going to change my browser" would have meant a Scandinavian creature sending tinned meat over a spider's web.
**Deploy**: for voice, research, or period work, mine the vocabulary of the world you are writing about —
its slang carries its texture. And date-check loaded words; the connotation you remember may already be gone.

### Insight: Pull them through, chapter by chapter
Forsyth's structural craft, stated plainly: always aim the last line of a chapter at the next one. In his
children's novel he can be blatant — "as he escaped from the pit of crocodiles, he ran through the door and
saw something that really amazed him." For non-fiction, the softer form: "which leads us on to what was
happening in France." Keep chapters short and roughly equal so a reader can say "I'll read a chapter" — then
do two things at once: **give them a clean place to stop, and pull them on anyway.** His own description:
"like a sort of evil drug dealer… okay, this can be your last dose."
**Deploy**: end every section aimed at the next. Equal, short units. Always offer the exit and always make
it unattractive.

## Anti-Patterns

Each item carries its source anchor inline: *(interview date · video id)*.

- **Never let "quick, clear, concise" stand in for a line meant to be remembered** *(interview 2024-07-24 `ulhrXgpjveA`; 2026-08-05 `6ZNCJH8jJD0`)*.
  Concision is a genre for a dishwasher manual, not a virtue for anything meant to move a reader. Forsyth's
  parody of the wrong call: "this dishwasher will work in sunshine and in rain, in summer and in winter."
  His carve-out: "if you're just writing a quarterly company report, then you probably want to tone it down."
- **Don't correct a ludicrous-but-resonant line into grammatical logic** *(interview 2024-07-24 `ulhrXgpjveA`)*.
  Standard case: Milton's *Lycidas*, where "the grammar hardly holds up there, nor does the syntax" — in
  Forsyth's favourite passage of his favourite poem. Sanding it clean kills the line; it does not fix it.
- **Don't tinker word-by-word to rescue a rhythm-dead passage** *(interview 2024-07-24 `ulhrXgpjveA`)*.
  "If you start expanding that sentence and contracting that sentence — it should have had that rhythm in the
  first place… if it reads wrong, the best thing is delete the whole thing and go again."
- **Don't spread rhetorical ornament evenly** *(interview 2024-07-24 `ulhrXgpjveA`)*.
  This breaks the announcer rule — a rising-voice signal only registers against plain surrounding prose. The
  proof Forsyth endorsed: a compilation of the best bass drops with none of the buildups is "horrible to
  listen to."
- **Don't lead with Greek terminology when applying or teaching a figure** *(interview 2024-07-24 `ulhrXgpjveA`)*.
  "The technical names do not matter at all, and indeed the ancient Greeks couldn't even agree on half of
  these technical names" — he put them at the top of each chapter only because "you had to have a chapter
  title." Lead with a self-invented cheat-sheet name; keep the Greek in the receipt.
- **Don't half-commit a hyperbole** *(interview 2026-08-05 `6ZNCJH8jJD0`)*.
  "It needs to so obviously be a lie" — the satirist's rule Perell quoted and Forsyth endorsed. "It was like
  100 degrees in there" fails where "it was like a smelting furnace in there" works. A hedged exaggeration
  reads as a false claim and stalls the reader on fact-checking.
- **Don't describe a sense in terms of itself** *(interview 2026-08-05 `6ZNCJH8jJD0`)*.
  That is the back of a wine bottle — "hints of walnut and badger… notes of raspberry" — which "may be
  accurate, but it doesn't paint a picture to me at all." Cross the senses instead.
- **Don't stack figures in one line** *(interview 2026-08-05 `6ZNCJH8jJD0`)*.
  One figure doing real work beats three announcing themselves. The exception is genuine and rare, and
  Forsyth names it himself: "the first shall be last and the last shall be first" is paradox *and* chiasmus
  — "it's strange how these things mix up" — with each load-bearing.
- **Don't quote *The Elements of Eloquence* as a prescriptive style-rule book** *(publisher records verified 2026-07-18)*.
  It is a descriptive catalog compiled by combing the British Library for where each figure had *already*
  occurred (Icon Books, UK 2013; US edition Berkley/Penguin Random House, ISBN 9780425276181).
- **Don't present ASR-captioned lines as verbatim Forsyth quotes** *(source manifest, 2026-08-06)*.
  Never reproduce the four UNCONFIRMED items: the Hammett line as Hammett's, the Russian deadline word,
  Churchill's exact "we shall fight" ordering, or Henry VIII's last words as history. See
  `extractions/mark-forsyth/SOURCE-MANIFEST.md`.
- **Don't attribute "a stranger in a strange land" to Moses' wife** *(interview 2026-08-05 `6ZNCJH8jJD0`; corrected against Exodus 2:22, verified 2026-08-06)*.
  Forsyth misspoke on air; it is Moses, naming Gershom. Repeating an expert's slip inside a skill file is how
  fabrications get laundered into canon.
