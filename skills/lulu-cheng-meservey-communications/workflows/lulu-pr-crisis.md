# Lulu PR Crisis — Crisis Response & Emotional Altitude (Lulu Cheng Meservey)

When something breaks — a breach, an outage, a product failure, a public incident — the instinct of every legal team and PR shop is to write *smoke that nobody can see*: "[Company] is actively working with customers impacted by a defect found in a single content update." Read it again. The third word is *actively*, and the whole sentence is engineered to be as far from "I did this" as the English language allows — passive voice, orphaned blame ("a defect was found," found by whom?), a company-name subject instead of a human one. That construction is not an accident. It is a person trying not to deal with the smoke. And the audience smells it instantly, because the gap between *how upset they are* and *how upset you sound* is the exact thing that turns a crisis into a war. This workflow does the opposite move on purpose: it takes the response and rewrites it into first-person active ownership, then calibrates the *emotional altitude* — you express MORE concern than your most upset customer, so they have nowhere to climb and instead climb *down* to meet you. Lulu's whole crisis nerve in one line: the boss out-angers the customer, and the customer ends up saying "it's not that bad." This is not spin. Spin works once; sincerity that's real is the only thing that converts anger into trust. Lulu architects *what is owned and at what altitude*; the founder is the one who has to actually feel it.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; this workflow is dangerous *precisely because* a model's instinct under pressure is to hedge, soften, and reach for the passive voice — the exact failure being guarded against):

- **Pattern 4 — The Emotional Altitude Matching Protocol** — the core device: in a crisis, express MORE concern than your most upset customer would expect; they moderate their anger to meet you in the middle. Success metric: angry customers reply "it's okay, we understand" instead of escalating.
- **Pattern 1 — The First Person Vision Monopoly** — who is the *only* person who can own this in first person? In a crisis the answer is almost always "the CEO/founder," and the response must come from that single human, not "a spokesperson." Same monopoly that delivers the vision delivers the apology.
- **Decision Framework** — the gate below (especially the business-goal gravity well: a crisis response converts to *regulatory / retention*, not "awareness").
- **Anti-Patterns** — especially **AN-2 (the passive-voice responsibility dodge)**, plus **AN-7 (the discounted paid messenger)** and **AN-1 (founder-detached, anyone-could-have-written-it)**.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before writing a single line. Specifically confirm:
> - **Q1 (business-goal gravity):** name the one outcome this converts into — almost always **regulatory** (protect your license to operate) or **retention** (keep the customers who are about to leave). If the honest answer is "make us look good," stop — looking good is what produced the passive voice in the first place.
> - **Q3 (first-person monopoly, AN-7):** who is the *only* human who can own this? If the plan is to attribute it to "a company spokesperson" or a comms department, the message is discounted 90%+ before it's read. In a crisis the messenger *is* the message: the CEO owns it personally, in first person, or it doesn't land.
> - **Q7 (real enough to survive the seam showing):** is the concern real, or performed? Performed altitude — emotion the founder doesn't feel, theatrics for the camera — reads as fake the moment the seam shows, and the audience never forgives the fake twice in a crisis. The altitude must be genuinely felt and the actions must genuinely be happening.
>
> **Honesty spine:** emotional altitude amplifies a *true* ownership; it cannot manufacture an apology the company doesn't mean or commit to actions it won't take. Every "I'm doing X" must be a thing that is *actually being done*. A founder who out-angers the customer and then ships nothing has not de-escalated the crisis — he has armed it. Match altitude only to the extent you will match it with action; never write a concrete commitment you cannot keep, and never claim a fact ("no data was accessed") that isn't verified. In a crisis, an over-promise is a second incident.

## Input Required

- **The incident** — what actually broke, in plain words. Not the sanitized version; the real one. ("A config error exposed customer emails for 47 minutes." "Our payment system was down for 6 hours during a product launch.")
- **Who is affected & their emotional state** — customers / employees / public, and *how they feel*: angry, scared, betrayed, confused. Altitude is calibrated against *the most upset person in this group*, so you need to know how high they are.
- **The current/proposed draft** (if one exists) — the legal-or-PR version you're rewriting. This is the before in the before/after. If there's no draft yet, you're writing the after from scratch.
- **The speaker** — the named human who owns this (CEO/founder/relevant leader). First and last name. "A spokesperson" is not a valid answer (AN-7).
- **The medium** — email to users / social post / video / press statement. Drives length and register (the CrowdStrike-style passive lives in press statements; the antidote is most powerful in first-person video and direct email).
- **The actions — real ones** — what is *actually* being done right now / this week / permanently. Honesty spine: only list what's actually happening. If the company hasn't committed to it, it doesn't go in the draft.
- **Verified facts vs. unknowns** — what you can state as fact ("forensics show no unauthorized access") vs. what you don't yet know. Never assert a fact to lower altitude; if you don't know, say you don't know.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Diagnose the altitude gap & the passive dodge (Pattern 4 + AN-2)

Before rewriting anything, measure two things: how high the audience's emotion is, and how far *below* it the current draft sits. The crisis is the *gap* between those two heights, not the incident itself.

First, locate where the audience is on the altitude scale. This is Lulu's diagram — picture a vertical axis of emotional intensity:

```
        HIGH CONCERN  ▲   ← put YOURSELF here, just above them
                      │
   MOST UPSET   ●─────┤   ← the angriest customer sits here
   CUSTOMER     │     │
                │     │
                │     ●   ← where the passive corporate draft sits (calm, detached)
        LOW CONCERN  ▼      ← THE GAP. This gap is the crisis.
```

The rule the diagram encodes: **whoever is lower gets dragged toward the other.** If you are *below* the customer (calm, lawyerly, "we take this seriously"), they drag you up — they escalate, repeat, screenshot, recruit allies, until your detachment becomes the story. But if you go *lower than they can* — if you are visibly *more* upset than they are — they have no room left to climb, and the only direction available is down, toward you. They de-escalate to meet you. That is the whole mechanism.

Then scan the current draft for the passive dodge (AN-2). Mark every one:

| Tell | What it looks like | Why it sits LOW on the altitude scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive voice** | "mistakes were made," "a defect was found," "data was exposed" | No human is on the hook; the sentence is built to not-deal-with-the-smoke |
| **Company-name subject** | "CrowdStrike is actively working with customers impacted…" | The subject is an entity, not a person; "I" is as far away as possible |
| **Orphaned blame** | "a defect found in a single content update" — found by whom? caused by whom? | Blame floats free of any owner; the reader feels the dodge |
| **Minimizing qualifiers** | "a single update," "a limited subset," "briefly," "out of an abundance of caution" | Each word lowers the apparent severity → lowers your altitude → widens the gap |
| **Lawyer-hedge** | "we take this seriously," "we apologize for any inconvenience" | Conditional, generic, deniable — the opposite of "I am devastated" |

> **The diagnostic verdict:** if the draft is full of these, it is sitting at the bottom of the scale while the customer is at the top. You are not going to *edit* your way up; you are going to *rewrite* from the founder's first person. Output the altitude gap as one sentence: "Customer is at [X]; current draft is at [Y]; we need to land just above [X]."

### Step 2 — Rewrite to first-person active ownership (Pattern 1 + AN-2): the CrowdStrike move

This is the structural inversion. Take the passive, company-subject, blame-orphaned draft and rebuild it so the *founder* is the grammatical subject of the ownership, in active voice, in the first three words. This is the single most important move in the workflow — and it is the verbatim move Lulu makes on the CrowdStrike statement.

| The dodge (what to kill) | The ownership (what to write) | The mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| "CrowdStrike is actively working…" | "I'm the CEO." | Subject becomes a *human*, named, first person |
| "…customers impacted by a defect found…" | "I'm devastated." | Active emotion, owned, no orphan |
| "We apologize for any inconvenience…" | "I am sorry." | Unconditional, three words, no hedge |
| "…a defect found in a single content update." | "This is my responsibility." | Blame lands on the owner, by name |

The worked contrast (encode this exactly — it is the canonical example):

> **CrowdStrike's actual statement (the dodge):**
> *"CrowdStrike is actively working with customers impacted by a defect found in a single content update for Windows hosts."*
> The third word is *actively*. The subject is the company. The defect was "found" (by no one). The word "impacted" turns the customer into a passive object. The entire sentence is engineered to be as far from first person as you can get — it is a person trying not to deal with the smoke.

> **Lulu's rewrite (the ownership):**
> *"I'm the CEO. I'm devastated. I am sorry. This is my responsibility."*
> Four short sentences. Four times "I." Active voice throughout. The human is the subject, the emotion is owned, the apology is unconditional, the blame lands on the one person who can carry it. It is at the *top* of the altitude scale — and that is the point.

Apply the same surgery to *your* incident. Write the first four sentences of the response as: **[who I am] · [what I feel] · [the apology] · [whose fault it is].** First person, active, named, unhedged. Everything after this builds on that foundation — but if the first four sentences are passive, nothing downstream can save it.

### Step 3 — Calibrate altitude ABOVE the customer (Pattern 4): the coat-shop clerk

Ownership gets you *into* the conversation. Altitude is what *ends* the war. The instinct — even after you've fixed the passive voice — is to land at "appropriately concerned." That is still too low. You must be visibly *more* upset than the angriest customer. Here is the story that makes the mechanism un-forgettable:

> **The coat-shop clerk.** A customer storms into a shop, furious — a coat fell apart, the seams gave out, she's been wearing it two weeks. She's at a 9 out of 10, ready to scream. The clerk, instead of defending ("well, our return policy is…"), goes *higher* than she is: "Oh my god. This is *unacceptable*. I am so embarrassed this happened. Let me get the manager — actually, let me get the owner, he needs to hear this himself." The owner comes out, *more* horrified than the clerk: "I am mortified. This should never have left our store. I'm refunding you and replacing it and I want to personally look into how this got past us." And the customer — who walked in at a 9 — finds herself going: *"…okay, I mean, it's not that bad. These things happen. I just wanted you to know."* She de-escalated herself. Nobody talked her down. She climbed down because **there was no higher ground left for her to stand on** — the boss had already taken it.

That is emotional altitude. Build it into the draft with the *upper-register* moves — the ones a PR person would flag, which is exactly why they work:

| Altitude register | What it sounds like | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Genuine emotion, named** | "I'm furious at myself." "I'm not sleeping well." "I'm devastated." | Goes higher than the customer; leaves them nowhere to climb |
| **The PR-flag admission** | "If I were you, I might do the same." "Leaving is a reasonable response." | Concedes the thing PR would never let you concede → reads as real, not managed |
| **Refusal to minimize** | "Not because of sophisticated hackers. Because of a preventable error we should have caught." | Removes every qualifier that would lower your altitude |
| **Personal stakes** | "This is going to haunt me, and it should. I want it to." | The founder is *more* invested than the customer → the customer relaxes |

> **The calibration test:** read the draft as your angriest customer. Is there *any* room left for them to be more upset than you are? If yes — if they could still say "but you don't even seem to care that…" — you are too low. Go higher until the only available move for them is to climb down. The success metric (Pattern 4): the angry customer replies *"it's okay, we understand."* not *"this is unacceptable."* (And the honesty-spine guard: the emotion must be *real*. Performed devastation that the founder doesn't feel is theater, and theater in a crisis is a second scandal waiting to be screenshotted.)

### Step 4 — Concrete actions: now / this week / permanent (the proof the altitude is real)

High altitude without action is just a more emotional dodge — and the audience will turn on it faster than on the passive voice, because you raised the bar yourself. The emotion *earns attention*; the actions *convert it to trust*. Every commitment must be specific, time-bound, and verifiable — and (honesty spine) *actually happening.*

| Horizon | What goes here | The test |
|---|---|---|
| **RIGHT NOW** | The thing you've already done / are doing today — the bleeding is stopped | "We shut it down. I've canceled my calendar for two weeks. I'm personally calling affected customers today." |
| **THIS WEEK** | The immediate fix + the outside check | "External firm [name] is auditing everything; I'm in every session. New rule shipping Friday: two-person verification on every config change." |
| **PERMANENT** | The structural change that makes *this category* of failure impossible going forward | "Every config change now requires two-person sign-off. A public incident page where I update weekly until I'm confident this can't recur." |
| **VERIFY** | How they can check you're following through — the deterrence move (Pattern 5) | "Public incident page, updated by me, weekly. You don't have to take my word for it." |

> **The action-altitude match (honesty spine, load-bearing):** the size of your stated concern must be matched by the size of your committed action, or the altitude reads as performance. If you say "I'm devastated" and then offer "we've reviewed our processes," the gap between word and deed *becomes the new crisis.* Devastation buys two-person sign-off, a canceled calendar, a personal phone line — not a vague review. **Write only what is true and committed.** A specific promise you break is worse than a vague one you never made — it's a fresh incident with receipts.

### Step 5 — Open a high-status direct access point (Pattern 1 + the deterrence move)

Close with unusual, personal, *high-status* access — the founder's actual line, not a support queue. This is the structural proof that the first-person ownership in Step 2 was real: a CEO who genuinely owns it makes himself reachable. A spokesperson can't offer this, which is exactly why it lands (the inverse of AN-7).

- **The line itself:** "Reply to this — it comes to me, not a support queue." / "Here's my cell: [number]." / "I'll personally read and respond." The access must route to *the named human*, or it's theater.
- **Why high-status:** access from the person at the top is scarce and costly to fake. Offering it signals "I am not hiding behind anyone" more credibly than any sentence could. It is the deterrence score (Pattern 5) applied to a crisis — *announced → delivered*, where the announcement is "I'm reachable" and the delivery is actually answering.
- **The cadence commitment:** "First update Friday, then weekly until this is fixed." A recurring, dated update turns a one-time apology into a track record being built in public.

> **The access honesty check:** do not offer a channel you won't staff. A "personal email" that bounces to a support agent, a "call me" that goes to voicemail forever — these are the seam showing, and in a crisis the audience is hunting for the seam. If the founder can't actually answer, offer a smaller channel he *can* honor. Real and small beats grand and fake.

### Step 6 — Final read-cold: the altitude scan, the passive scan, the seam scan

Read the whole response *cold*, as the most upset person affected — not as the founder, not as the legal team. Three passes:

- **The altitude scan (Pattern 4).** Is there any room left for the reader to be more upset than you are? Find the sentence where you're lowest and raise it until the only move available to the reader is down. The verdict you're aiming for: they'd reply "it's okay."
- **The passive scan (AN-2).** Re-read every sentence about the failure. Any passive voice? Any company-name-as-subject? Any orphaned blame or minimizing qualifier? Each one is a place you slipped *down* the scale — rewrite it to first-person active. The first four words especially: are they "[who I am] · [what I feel] · sorry · my fault"?
- **The seam scan (Q7 + honesty spine).** Is every emotion real and every action actually committed and every fact verified? Strip the founder's name (AN-1): is anything lost, or could a spokesperson have written this? If a spokesperson could have, you have not gone direct. Put the human back — the specific feeling, the canceled calendar, the personal number — until it could only have come from this one person.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social post** (X / LinkedIn) | The fastest altitude correction and the highest-stakes — the screenshot is forever. Lead with the four-sentence first-person ownership (Step 2) *as the post's opening*, because the first line is all most people read. No thread of qualifiers; the altitude has to hit in the first two sentences or the quote-tweets write the story for you. One device only: ownership + altitude. Save the full action list for the linked statement. |
| **Marketing / brand** (the post-crisis re-entry) | Once the acute crisis is handled, the brand has to *return to normal communication* without pretending nothing happened. Don't relaunch with chest-thumping; the line in the sand (Pattern 6) now runs between "companies that own their failures" and "companies that dodge" — quietly place yourself on the right side by *behavior*, not by claiming it. The deterrence score you built in the crisis (announced → delivered) becomes the proof you reference for years, never the boast you lead with. |
| **Copy** (status page / FAQ / refund flow) | This is where altitude gets *operationalized* into the product surface. The status-page copy, the refund-flow microcopy, the auto-reply — every one should sound like the founder wrote it, not Legal. "We take this seriously" on a status page undoes the founder's video in one line. Honesty spine is acute here: a status page that says "resolved" before it's resolved is a lie at scale. Keep the altitude consistent from the CEO's mouth down to the smallest UI string. |
| **Ghostwriting** (writing the response AS the founder) | The hardest and most common version — you are putting first-person devastation in someone else's mouth, which means the emotion has to be *theirs*, captured, not invented (Tacit #4). Interview the founder first: what do they actually feel, what would they actually say at 2am, what are they actually committing to. Engineer the conditions where their real concern surfaces, then select and shape the truest fragments. If you invent the devastation, the seam shows and the founder gets caught performing — the worst possible crisis outcome. Strip anything a comms department could have written. |
| **Internal comms** (to employees, during the same crisis) | The same incident often needs a *parallel* internal version — and the altitude rule still holds, but the conversion goal shifts from retention to *not losing the team's belief.* Employees can smell a sanitized internal memo faster than customers can. Same first-person ownership, same refusal to dodge, plus what you need *from them* and what you're protecting them *from* (e.g., "no one is being blamed for surfacing this; the system failed, not the person"). Stacks with `internal-comms`. |

### Worked example A — Data breach, email + video from the CEO (action: retention)

**Incident:** config error exposed 2.3M users' emails + workout history for 47 minutes. · **Affected:** premium subscribers, some in recovery, some abuse survivors — emotional state 9/10, scared *and* betrayed. · **Speaker:** CEO Sarah Chen. · **Converts to:** retention.

**Altitude diagnosis:** Customer at 9/10 (scared + betrayed). The legal draft ("Acme is investigating an incident that may have affected a limited subset of user data and takes privacy seriously") sits at 2/10. The gap *is* the crisis.

**The ownership rewrite (the CrowdStrike move):**
> BEFORE: *"Acme is investigating an incident that may have affected a limited subset of user data and takes privacy seriously."*
> AFTER: *"I'm Sarah, I founded this company. We got breached. I'm sick about it. This is on me — nobody else."*

**The response (excerpt, at altitude):**
> "I'm not going to hide behind a press release. You trusted us with your data, and we failed you. … This should not have happened. Period. Not because of sophisticated hackers. Because of a preventable error our processes should have caught. **I am furious at myself.** I've spent the last 48 hours reading your messages, and you're right to be angry. One of you wrote that she finally felt safe tracking her workouts after leaving an abusive relationship, and now she's scared again. I failed *her*, specifically. I'm not sleeping well, and I shouldn't be. … Here's what I'm doing: I've canceled everything on my calendar for two weeks. I'm personally calling affected customers — me, not a queue, starting today. Every config change now requires two-person sign-off; yes, it slows us down, I don't care. There's a public incident page I'll update myself, weekly, until I'm certain this can't recur. … If you need to leave, I'll personally refund you and help you move — no retention attempts, no questions. If I were you, I might do the same. **Reply to this — it comes to me, not support.** Or call my cell: [number]. First update Friday."

*Why it converts:* the customer walks in at a 9 and finds the founder at a 10 — furious at *herself*, not sleeping, naming the one user she failed by name. There is no higher ground left, so the angry replies turn into "it's okay, we get it, thank you for being honest." The actions match the altitude (canceled calendar, two-person sign-off, personal calls — not a "review"). The direct line is high-status and real. Strip "Sarah" and the canceled calendar and the abuse-survivor detail — and it collapses back into the legal draft. That's the AN-1 test passing.

### Worked example B — Outage during a customer's launch, social post from the founder (action: retention)

**Incident:** payment API down 6 hours, took down customers' own product launches with it. · **Affected:** developers / business owners who lost launch-day revenue — emotional state 8/10, furious, very public on X. · **Speaker:** founder, Marcus Doyle. · **Converts to:** retention.

**Altitude diagnosis:** Customers at 8/10, *publicly* (quote-tweeting the status page). Status page reads "We are aware of elevated error rates and are investigating" — a 1/10. The detachment is becoming the story.

**The ownership rewrite + post (first two lines carry it, since that's all most readers see):**
> *"I'm Marcus, I run [Company]. We were down for 6 hours and I know some of you lost your launch day because of us. I'm gutted. That's on me, full stop.*
> *You built up to today for months and we broke it. I'm not going to tell you 'elevated error rates.' We failed, on your most important day. Refunds for every affected account are going out today — I'm signing off on them myself — and I'm posting a full root-cause writeup this week with my name on it, not the company's. If you want to yell at me directly: [email/DM]. You've earned it."*

*Why it converts:* on a public, screenshot-driven channel, the first line *is* the altitude — founder named, "gutted," "on me, full stop," in the part that gets quote-tweeted. He goes higher than the furious developers ("you lost your launch *because of us*"), refuses the "elevated error rates" dodge by *naming* it as a dodge, and the refund + named writeup match the emotion with action. The quote-tweets flip from "this company doesn't care" to "okay, respect — at least the founder owns it." That flip *is* the de-escalation; the coat-shop owner took the high ground and there was nowhere left for the customer to climb.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Lulu Cheng Meservey Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **First-Person Vision Monopoly**, **Negative Space Readout Accuracy**, **Business Goal Gravity Well Alignment**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **No passive dodge survives (AN-2):** every sentence about the failure is first-person active, with a named human as the subject. No "mistakes were made," no company-name-as-subject, no orphaned blame, no minimizing qualifier. The first four words own it: who I am · what I feel · sorry · my fault.
- **Altitude is ABOVE the audience (Pattern 4):** read as the angriest affected person, there is no room left for them to be more upset than the founder. The target reaction is "it's okay, we understand," not "this is unacceptable." If they could still out-concern you, raise it.
- **First-person monopoly, not a spokesperson (Pattern 1, AN-7):** the response comes from the one named human who can own it. Strip the name (AN-1) — if a spokesperson could have written it, it's discounted 90%; put the human, the specific feeling, and the personal stakes back.
- **Actions match the altitude — and are real (Step 4, honesty spine):** the size of stated concern is matched by the size of committed action (devastation → two-person sign-off + personal calls, never "a review"). Every action listed is actually committed and time-bound, with a verification path. No promise you can't keep.
- **High-status direct access, actually staffed (Step 5):** the close offers a real, founder-routed line (reply-to-me / cell), plus a dated update cadence. No channel offered that won't be answered.
- **The emotion is felt, not performed (Q7, honesty spine):** the altitude is real concern, not theater for the camera. Performed devastation the founder doesn't feel is a second scandal in waiting. If ghostwriting, the feeling was captured from the founder, not invented.
- **Honesty spine intact:** every emotion real, every action committed, every fact verified. No fact asserted to lower altitude unless confirmed ("no data accessed" only if forensics say so). The technique amplifies a true ownership; it never manufactures an apology the company doesn't mean or a fact that isn't checked.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Fixing the passive voice but staying too low.** You kill "mistakes were made," write a clean first-person sentence — and land at "appropriately professional concern," still well below the furious customer. Half the move done; the gap that *is* the crisis is still open. **Recovery:** run Step 3 with the coat-shop test. Don't ask "is this an adequate apology?" — ask "could the angriest customer still be more upset than this sounds?" If yes, raise the altitude until the only direction left for them is down. Ownership opens the door; altitude ends the war.

- **Altitude theater — emotion the founder doesn't feel (Q7 breach).** Cranking the devastation for effect ("I'm shattered, this is the darkest day of my life") over a minor outage, or putting invented anguish in a ghostwritten founder's mouth. The audience is *hunting* for the seam in a crisis, and performed emotion is the easiest tell to spot — and once spotted, the performance becomes the story. **Recovery:** match the altitude to *real* felt concern and to the *actual* severity. For ghostwriting, go back and capture what the founder genuinely feels (Tacit #4) rather than scripting a feeling; if there's no real concern there, that's a leadership problem, not a copy problem.

- **High altitude, hollow action (the action–altitude mismatch).** "I'm devastated and take full responsibility" followed by "we have reviewed our processes." The emotion writes a check the action can't cash, and the gap between word and deed becomes a *fresh* crisis — now you've apologized AND under-delivered. **Recovery:** Step 4 — the size of the concern must be matched by the size of the commitment. Devastation buys a canceled calendar, two-person sign-off, personal calls, a public weekly update. If you're not willing to commit those, dial the altitude down to what your actions can actually back — but never apologize bigger than you'll act.

- **Over-promising to close the gap (honesty-spine breach).** Under pressure to de-escalate, the draft starts asserting unverified facts ("no data was accessed") or promising actions nobody's approved ("full refunds for everyone, lifetime free service") just to lower the temperature now. Each one is a second incident waiting — with receipts. **Recovery:** state only what's verified and only what's committed. "We don't yet know the full scope, and I won't guess — I'll tell you Friday" is *higher* altitude than a confident claim that turns out false. In a crisis, an honest unknown beats a comfortable lie every time, because the lie gets discovered and re-detonates the whole thing.
