# Hammer-First Outliner

Map the emotional destination of a piece before writing the first word. Thompson: "I always want to know what the ending is... I like to make decisions based on how to maximize the power of the ending." This workflow reverses the conventional outline — you start at the ending and build backward.

## Execution

### Requirements

The user provides **a subject, a topic, or raw material** — notes, a brief, a client description, reporting notes, or an existing draft that needs structural overhaul. This workflow produces an ending-first architectural outline.

### Step 1: Find the Hammer

Before anything else, answer this question: **What is the image, line, or moment that will make the air feel like it got sucked out of the room?**

This is not a summary. This is not a conclusion. The hammer is:
- An image that carries the weight of the entire piece
- A concrete detail that lands with emotional force
- Something specific enough to photograph, but resonant enough to haunt

Thompson found Lou Gehrig's widow dying alone at the end of a Yankee Stadium piece. He found Michael Jordan falling asleep to westerns. The hammer is always a **concrete human moment**, never an abstraction.

**Output**: One sentence describing the hammer. If you can't write it in one sentence, you haven't found it yet.

### Step 2: The Elliptical Test

Test the hammer against Thompson's two rules for endings:

1. **Does it resolve the narrative arc?** The reader must feel the story has arrived somewhere. No spaghetti.
2. **Does it open a door rather than close one?** The best endings are "elliptical" — they resolve the surface question while leaving the deeper one unresolved, "authentic to our lives."

If it only resolves → it's a kicker, not a hammer. Rework.
If it only opens → it's a cliffhanger, not a hammer. Rework.
If it does both → proceed.

### Step 3: Backward Architecture

Now build the outline in reverse. For each section, ask:

**"What does the reader need to know or feel BEFORE the hammer lands for it to hit with maximum force?"**

Work backward from the ending:
- **Pre-hammer section**: What scene, detail, or revelation sets up the hammer without telegraphing it?
- **Mid-piece turning point**: Where does the piece pivot from setup to trajectory? (This is where the second-section verb does its work — see Pattern 7.)
- **Opening**: What specific, concrete scene drops the reader into the world? (Not a thesis. Not context. A scene.)

### Step 4: The Protection Audit

Thompson's phrase: "protecting the hammer." Go through every proposed section and ask:

- Does this section serve the hammer? If not → cut it.
- Does this section telegraph the hammer? If so → rework it so the reader feels the trajectory without seeing the destination.
- Does this section compete with the hammer for emotional attention? If so → reduce its intensity. The hammer must be the loudest moment.

### Step 5: The Feel Check

Read the outline from beginning to end. Does it feel **inevitable** — like every section had to be there, in that order? Or does it feel **assembled** — like parts could be rearranged without consequence?

If assembled → something in your architecture is decorative, not structural. Find it and cut it.

## Output Requirements

**Deliver**:
1. **The Hammer** — one sentence, the emotional ending
2. **Elliptical Test results** — how the hammer resolves and opens simultaneously
3. **Backward Outline** — sections in reading order (not the order you built them), each with a one-line description of what it accomplishes structurally
4. **Protection Audit** — any sections flagged for cutting or reworking
5. **Inevitability Score** — 1-10 rating on structural inevitability, with notes on weak points

## Content Type Adaptations

| Content Type | Hammer Adaptation |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Magazine Profile** | The hammer is almost always a concrete human moment — a gesture, a detail, an image. Full workflow applies. |
| **Personal Essay** | The hammer is a self-discovery moment — something the writer realizes through the act of writing. Lighter architecture. |
| **Newsletter** | The hammer is the one line the reader carries into their day. Rapid version — Steps 1, 2, 5 only. |
| **Brand Narrative** | The hammer is the emotional transformation the brand promises. Step 4 (Protection Audit) is critical — brand content over-telegraphs. |
| **Book Chapter** | Multiple hammers per section, one master hammer for the book. Build the chapter hammer to serve the master hammer. |
| **Ghostwritten Piece** | The hammer must emerge from the client's voice and experience, not yours. |
