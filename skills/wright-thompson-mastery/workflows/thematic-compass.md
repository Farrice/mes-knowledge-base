# Thematic Compass

Theme is not a thesis statement. It's a gravitational field. Thompson: "I knew this piece was about retirement and death. I don't think I'm ever gonna say that explicitly, but hopefully when you read the piece it'll vibrate with that." This workflow establishes the thematic compass for a piece — the invisible force that organizes structure, scene selection, detail choice, and emotional trajectory without ever appearing on the page.

## Execution

### Requirements

The user provides **subject material — a topic, a subject, a draft, or raw notes**. This workflow produces a thematic compass that guides all downstream decisions. Run this BEFORE building scene architecture or selecting details.

### Step 1: Surface the Candidates

The theme is not chosen — it's discovered. List 3-5 potential themes that emerge from the material.

For each candidate theme:
- **State it in one sentence** (the sentence that will never appear in the piece)
- **What pattern in the material suggests this theme?** (Evidence, not interpretation)
- **Does this theme connect the subject to the reader?** The theme must be universal — not "Tiger Woods struggles with fame" but "what do you do when the infrastructure built to protect you is the thing eroding you?"

Thompson's test: "Will this help the reader discover something about themselves?" If the theme only illuminates the subject, it's too narrow. If only illuminates the reader, it's too abstract. It must do both.

### Step 2: The Gravitational Test

Pick the strongest candidate. Test whether it can organize the entire piece:

**Structure Test**: Can every section serve this theme without the theme being stated? List each planned section and describe how it contributes to the theme — not by arguing for it, but by vibrating with it.

**Detail Test**: Take 3-5 key details from the material. Does this theme make those details resonate more powerfully? A strong thematic compass makes details feel inevitable. A weak one makes details feel selected.

**Hammer Test**: Does this theme make the ending hit harder? Thompson's endings don't state the theme — they embody it. If the theme is "retirement and death," the ending is a man watching westerns alone. The theme makes the ending's power legible without being spoken.

**Anti-Thesis Test**: Could a smart reader summarize the piece with a different theme entirely? If yes, good — the piece has richness. If the theme is so obvious that every reader would name the same one, it's too on-the-nose.

### Step 3: Calibrate the Vibration Frequency

Thompson says the theme should "vibrate" — never appear explicitly but always be felt. Calibrate:

**Too Loud** (Theme bleeds through):
- A character says something that sounds like a thesis statement
- A paragraph of reflection that narrates the meaning
- A metaphor so on-the-nose it reads as symbolic rather than real

**Right Frequency** (Theme vibrates):
- Scenes are juxtaposed in ways that create meaning without commentary
- Details carry thematic weight because they were chosen, not because they were explained
- The reader senses the theme without being told — they feel it in their chest

**Too Quiet** (Theme doesn't register):
- The piece reads as a series of interesting scenes without cumulative effect
- Nothing connects the sections beyond chronology or topic
- The ending feels like it stops rather than lands

### Step 4: Build the Compass Rose

Produce the operational compass — the document that guides every downstream decision:

1. **True North**: The theme in one sentence (never appears in the piece)
2. **Magnetic Declination**: How the theme relates to the central complication (these are different things — the complication is the dramatic engine; the theme is the meaning)
3. **Cardinal Points**: 4 sub-themes or thematic notes that the piece will touch (e.g., if the theme is "retirement and death," the cardinal points might be: identity loss, physical decay, memory as refuge, the performance of relevance)
4. **Forbidden Territory**: What the piece is NOT about. What thematic lanes will you refuse to enter? (This is as important as what you include.)
5. **The Vibration Test Word**: A single word that should describe what the reader feels at the end. Not thinks — feels. (e.g., "ache," "vertigo," "recognition," "smallness")

## Output Requirements

**Deliver**:
1. **Theme Candidates** — 3-5 options with evidence and universality test
2. **Gravitational Test Results** — structure, detail, hammer, and anti-thesis tests
3. **Vibration Calibration** — assessment of whether current material runs too loud, right frequency, or too quiet, with specific passages cited
4. **Compass Rose** — True North, Magnetic Declination, Cardinal Points, Forbidden Territory, Vibration Test Word
5. **Compass Score** — 1-10 on whether the theme can organize the entire piece as a gravitational field

## Content Type Adaptations

| Content Type | Thematic Adaptation |
|-------------|---------------------|
| **Magazine Profile** | Full workflow applies. The theme is the difference between a profile and a list of facts about a person. |
| **Essay/Newsletter** | The compass is simpler — often one True North and 2 Cardinal Points. But it must still vibrate. Even a 500-word newsletter has a gravitational field. |
| **Brand Narrative** | True North = the brand's core belief (not its tagline — its actual belief). Cardinal Points = the brand's emotional territory. |
| **Book** | Each chapter has its own compass, but every chapter compass must be a sub-theme of the book's master compass. Map the hierarchy. |
| **Content Series** | The series has a master compass. Each episode/post has a sub-compass. Test: does each episode serve the master theme while hitting its own note? |
| **Ghostwriting** | The compass captures what the client cares about at the deepest level — the theme that runs beneath all their content, whether they know it or not. |
