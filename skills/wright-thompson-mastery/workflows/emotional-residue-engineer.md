# Emotional Residue Engineer

Thompson on the goal: "A good piece of writing is new and true and both specific and universal and it's something that helps somebody maybe discover something about themselves." The residue is the discovery. It's not what the reader remembers about the subject — it's what the reader discovers about themselves. This workflow engineers the conditions for that residue to form.

## Execution

### Requirements

The user provides **a draft or near-final piece** that works structurally but doesn't yet stay with the reader. Alternatively, the user is planning a piece and wants to design the residue architecture from the beginning. This workflow produces a residue map — the specific moments, techniques, and structural choices that create lasting emotional impact.

### Step 1: The Residue Inventory

First, identify what residue the piece currently produces — or should produce.

**If working with a draft**:
Read the draft and mark every moment where you feel something. Not where you think the writer wanted you to feel something — where you actually feel it. Be honest. If the piece produces zero genuine emotional responses, document that. The absence of residue is the diagnosis.

**If planning from scratch**:
Describe the emotional state the reader should occupy 24 hours after reading. Not during reading — after. What keeps surfacing? What does the reader find themselves thinking about at dinner, or in the shower, or when they see a stranger?

Thompson's examples of residue:
- Michael Jordan falling asleep watching westerns → the loneliness of a god
- Tiger's boats named Privacy and Solitude → the architecture of isolation
- Pat Riley's widow dying alone → what loyalty costs
- "The mask eats the face" → the moment a role becomes the self

### Step 2: The Residue Taxonomy

Not all residue is the same. Classify what kind of residue this piece should produce:

| Residue Type | What It Does | Thompson Example | Signal Phrase |
|-------------|-------------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Ache** | A beautiful sadness the reader carries | Jordan watching westerns | "I can't stop thinking about that" |
| **Recognition** | The reader sees themselves | Any identity piece | "That's exactly what it's like" |
| **Vertigo** | Reality shifts for the reader | Myth/reality gap revelation | "I never saw it that way before" |
| **Haunting** | An image that won't leave | A widow dying alone | "I keep seeing that scene" |
| **Question** | An unanswerable question the reader absorbs | The mask eating the face | "I don't know the answer but I can't stop asking" |

A piece can produce more than one type. But one should be primary. The primary residue type should align with the thematic compass (Pattern 9).

### Step 3: The Residue Architecture

Residue doesn't come from a single moment — it comes from accumulation. The hammer (Pattern 1) delivers the payload, but the earlier sections charge it. Design the charging sequence:

**Pre-Load Phase** (Early sections):
Establish the emotional foundation through concrete specifics. The reader doesn't know they're being prepared for an emotional landing. Key move: present details that seem merely interesting but will become devastating when the ending reframes them.

- **What details in the opening will change meaning after the ending?** (These are planted detonators — they look inert on first reading.)
- **What emotional tone should the early sections establish to maximize the contrast or resonance of the ending?**

**Building Phase** (Middle sections):
Layer the interior experience. Use the Interiority Bridge (Pattern 4) to connect the reader to the subject's internal world. The deeper the reader goes into the subject's interiority, the harder the ending will hit.

- **Where does the reader first access the subject's private experience?** (This is the moment the reader stops being an observer and starts being a participant.)
- **How does each subsequent section deepen that access?**

**Delivery Phase** (The ending):
The hammer lands — but it lands because of everything before it. The ending is not where the emotion is created. It's where the emotion is released.

- **What is the final image?** (Not the final sentence — the final image. The reader should see something.)
- **How does this image activate the detonators planted in the opening?**
- **What is left unsaid?** (Residue lives in the space between what was shown and what the reader infers.)

### Step 4: The Restraint Protocol

The biggest threat to emotional residue is the writer's inability to let the moment breathe. Thompson's method: show the moment and stop. Don't explain. Don't narrate the emotion. Don't tell the reader what to feel.

**Restraint Audit** — check the draft for:
- Sentences that narrate the reader's emotion ("It was heartbreaking")
- Sentences that explain the significance ("This moment showed...")
- Sentences that summarize the theme after it's already landed
- Any sentence in the final 3 paragraphs that is abstract rather than concrete

Every one of these is an erosion target. Cut them. The image does the work. The silence after the image is where the residue forms.

Thompson via Hemingway: "Prose is architecture, not interior decoration." Residue is architecture. The emotional space is built by the structure — not decorated by the language.

### Step 5: The 24-Hour Test

Imagine the reader 24 hours later. What stays?

- **The image**: What single image will surface unbidden?
- **The question**: What question will they carry?
- **The feeling**: What name would they give the feeling? (It should be hard to name — residue is specific enough to be felt but too complex to be labeled.)

If you can clearly answer all three, the residue architecture is working. If any is blank, the piece is missing a layer.

## Output Requirements

**Deliver**:
1. **Residue Inventory** — current emotional moments in the draft (or planned emotional targets)
2. **Residue Taxonomy** — primary and secondary residue types with rationale
3. **Residue Architecture** — pre-load phase (detonators), building phase (interiority deepening), delivery phase (image + silence)
4. **Restraint Audit** — specific sentences/passages to cut because they explain the emotion rather than produce it
5. **24-Hour Test** — the image, the question, and the feeling the reader will carry
6. **Residue Score** — 1-10. Below 6 = the piece is interesting but forgettable. Above 8 = the piece will stay with the reader for days.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Content Type | Residue Adaptation |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Magazine Profile** | Full workflow applies. This is the ultimate quality test for long-form journalism. |
| **Personal Essay** | The residue is your own truth made transferable. The reader should feel THEIR version of your experience, not yours. |
| **Newsletter** | One residue moment maximum. Choose the type (usually Recognition or Question) and engineer only that. Compressed architecture — 3 paragraphs of pre-load, 1 paragraph of delivery. |
| **Brand Story** | The residue should be about the CUSTOMER's identity, not the brand. The brand vanishes; the customer's sense of self remains. |
| **Video Script** | Visual residue is primary. What single shot will the viewer see when they close their eyes? Design that first. |
| **Ghostwriting** | The residue must be authentic to the client's experience. You can engineer the architecture — but the images, the moments, the truth must come from them. |

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: Before delivering, review output against the **Anti-Patterns** in `genius.md` § Anti-Patterns. If the piece explains its own emotional impact anywhere — ANYWHERE — the residue will not form. Cut the explanation. Trust the image. Trust the silence. Trust the reader.
