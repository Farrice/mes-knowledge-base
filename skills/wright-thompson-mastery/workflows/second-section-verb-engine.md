# Second-Section Verb Engine

The second section of a piece is not a noun — it's a verb. It *expands*. Thompson: "A second section is almost a verb... it flows off of the lead in such a way that it feels inevitable and chronological but actually what you're doing is expanding out." This workflow engineers the structural move that separates a good piece from a great one.

## Execution

### Requirements

The user provides **a lead/opening section and subject material** — a completed opening, a draft with a weak second section, or raw notes about a subject where the lead is known. This workflow produces the structural blueprint for the second section.

### Step 1: Diagnose the Lead's Scope

Read the lead section and answer precisely:

- **What specific scene, moment, or detail does the lead live in?** (This is the micro.)
- **What is the emotional temperature of the lead?** (Intimate? Tense? Elegiac? Kinetic?)
- **What question does the lead implicitly raise without answering?**

The lead should be concrete, specific, grounded in one moment. If it's not — if the lead is already trying to be thematic — flag it. The second section can't expand what's already broad.

**Output**: Three sentences: The micro scene, the emotional temperature, the implicit question.

### Step 2: Find the Thematic Expansion

The second section asks the broad question the rest of the piece will answer. It takes the implicit question from Step 1 and *expands* it from personal to universal.

Thompson's method:
- The lead shows Michael Jordan at 50 → the second section asks: what does any human do when their identity had a shelf life?
- The lead shows Tiger Woods on his boat → the second section asks: what happens when you force extrovert infrastructure onto an introvert?
- The lead shows Caitlyn Clark at a specific moment → the second section asks: what's coming for her and can she survive it?

**The formula**: [Specific person doing specific thing] → [Universal human question this illuminates]

Generate 3 candidate expansions. For each:
- State the universal question in one sentence
- Explain how it flows "inevitably" from the lead — the reader should feel chronological momentum even though the move is thematic

### Step 3: The Inevitability Test

Pick the strongest candidate. Now test it:

1. **Flow test**: Read the lead and then the proposed second section opening. Does it feel inevitable — like you're being carried forward? Or does it feel like a hard cut — like the writer changed subjects?
2. **Chronological illusion**: Thompson says the expansion should "feel inevitable and chronological but actually what you're doing is expanding out." The reader shouldn't feel the gear change. Does the second section sustain the forward momentum while quietly widening the lens?
3. **Thematic load-bearing test**: Will the question posed in the second section sustain the rest of the piece? Is it large enough to organize 5-10 sections of exploration?

If any test fails → revise or select a different candidate.

### Step 4: Write the Section Opening

Draft the first 2-3 sentences of the second section. These sentences must:

- **Carry forward** at least one concrete element from the lead (a name, a detail, a line of dialogue)
- **Widen the frame** — the reader should feel the camera pulling back
- **Pose the question** without asking it explicitly. The question vibrates. It is never a topic sentence.

Thompson's anti-pattern (from genius.md): "I don't think I'm ever gonna say that explicitly, but hopefully when you read the piece it'll vibrate with that."

### Step 5: Map the Rest

Now that you have the thematic question, map how the remaining sections answer it. For each subsequent section:

- **What piece of the answer does this section provide?**
- **How does this section hit a different note from all others?** (Pattern 5 — no repetition of effect)
- **Does this section serve the hammer?** (Pattern 1)

## Output Requirements

**Deliver**:
1. **Lead Diagnosis** — the micro, the temperature, the implicit question
2. **3 Expansion Candidates** — each with universal question and inevitability logic
3. **Selected Expansion** with all three tests passed
4. **Section Opening Draft** — 2-3 sentences showing the transition
5. **Section Map** — how remaining sections answer the thematic question
6. **Expansion Score** — 1-10 on whether the second section feels like a verb (movement) or a noun (description)

## Content Type Adaptations

| Content Type | Second-Section Adaptation |
|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Magazine Profile** | Full workflow applies. The second section is the single most important structural move. |
| **Personal Essay** | The lead is your specific experience; the second section expands to why it matters to the reader. Lighter architecture — may be a single paragraph. |
| **Newsletter** | The lead is the hook; the second section is the "so what?" expansion that makes it worth reading. Rapid version — Steps 1, 2, 4 only. |
| **Brand Narrative** | The lead is the customer's specific moment; the second section expands to the identity-level transformation. |
| **Book Chapter** | Each chapter has its own lead→expansion arc, but also serves the book's macro expansion. Map both. |
| **Landing Page** | Hero section = lead. Expansion section = second-section verb. The page expands from specific pain to universal identity. |
