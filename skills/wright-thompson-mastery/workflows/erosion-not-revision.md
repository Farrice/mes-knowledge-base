# Erosion, Not Revision

Thompson doesn't revise. He erodes. "It gets shorter." "I think for me it starts to get shorter and sharper." The difference is fundamental: revision adds and rearranges — erosion removes what was never essential. Like water shaping stone, the final piece was always inside the draft. Your job is to wear away everything that isn't it.

## Execution

### Requirements

The user provides **a completed draft** — a piece that has been written but feels too long, too loose, too explanatory, or lacks sharpness. This workflow produces a shorter, sharper version through systematic erosion.

### Step 1: The Length Diagnosis

Before touching a word, assess the draft's bulk:

- **Current word count**: ___
- **Target word count**: Typically 15-30% shorter than current. Thomson's drafts always get shorter.
- **Where does the draft feel effortful?** Mark every passage where you sense the writer working — explaining, justifying, contextualizing. These are the first erosion targets.
- **Where does the draft feel effortless?** Mark every passage where the writing disappears and the reader is simply there. These are load-bearing — protect them.

### Step 2: The Five Erosion Passes

Run each pass independently. Do not combine. Each pass removes a different kind of excess.

**Pass 1 — Throat-Clearing Erosion**
Remove every sentence that exists to warm up, set up, or explain what's about to happen. Writers write their way into scenes — readers don't need the runway.

Targets:
- Opening sentences of sections that describe rather than show
- "Context paragraphs" that tell the reader what to think before showing them
- Any sentence that begins with "It's important to understand..." or equivalent

**Pass 2 — Abstraction Erosion**
Remove every abstraction that isn't earned by a preceding concrete detail.

Targets:
- Adjectives that interpret rather than observe ("devastating," "remarkable," "iconic")
- Emotional labels ("He was sad" → cut. If the detail before it doesn't convey sadness on its own, the detail needs to be stronger, not the label)
- Topic sentences that announce what a section is about

**Pass 3 — Repetition Erosion**
Thompson: "Every single section, every scene needs to hit a different note so it's not repetitive."

Targets:
- Scenes that hit the same emotional note as another scene (cut one)
- Points that are made in more than one place (keep the strongest instance)
- Details that carry the same thematic weight as another detail (keep the more concrete one)
- Sentence rhythms that repeat (three long sentences in a row, three short sentences in a row)

**Pass 4 — Safety Erosion**
Remove everything the writer included to feel safe — the hedges, qualifications, and preemptive defenses.

Targets:
- "To be fair..." and all equivalents
- Balanced-perspective insertions that dilute a clear position
- Caveats that the reader doesn't need
- Transitions that over-explain the connection between sections (trust the reader to follow)

**Pass 5 — The Final Pass**
Read the eroded draft aloud. Cut anything your mouth wants to skip.

Thompson: "I make it a little shorter, and maybe I refine the word choice... I kinda know at a certain point like it's all there and it's correct and I've kind of found the way I want to say most of the stuff."

This is the pass where individual words are weighed. "I will go back through and change individual words." Not sentences — words.

### Step 3: The Rebuild Test

After all five passes, test the eroded draft:

1. **Structural Integrity**: Does it still stand? Or did you erode a load-bearing section? If the piece collapses, put that section back and find something else to cut.
2. **The Ball-Downhill Test**: Thompson quoting his editor: "Push the ball downhill and clear out all the obstacles so it rolls." Does the eroded version roll more smoothly?
3. **The Sharpness Test**: Is every remaining sentence doing exactly one thing, and doing it well? "Shorter and sharper" — if it's shorter but not sharper, you cut the wrong things.

### Step 4: The Word-Level Polish

Thompson's final stage: individual word refinement.

For each sentence in the eroded draft:
- Can any word be replaced with a more precise word?
- Can any word be removed without losing meaning?
- Does the sentence rhythm match its content? (Short sentences for impact. Longer sentences for immersion. Never the reverse.)

## Output Requirements

**Deliver**:
1. **Length Diagnosis** — current count, target count, effortful/effortless passage map
2. **Erosion Log** — for each of the 5 passes: what was removed and why (specific passages cited)
3. **Eroded Draft** — the full revised text
4. **Rebuild Test Results** — structural integrity, ball-downhill, and sharpness assessments
5. **Word-Level Changes** — specific word substitutions in final polish
6. **Erosion Score** — 1-10 on whether the draft feels like stone after water vs. text after editing. Stone = the essential shape was revealed. Text = it was merely shortened.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Content Type | Erosion Adaptation |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Magazine Profile** | Full workflow applies. Long-form profiles are the primary erosion territory. Multiple passes critical. |
| **Newsletter** | Passes 1 (throat-clearing) and 4 (safety) are most impactful. Short-form doesn't need 5 passes — run the two that matter most. |
| **Personal Essay** | Pass 2 (abstraction) is critical. Personal essays default to emotional labeling. Erode the labels; keep the moments. |
| **Brand Copy** | Pass 4 (safety) is the kill shot. Brand copy is full of hedges and qualifications. Strip them. |
| **Ghostwriting** | All passes apply, but be careful in Pass 5 — don't erode the client's voice. Erode the writer's voice; preserve the client's. |
| **Video Script** | Pass 1 (throat-clearing) is essential. Spoken content has zero tolerance for warm-up. Cut straight to the scene. |

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: Before delivering, review output against the **Anti-Patterns** in `genius.md` § Anti-Patterns. Erosion should reveal the piece's essential shape — not impose a new one.
