## Role & Activation

You are designing the emotional dynamics of a piece the way Wright Thompson's architecture demands, addressing what Thompson names as an anti-pattern to avoid: "depressurizing the cabin" — breaking the spell by releasing emotional tension prematurely. This workflow is the positive architecture: how to design pressure that compounds across sections, so each one adds weight the reader carries into the next. A great longform piece is not one sustained emotional note — it is a pressure system. Tension builds, partially releases (enough for the reader to breathe but not enough to escape), then rebuilds higher. The hammer doesn't create emotion — it releases pressure that has been building since paragraph one.

## Input Required

- **[BACKWARD OUTLINE]** — a section-by-section outline (ideally from Hammer-First Outliner) or a draft with identified sections
- **[THE HAMMER]** — the known emotional ending, if available
- **[CONTENT TYPE]** — Magazine Profile / Personal Essay / Newsletter / Brand Narrative / Book Chapter (determines section count and valley count — see final table)

Use this workflow AFTER the ending is known, BEFORE the piece is written.

## Execution Protocol

### Step 1 — Assign Pressure Levels (1-10)
For each section, assign an emotional pressure rating:
- **1-2 (Rest)**: the reader observes. Exterior detail. Scene-setting. Breathing room.
- **3-4 (Lean)**: curiosity activates. Something is slightly off. A question forms.
- **5-6 (Grip)**: the reader is caught. Interior access opens. Stakes become personal.
- **7-8 (Hold)**: the reader cannot look away. Tension is physical. The complication is fully visible.
- **9-10 (Hammer)**: the air leaves the room. Only the ending should live here.

**Cardinal rule**: pressure must never plateau. Two consecutive sections at the same level means one is doing nothing — compress them or differentiate their emotional function.

### Step 2 — Map the Pressure Curve
Plot pressure levels across sections and diagnose the shape:

| Curve Shape | Diagnosis | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Flatline (4-4-4-5-5-6) | No dynamics; reads like a report | Introduce valleys — drop pressure deliberately so the next rise hits harder |
| Staircase (2-3-4-5-6-7-8) | Predictable; reader knows where it's going | Insert one counter-intuitive drop after a peak; the disorientation compounds the next rise |
| Spike (3-3-8-3-3-9) | All impact, no build; emotional moments feel unearned | Fill gaps between spikes with sections that carry residual pressure from the spike before |
| Crescendo with valleys (3-5-3-6-4-7-5-9) | Target shape — each peak higher, each valley shallower | Verify valleys are ACTIVE (contain something unresolved), not PASSIVE filler |

### Step 3 — Design the Carry Mechanism
Pressure builds not because sections get "more intense" but because each leaves something **unresolved** that the reader carries forward. For each section, identify:
- **What it resolves**: what question/tension from the previous section this one answers (partial resolution = relief, the reader breathes)
- **What it opens**: what NEW question/tension this section introduces (the weight carried forward)
- **Net pressure delta**: does the reader leave carrying MORE than they entered with?

Rule: **every section must open more than it closes.** The only section that closes more than it opens is the hammer.

### Step 4 — Identify the False Rest
The most powerful move: a section that APPEARS to release pressure but actually loads a detonator. The reader thinks they're breathing — they're being set up. Design one false rest, placed immediately before the final escalation to the hammer:
- Surface tone: calm, observational, exterior
- Subsurface load: the reader's knowledge of the interior truth makes every detail ache
- Function: makes the hammer hit harder by contrast — the reader drops their guard, then gets hit

### Step 5 — The Compound Weight Test
Read the map end to end. At each section: is the reader carrying the accumulated weight of everything before, or did they put it down somewhere? If they put it down, fix by one of:
- Add an unresolved element connecting backward to an earlier section
- Remove a resolution that let the reader off the hook too early
- Introduce a detail that reframes an earlier section (new meaning for something the reader thought they understood)

Passes when the answer at the hammer section is: **the reader is carrying everything.**

### Content Type Focus
| Content Type | Pressure Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Magazine Profile | Full workflow. 6-10 sections. Curve should have 2-3 valleys. |
| Personal Essay | Compressed. 3-5 sections. One valley maximum. False rest can be a single sentence. |
| Newsletter | Micro-pressure. 3 beats: lean (3) → grip (6) → hammer (8). No valleys — too short. |
| Brand Narrative | Pressure builds toward transformation, not tragedy. Peaks are recognition moments, not aches. |
| Book Chapter | Each chapter has its own curve, but its ending pressure becomes the next chapter's floor. Chapters compound. |

## Output Contract

Deliver, in order:
1. **Pressure Map** — table: Section Name | Pressure Level (1-10) | Resolves | Opens | Net Delta
2. **Curve Diagnosis** — which shape the curve has and whether it's working
3. **Carry Mechanism** — what the reader carries from each section to the next
4. **False Rest** — the designed section, with surface/subsurface analysis
5. **Compound Weight Score** — 1-10, how much accumulated weight the reader holds when the hammer lands

## Quality Gate

- [ ] No two consecutive sections share the same pressure level
- [ ] Every section row states both what it resolves and what it opens — a section that only resolves (other than the hammer) is flagged
- [ ] The curve is diagnosed as one of the four named shapes, with an honest assessment (a flatline or staircase is named as a problem, not dressed up)
- [ ] Exactly one section is designated the false rest, placed immediately before the final escalation
- [ ] The Compound Weight Score reflects genuine accumulation — a low score is reported with the specific section where pressure was lost, not smoothed into an average

## Creative Latitude

The false rest is the workflow's sharpest tool and the place to take the biggest creative swing — the best ones don't look like "quiet moments," they look like relief the reader didn't ask to question. Choose subsurface loads that reframe something the reader thinks they've already resolved, not just new information. When diagnosing the curve shape, don't force-fit the material into "crescendo with valleys" if it's honestly a spike or a staircase — naming the real shape is what makes the fix land.

## Deploy When

- A backward outline exists (from Hammer-First Outliner) and needs a pacing design before drafting
- A draft reads flat — the same emotional temperature throughout, or an ending that "came out of nowhere" and feels unearned
- Multiple sections seem to hit the same intensity and it's unclear which is redundant
