# Wright Thompson — Cross-Domain Patterns

12 codified patterns extracted from Thompson's methodology. Each workflow references these by number.

---

## Pattern 1: The Hammer (Ending-First Architecture)

**Definition**: Know the emotional destination before writing the first word. Every structural decision serves the ending.

**Thompson's Language**: "I always want to know what the ending is... I like to make decisions based on how to maximize the power of the ending."

**The Feel**: "You read an ending of a piece that's really — it almost feels like the air gets sucked out of the room and there's a hollowness. It feels hollow. It feels echoey. I love that feeling."

**Critical Rule**: Endings are elliptical, not conclusive. "Don't hammer the door shut. A story is supposed to open a door, not close it."

**Cross-Domain**: Sales pages (the transformation must be felt before the CTA), newsletters (the last line should echo), brand narratives (the ending creates the memory).

**Connected Patterns**: 12 (Emotional Residue), 5 (Scene Architecture), 9 (Thematic Compass)

---

## Pattern 2: Central Complication

**Definition**: Every profile has one irreducible tension that explains everything. Find it or you don't have a story.

**Thompson's Language**: "What is the central complication of someone's life and how on a daily basis do they go about solving it?" (Gary Smith, adopted by Thompson as core method.)

**Critical Rule**: The complication is almost always interior — "Almost all of that is happening inside." Your job is to make it visible.

**Cross-Domain**: Client profiles (what's the one tension driving their business?), brand stories (what's the unresolved tension in the founder's life?), personal essays (what's the question you keep avoiding?).

**Connected Patterns**: 4 (Interiority Bridge), 6 (Myth/Reality Gap), 10 (Universal-Specific)

---

## Pattern 3: Reporting > Writing (The Purple Prose Cure)

**Definition**: Overwriting is always under-reporting in disguise. The cure for bad prose is more evidence, not better sentences.

**Thompson's Language**: "When somebody says something is purple, something is overwritten, all overwritten really means is under-reported." / "A great detail will do the work of 50 shitty sentences." / "Every time I feel like I can write myself out of — I can write around a hole in my knowledge, it's just bad."

**The Method**: Figure out what you want to know → figure out who would know that information → figure out how to get them to tell you.

**Cross-Domain**: Content writing (research density determines quality), ghostwriting (more interviews = better voice capture), case studies (go deeper into the data, not wider into the language).

**Connected Patterns**: 8 (Detail as Metaphor), 2 (Central Complication), 11 (Erosion)

---

## Pattern 4: Interiority/Exteriority Bridge

**Definition**: Surface the subterranean interior life so that exterior actions become "freighted with meaning." Then pull back to pure description — let the reader do the work.

**Thompson's Language**: "Bringing to the surface people's subterranean interior lives and then doing it where the things they're doing, if you set it up right, become freighted with meaning." / "I am only describing his exterior actions and yet the story is so rooted in his interior life that I don't have to explain it anymore."

**The Example**: Michael Jordan falling asleep to westerns. If you've established he misses his father and feels closest to him watching westerns, describing the exterior action carries the full weight of grief without ever naming it.

**Critical Rule**: "Scenes without interiority are two-dimensional. They're origami. It's not a swan."

**Cross-Domain**: Character development in any medium, brand storytelling (show the founder's private motivation through their public action), testimonial writing (the internal shift matters more than the external result).

**Connected Patterns**: 2 (Central Complication), 5 (Scene Architecture), 12 (Emotional Residue)

---

## Pattern 5: Scene Architecture

**Definition**: Every section hits a different note. Scenes are the fundamental unit of narrative. Repetition of effect is death.

**Thompson's Language**: "Every single section, every scene needs to hit a different note so it's not repetitive." / "A section is the equivalent of a chapter."

**Structural Method**: Post-it notes or note cards on the wall. Broad outline — you know where you need to get from and to — but within sections, feel your way. "I'm going to have a better idea in the seat of what works than whatever theory I had standing in front of my wall."

**Cross-Domain**: Newsletter series (each edition hits a different note), video scripts (scene cuts not logical connectors), course design (each module must justify its existence against every other module).

**Connected Patterns**: 7 (Second-Section Verb), 1 (Hammer), 9 (Thematic Compass)

---

## Pattern 6: The Myth/Reality Gap

**Definition**: The most compelling subjects live in the tension between their public mythology and their private reality.

**Thompson's Language**: "The mask eats the face" (Updike, adopted by Thompson). / Tiger Woods: "Nobody in that room stopped for a second to ask, 'What happens when we take a plan designed for an extreme extrovert and force it onto an extreme introvert?'"

**The Method**: "10 different people who know [someone] from 10 different places will have 10 different versions of them. I don't think my job is to sort those out. I think my job is to present a fractal image of a complicated human being."

**Cross-Domain**: Brand audits (gap between brand promise and customer experience), personal branding (the audience's perception vs. your actual life), founder stories (the pitch deck narrative vs. the 3am reality).

**Connected Patterns**: 2 (Central Complication), 4 (Interiority Bridge), 10 (Universal-Specific)

---

## Pattern 7: Second-Section Verb

**Definition**: The second section of a piece is not a noun — it's a verb. It expands from micro (the lead) to macro (the thematic question the rest of the piece will answer).

**Thompson's Language**: "A second section is almost a verb... it flows off of the lead in such a way that it feels inevitable and chronological but actually what you're doing is expanding out this way. You're asking the question in the second scene in a broad thematic way that the rest of the story is going to answer."

**Cross-Domain**: Landing pages (hero section → expansion section), email sequences (hook email → context email), podcast episodes (cold open → thematic frame).

**Connected Patterns**: 5 (Scene Architecture), 9 (Thematic Compass), 1 (Hammer)

---

## Pattern 8: Detail as Metaphor

**Definition**: Choose concrete details that carry thematic weight without being explicitly symbolic. A detail that doesn't do thematic work is decoration.

**Thompson's Language**: "A great detail will do the work of 50 shitty sentences." / Tiger Woods's boats: Privacy alongside Solitude — the detail tells the whole story.

**The Test**: Does this detail serve the central complication? Does it carry thematic weight? If not, cut it — no matter how interesting it is on its own.

**Cross-Domain**: Copy (one proof point that carries the whole argument), brand assets (the visual detail that encodes the brand's tension), content (the anecdote that makes the abstract concrete).

**Connected Patterns**: 3 (Reporting > Writing), 4 (Interiority Bridge), 9 (Thematic Compass)

---

## Pattern 9: Thematic Compass

**Definition**: Theme is a gravitational field organizing every decision — not a thesis statement the writer announces.

**Thompson's Language**: "I don't think I'm ever gonna say that explicitly, but hopefully when you read the piece it'll vibrate with that." / Joe Montana: "You were watching him struggle with what to do when time was diminishing your most valuable asset, which was the way people remembered you. I don't think I ever said that."

**Critical Rule**: The theme should never be stated in the piece. It should "vibrate."

**Cross-Domain**: Content strategy (what's the gravitational field pulling all your content together?), brand voice (what's the unspoken thing all your messaging is really about?), curriculum design (the invisible thread connecting all modules).

**Connected Patterns**: 1 (Hammer), 7 (Second-Section Verb), 10 (Universal-Specific)

---

## Pattern 10: Universal-Specific Bridge

**Definition**: The more specific you are, the more universal it becomes. Extreme individual experience is a magnified version of something everyone deals with.

**Thompson's Language**: "Michael Jordan at 50 is trying to imagine what do you do when you used to be Michael Jordan. And that's an extreme version of something that everybody deals with." / "Watching somebody so extreme... allows everybody to try to see pieces of themselves in other people's struggles."

**The Method**: Don't chase universality directly. Go deeper into the specific. The universal will surface on its own when the specific is true enough.

**Cross-Domain**: Case studies (go deep on one client, not broad on many), testimonials (the specific transformation resonates universally), thought leadership (your particular experience, fully excavated, teaches more than general advice).

**Connected Patterns**: 2 (Central Complication), 9 (Thematic Compass), 4 (Interiority Bridge)

---

## Pattern 11: Erosion Not Revision

**Definition**: Refinement through subtraction — re-reading from page one, cutting what doesn't serve the story, rather than "rewriting" with new material.

**Thompson's Language**: "I love to cut." / The Barn: "The first draft was 280,000 words and I cut 173,000. It ran at 107." / "I felt like I had a real big idea and I didn't have the toolbox to land it and so I did it the best I could and it was unbelievably inefficient and I don't ever want to do that again."

**The Method**: Write fast, cut slow. The first draft is for finding what you have. The erosion is for finding what you need.

**Critical Rule**: "Not being so hamfisted." Cut beautiful material if it doesn't serve the story. "Protecting the hammer" means being willing to sacrifice everything that doesn't maximize the ending.

**Cross-Domain**: Content editing (cut 40%+ — if it doesn't serve the central complication, it goes), product design (feature erosion), pitch decks (every slide must justify existence against the ending).

**Connected Patterns**: 1 (Hammer), 3 (Reporting > Writing), 5 (Scene Architecture)

---

## Pattern 12: Emotional Residue

**Definition**: The goal of every piece is to leave something with the reader that stays — a feeling, an image, a line they'll carry.

**Thompson's Language**: "The air gets sucked out of the room... hollow... echoey." / Endings must be "elliptical" — resolving the narrative arc while leaving deeper questions open.

**The Method**: A satisfying ending to the narrative arc + unresolved deeper questions that are "authentic to our lives." The reader should feel the story ended and also feel that something just began.

**Cross-Domain**: Email closings (the line they'll quote to someone), newsletter endings (the thought they carry into their day), keynote speeches (the moment the audience remembers at dinner).

**Connected Patterns**: 1 (Hammer), 4 (Interiority Bridge), 9 (Thematic Compass)
