This skill system extracts the operating method from Liam Ottley's framing and Lara Acosta's demonstrated LinkedIn method: use AI to help an expert turn what they know into LinkedIn content that attracts decision-makers, builds authority, and routes attention into a lead magnet or offer.

These ten patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist to stamp in order. Absorb monetizable-expertise-first, strategic arbitrage, hook-plus-rehook, broad-narrow-niche, SLAY, and PAS, then build a plan that fits the specific expert in front of you. If the output labels sections "Pattern 3 applied here" or narrates "using the broad-narrow-niche framework," you have failed — the machinery must be invisible on the page, felt only in how the post moves.

The test: **would Lara Acosta recognize this as her own two-pillar operating method** (monetizable expertise + strategic arbitrage, stated verbatim at 00:11:48 — "those are the two key pillars that every single time we implement them, we get results") — **or as someone using LinkedIn-guru vocabulary borrowed from a swipe file?** If it reads like a template with the nouns swapped, rebuild it.

Specifically:
- Do NOT open a hook with "how to" unless it is paired with a specific outcome, number, or named mechanism — Lara's own rule, stated at 00:50:04: "eight word long hooks are best. Always use a specific outcome or number."
- Do NOT let a story stand alone without a business bridge. Her own diagnosis of why LinkedIn stories read as cringe (00:36:46): "stories tend to seem cringe on LinkedIn... because they'll be like, 'Oh my god, my dad died.' And it's a selfie of this girl" — with no line back to the buyer problem.
- Her texture is conversational and self-deprecating about her own frameworks (she jokes the SLAY framework exists "because I'm a girl and I slay all the time," 00:45:09) — not academic or hedge-heavy. Keep delivery direct, a little blunt, structurally loose even when the underlying logic is tight. Polish that erases the bluntness is the tell.

## Core Thesis

LinkedIn is valuable for B2B and service businesses because decision-makers are dense on the platform — the opening claim of the source is a direct stat: "Studies show that LinkedIn is responsible for generating 80% of all B2B leads" (00:00:01, `transcript.txt`). The game is not "AI versus humans." It is people using AI well versus people using AI badly.

The source argues that most AI LinkedIn content fails because it asks a model for generic drafts before the strategy is clear. The better order is:

1. Clarify the buyer outcome.
2. Extract monetizable expertise.
3. Find strategic arbitrage angles.
4. Generate hooks and rehooks.
5. Draft with a proven structure.
6. Bridge the post to a lead magnet or offer.
7. Be present for the engagement window.

## Source-Grounded Patterns

### Pattern 1: Monetizable Expertise First

Monetizable expertise is knowledge that directly changes someone's outcome in a way they would pay for. The content does not begin with "what I know." It begins with "what changes for the buyer if they understand this?" Lara names this as one of "the two key pillars that every single time we implement them, we get results" (00:11:48, `transcript.txt`).

Use this before any AI drafting. If the expertise cannot name a buyer outcome, payment reason, or business consequence, it is not ready to become lead-generating content.

### Pattern 2: Strategic Arbitrage

Strategic arbitrage means borrowing the structure, timing, or attention of what already works while applying the creator's story, skill, or offer. It can come from trends, authority figures, popular post structures, buyer anxieties, industry shifts, or high-performing swipe examples — the source's own case is a creator who is "educating on what's happening as well. And then he actually turned this into a lead magnet" (00:42:53, `transcript.txt`).

The goal is not to copy ideas. The goal is to reuse attention patterns with a different insight and commercial bridge.

### Pattern 3: Hook Plus Rehook

The first line stops the scroll. The second line keeps the reader from bouncing before the "see more" click: "the rehook is your second chance to retain someone and get them to click more" (00:14:35, `transcript.txt`). Weak posts jump from a generic hook into a list. Strong posts extend the hook with specificity, proof, tension, or an outcome.

Good hook raw material:

- exact result
- specific number
- named mechanism
- "how I" rather than generic "how to"
- buyer pain with commercial consequence
- timely trend translated into a useful take

### Pattern 4: Broad-Narrow-Niche

A post should be broad enough to catch a larger professional audience, narrow enough to be relevant to a buyer problem, and niche enough to prove the creator is not generic. Lara's own worked example uses her real number: "my one person business is about to cross $200,000 a month. Okay, that's broad enough but still kind of niche. And then I go narrow" (00:34:58–00:35:13, `transcript.txt`).

Default sequence:

1. Broad hook: recognizable business desire, result, fear, or trend.
2. Narrow setup: specific buyer segment or problem.
3. Niche body: creator's method, evidence, mechanism, or example.
4. Commercial bridge: lead magnet, DM prompt, booking path, or offer.

### Pattern 5: Story Plus Education

The strongest personal brand posts both storytell and educate. Story earns attention and retention; lesson creates usefulness; actionable advice creates trust; a buyer-centered CTA turns the post into a lead magnet surface. Lara names the failure mode directly: "the main mistake that people make when storytelling and using authority jacking... is that they get too broad that they forget to talk about their business themselves or what they actually help people with" (00:33:47–00:34:00, `transcript.txt`).

Stories are not about the creator. They are about what the reader sees in themselves, their goal, their fear, or their future self.

### Pattern 6: SLAY Structure

Use SLAY as a post structure:

- Story: open with a specific lived moment or concrete setup.
- Lesson: name the business or buyer insight.
- Advice: give tactical steps the reader can use immediately.
- You: point the ending back to the reader with a question, CTA, or next action.

Lara built and named this framework herself (00:45:09, `transcript.txt`) and notes it lands even with an audience she calls "mainly male-dominated" — she reports 40-year-old men telling her they use it. This is best for authority, founder story, personal lesson, and educational posts that need retention before the teaching begins.

### Pattern 7: PAS For Sales Content

Use problem-agitate-solution when the post must sell or route into a lead magnet. The source's own compressed worked example: "here's your problem. Founders, your LinkedIn content sucks. Agitate: it's costing you thousands of dollars daily. And then the solution is: here's how to write content that sells" (00:49:22–00:49:34, `transcript.txt`).

- Problem: name the buyer's current stuck state.
- Agitate: show the cost of leaving it unresolved — the source names it in dollars, not vague discomfort.
- Solution: teach the method or offer the diagnostic/asset that helps them move.

The CTA should be clear, not needy: download, comment, DM, audit, checklist, walkthrough, or book.

### Pattern 8: AI Needs Identity And Proof Before Drafting

The AI workflow starts with context, not a blank prompt: "we have a knowledge base where you basically just upload every single document that you've ever created" (00:52:14, `transcript.txt`). Give the model:

- what the expert sells
- who they serve
- proof and results
- voice preferences and banned language
- offer advantages
- monetizable expertise
- strategic arbitrage topics
- swipe structures or saved posts

Ask for hooks before full posts. Pick the strongest hook, then ask for a full draft using the selected structure.

### Pattern 9: Same But Different Remix

Use swipe files as structure libraries. A good remix keeps the shape that created attention but replaces the story, examples, expertise, proof, and CTA — the source's own AI-prompt phrasing: "let's say I say, 'I love this post that I just saved from my swipe file. Based on what you know about me, write me a similar post'" (00:51:19–00:51:26, `transcript.txt`).

The output must explain why the structure works so the creator learns, not just receives a draft.

### Pattern 10: First 30 Minutes Guard

Do not treat scheduling as "done." The source states this as a direct warning: "when you are choosing to schedule it, you're also choosing to forget about it... if you're not there within the first 30 minutes of the post going live, then the post has a higher chance of dying" (00:57:33–00:57:45, `transcript.txt`). The operating plan should reserve the first 30 minutes after posting for replies and high-quality comments — she calls this the one window where "you can [control the algorithm]... because it's the lead time" (00:57:49–00:57:53, `transcript.txt`).

## Anti-Patterns

- Generic AI draft before strategy: the source frames unstrategic AI posting as the reason LinkedIn is "literally seen as the cringiest platform," urging creators to "go beyond the cringe part of posting on LinkedIn and feeling like a robot" (00:05:44–00:07:02, `transcript.txt`).
- "How to" hooks with no proof, result, or rehook: contradicts the source's explicit rule that "eight word long hooks are best. Always use a specific outcome or number" (00:50:04, `transcript.txt`) and that a post needs a rehook, "your second chance to retain someone" (00:14:35, `transcript.txt`).
- Posting raw case-study or client-win videos and expecting reach: named directly as "one of the biggest mistakes I see with people whether it's LinkedIn content or even YouTube content" — creators post "a bunch of videos of like case studies... that's great but it's probably not... going to get [more than] a few hundred views realistically" (00:32:41–00:32:53, `transcript.txt`).
- Personal stories with no business relevance: "the main mistake that people make when storytelling... is that they get too broad that they forget to talk about their business themselves or what they actually help people with" (00:33:47–00:34:00, `transcript.txt`); the cringe-inducing version is a bare "Oh my god, my dad died" post with "a selfie of this girl" and no bridge to a buyer lesson (00:36:46–00:36:55, `transcript.txt`).
- Careless or spammy tagging: "LinkedIn hates spam... tagging people that you know you can't reach, that you don't have on your phone to message" damages reach and draws client complaints (00:38:57–00:39:07, `transcript.txt`).
- Trend jacking with no point of view: the source's only approved version pairs a trend with the creator's own take and a bridge — "he's educating on what's happening as well. And then he actually turned this into a lead magnet" (00:42:53, `transcript.txt`); a trend reference without that owned insight and bridge is out of spec.
- Lead magnets that collect attention but do not bridge to the paid offer: see `references/hidden-knowledge.md`, "The Real Product Is The Bridge" — the source's structural claim that the lead magnet is "the visible bridge between public expertise and a paid transformation."
- Scheduling posts and disappearing: "when you are choosing to schedule it, you're also choosing to forget about it... the post has a higher chance of dying" without a reply window (00:57:33–00:57:45, `transcript.txt`).
