## Execution

### Phase 1: The Narrative Haunting Map

Every narrative has three potential haunting layers. Map them before writing:

| Layer | What It Is | Where It Lives |
|-------|-----------|---------------|
| **The Image** | A single visual/sensory detail so precise and estranged that it embeds permanently | A specific moment — often peripheral, almost incidental |
| **The Threshold** | An unnamed state between two named states — the reader recognizes it but has never had language for it | The emotional center of the narrative — the moment of transformation |
| **The Sentence** | One syntactic construction so unique to this consciousness that it becomes the reader's internal language for the subject | Can live anywhere — often where the writer's thinking surprises even the writer |

**ALL THREE must be present for narrative haunting.** One haunting image in a flat narrative is a good line in a forgettable piece. One threshold in newspaper prose is a perception without a delivery mechanism. One sentence without a governing perception is virtuosity without purpose.

### Phase 2: Build the Image

The image is not the most beautiful moment — it's the most SPECIFIC moment. The detail that no one else would have noticed, described through behavioral displacement.

**Process**:
1. Identify the key visual/sensory moment in the narrative
2. What BEHAVIOR does this image exhibit? (Not appearance — behavior)
3. Cross-domain displacement: What from an unrelated world behaves identically?
4. Write the image through the displacement
5. Species Test: Google the core comparison. If 300K+ results, push deeper.

### Phase 3: Build the Threshold

The threshold is the heart — the unnamed moment the narrative exists to capture.

**Process** (from the Poietic Threshold Writer):
1. Map the two named states the narrative moves between (A → B)
2. What exists between A and B? The unnamed, undefined space
3. Observation: What HAPPENS in the threshold? What sensory data exists there?
4. Write the threshold using specific perceptual data, not metaphor for emotion

The narrative EXISTS to make this threshold visible. Every scene before it builds toward it; every scene after it resonates from it.

### Phase 4: Build the Sentence

The sentence is the thumbprint — the syntactic construction unique to this consciousness.

**Rules**:
- It should be identifiable as YOUR writing in a blind test
- It should carry the narrative's core perception in its STRUCTURE (not just its content)
- The syntax should enact what it describes — if the sentence is about disruption, the syntax should disrupt; if about accumulation, the syntax should accumulate
- It does NOT need to be the "most important" sentence. It needs to be the most DISTINCTIVE one

### Phase 5: Strategic Denial at Narrative Scale

At sentence level, denial is a single pivot. At narrative level, denial is structural:

1. **The Pattern Build** (first 40-60%): Establish the narrative's apparent trajectory. Let the reader build a prediction about what this story is ABOUT and HOW it will resolve.
2. **The Structural Denial** (60-75%): Break the pattern. Not a plot twist — a PERCEPTION twist. The reader realizes they've been seeing the story through the wrong lens.
3. **The Haunting Resolution** (75-100%): Deliver the actual story — which is richer, stranger, and more true than what the reader predicted. The haunting image, threshold, and sentence live here.

### Phase 6: Mimetic Scaffolding

After the three haunting elements are built, add the necessary mimetic architecture — the "normal" narrative prose that creates context, pacing, and accessibility. The scaffolding should be:
- Deliberately flat where flatness creates contrast
- Functional where function serves the haunting elements
- Brief — the scaffolding exists only to make the haunting visible

**Ratio**: 60-70% mimetic scaffolding, 30-40% poietic/haunting moments. The haunting elements work BECAUSE they're surrounded by normalcy. A piece that's 100% haunting is exhausting and nothing stands out.

### Phase 7: The Narrative Haunting Battery

- [ ] **The Image Test**: Can you describe the piece's central image to someone who hasn't read it, and watch them re-see something?
- [ ] **The Threshold Test**: Does the piece capture a moment the reader recognizes but has never had language for?
- [ ] **The Sentence Test**: Could you identify this writer from this sentence alone?
- [ ] **The Layering Test**: Do image + threshold + sentence work TOGETHER, or does any one element feel disconnected?
- [ ] **The Structural Denial Test**: Is there a moment where the reader realizes the story is about something other than what they predicted?
- [ ] **The Diachronic Test**: Read this alongside a canonical writer in a similar mode. Does this piece have its own territory?
- [ ] **The Browning Test**: Will someone think about this narrative every other day for 20 years?

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: Key risks at narrative scale:
> - **Overloading**: Too many haunting moments and nothing haunts — the reader can't hierarchize. Three is the maximum. Often one is enough.
> - **Purple prose**: Extended "literary" writing that mistakes lush description for haunting. Haunting is PRECISE and SPECIFIC, not ornate.
> - **Losing the story**: The narrative techniques shouldn't overwhelm the narrative itself. If the reader can see the technique but not the story, the craft is showing.
> - **Daring deficit**: At narrative scale, the Skater's Mindset matters most. If you haven't risked something — a perception that might be "too much," a sentence that might confuse, a threshold that might not land — the narrative will be competent but not haunting.
