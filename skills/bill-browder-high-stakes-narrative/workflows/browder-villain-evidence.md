# Villain Through Evidence — The Receipt You Can't Sue (Bill Browder)

Browder had a problem most writers never face: his villain could sue him. The lawyer who had switched sides to the Russian mafia was still a lawyer, and "would sue me" for anything wrong. Browder couldn't interview him, couldn't characterize him, couldn't put a single adjective on him that a libel attorney wouldn't turn into a payday. So he did the one thing the lawyer couldn't touch: "I had to rely entirely on the transcripts, the court transcripts. But the court transcripts told everything." The most damning portrait of a traitor ever written, and Browder never once calls him a traitor. The transcript does. That is the whole device. **You cannot be sued for the receipt.** The accusation that lives in your own voice is an opinion a lawyer can attack; the accusation that lives in the villain's own deposition is a fact the villain already swore to. This workflow builds a real adversary into a concrete, morally-clear villain rendered *entirely* through their own words, actions, and documents — so the reader feels the pull without being told whom to hate, and the portrait survives a hostile lawyer reading it line by line.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this workflow's failure mode is the most legally and morally dangerous in the entire skill — the accusation slipping back into your own voice):

- **Pattern 8 — Good Guys and Bad Guys, Rendered Not Caricatured** — the moral-structure half: a clear someone-to-root-against, rendered through evidence not adjectives, with "not too much information… a few details, because you don't have that much of people's attention." And the complexity clause: good guys can become bad guys; people turn. Never flatten a real adversary into a cartoon.
- **Pattern 9 — Evidence as the Narrative Skeleton (You Can't Sue the Transcript)** — the legal-spine half: build the accusation on a skeleton of verifiable receipts; "everything is researched." Distill the *interesting* facts from the document pile; don't dump the pile.
- **Hidden Knowledge #3 — The dangerous claim is told through the document, never the assertion** — "the most explosive accusations in his books are the ones he never makes in his own voice." Route the accusation through the receipt.
- **Hidden Knowledge #2 — Publicity-as-safety** — the deeper logic: telling the truth *loudly and provably* is a shield, not just a risk. A well-evidenced villain portrait raises the cost of retaliation; a sloppy one is an invitation to sue.
- **Decision Framework Q6** — the gate: *"Is the villain rendered through evidence, not adjectives — and not a caricature? Can the accusation survive a lawyer reading it?"*
- **Bill Browder Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)** — especially **AN-4 (The Villain as Cartoon, or the Accusation in Your Own Voice)** — flattening a real adversary into a strawman, or making explosive claims by authorial assertion instead of evidence. Also **AN-3 (describing for the sake of describing)** — the villain gets a few damning particulars, not a character study.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` — specifically **Q6** — before you write a single line of the portrait. Confirm four things: **(1)** the adversary is *real* — a named competitor, a documented system, an actual incumbent — and the conduct you're attributing to them is *true* (honesty spine; you cannot evidence a thing that didn't happen); **(2)** you have, or can get, the *receipts* — their own words, their own documented actions, dated records — that carry the accusation (Pattern 9; if you have none, route to `verify` / `deep-research` to gather them BEFORE drafting); **(3)** the accusation will live in the *evidence*, not in your adjectives — you are quoting and showing, not asserting (AN-4); **(4)** you will allow real complexity — the villain is concrete and a few details deep, never a cartoon (Pattern 8, AN-3). If you cannot answer (2) — if all you have is your own conviction that someone is the bad guy — **stop**. That is not a writing problem; it is a receipt problem. Gather evidence first, or render no villain at all. A confident accusation with no receipt is the exact thing that gets sued, and the exact thing a hostile reader dismisses as a smear.

## Input Required

- **The adversary** — *who or what* the villain is. A named competitor, a person, a system, a status quo, an incumbent, an institution. State it plainly. (e.g. "the data broker selling our users' info"; "the legacy CRM everyone's locked into"; "the certification body gatekeeping the industry.")
- **The accusation** — the *one core charge* against them, in a single plain sentence. What did they do, to whom, that makes them the bad guy? (e.g. "they switched sides and used the deposition to gather information that could get me killed"; "they raise prices the moment you can't leave"; "they charge $4,000 for a credential that teaches nothing.") This is the thing the receipts must prove — not your feelings about it.
- **The receipts available** (honesty/legal spine) — what *documentary evidence* do you have or can get: their own public statements, marketing copy, contracts, terms of service, depositions, court records, dated price changes, screenshots, on-record quotes, their own filings. List what you HAVE vs. what you NEED. If the NEED column has the load-bearing items, route to `verify` / `deep-research` first.
- **The good guy** — who or what the reader roots *for* against this villain (the everyman, the customer, the founder, the wronged party). The moral structure needs both poles; a villain with no victim is just a target.
- **Format** — book/long-form, founder origin, marketing campaign, converting copy, social post, advocacy piece. (Drives how many receipts you can carry and how the accusation gets staged — a book can run a full deposition thread; a LinkedIn post carries one screenshot.)
- **Litigation exposure** (optional but strong) — is this adversary the type to sue, complain, or retaliate? If yes, the legal-spine standard is non-negotiable: every charge in evidence, zero in your voice. (Browder's was a lawyer. Assume yours reads it with a highlighter.)

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Separate the accusation from the adjective (the smear diagnostic)

Before you gather anything, write the accusation in one plain sentence. Then run the diagnostic that catches the failure *this* workflow exists to prevent: the slide from *showing what they did* to *naming what they are*. The first is evidence; the second is a smear a lawyer can sue and a skeptic can dismiss.

| What you wrote | What kind of claim it is | Sue-able / Dismissible? | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| "They're a predatory, dishonest company." | Adjective in YOUR voice — opinion/characterization | Yes — pure libel bait; a skeptic reads it as your bias | Replace with the *act* that earns the adjective, in evidence (Step 3) |
| "They raised the price 340% the day after the contract auto-renewed." | A dated, documentable *fact* | No — it's true and provable; the reader supplies "predatory" | Keep. This is the spine. |
| "Everyone knows they screw their customers." | Hearsay / vibe in your voice | Yes — unsupported, weak | Find the receipt that shows one customer getting screwed, on the record |
| "In their own deposition, the lawyer admitted he was now working for the other side." | The villain's *own words* | No — you can't be sued for what they swore to | Keep. This is the most powerful kind. |

The rule, stated once and nailed: **let the reader supply the adjective.** Browder never calls the lawyer a traitor; he shows the lawyer switching sides in the lawyer's own deposition, and the reader's gut writes "traitor" — and the reader's own conclusion is something the lawyer cannot sue. Your job is not to convict. Your job is to lay the evidence so cleanly that the reader convicts, and feels it was their idea.

> **The smear test:** read every sentence about the villain and ask — *is this a thing they did/said (showable), or a thing I'm calling them (assertion)?* Highlight every assertion. Each highlight is either deleted or converted to the underlying act-in-evidence. If you can't find the act, you don't have the accusation — you have an opinion.

### Step 2 — Build the receipt inventory (Pattern 9: everything is researched)

"Everything is researched. The outline is in the head, but all the details are in the research." Before you render anything, inventory the evidence — because the portrait is built on the skeleton of what you can prove, not on what you believe. Sort every potential receipt by the two axes that matter: *can the villain dispute it* and *is it damning*.

| Receipt | Source (where it lives) | Disputable? (can they deny it's real?) | Damningness (does it carry the charge?) | Use? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (the villain's own quote / filing / TOS clause / price record) | (public statement, contract, court doc, screenshot, dated record) | (No = best — it's *their* artifact) | (High / Medium / Low) | (Keep / Cut) |

The hierarchy of receipts, strongest to weakest — **prefer the top of this list ruthlessly**:

1. **The villain's own words** (their deposition, their public statement, their marketing copy, their tweet, their earnings call). Unsuable — they said it. This is "the court transcripts told everything." The villain becomes the witness against themselves.
2. **The villain's own documented actions** (a dated price change, a contract clause they wrote, a product they shipped, a filing they made). The artifact is theirs; the inference is the reader's.
3. **A third-party record** (court ruling, regulator finding, audited data, a customer's on-record account). One step removed, still solid.
4. **Your own characterization** — last resort, used almost never, and only labeled as opinion. This is the layer that gets sued.

Then **distill, don't dump** (Pattern 9). Browser read a month of transcripts and pulled the *interesting* things — he did not reprint the deposition. From your inventory, the portrait will use the 3–5 most damning, least-disputable receipts. The rest are your backstop if challenged, not your text. A wall of evidence reads as a screed; three perfect receipts read as a conviction.

> **If the inventory is empty or all bottom-of-list:** stop and route to `verify` / `deep-research` to gather their own words and documented actions BEFORE proceeding. A villain portrait with no top-tier receipts is not a portrait — it's the smear the gate exists to block.

### Step 3 — Render the villain through the evidence (Pattern 8 + the accusation-via-document move)

Now write the portrait — and write it as Browder did: the accusation never appears in your voice; it appears in *their* artifact, and your prose only sets the stage for the receipt to speak. The pattern is always the same three-beat move:

| Beat | What it does | Browder's example | Your equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Frame the artifact** (your voice, neutral) | Tell the reader what they're about to see, factually, no adjective | "The deposition was supposed to be routine. But buried in it was a single exchange." | "Their pricing page in January said one thing. Here is what it said in February." |
| **2. Show the receipt** (the villain's own words/act) | Quote it, screenshot it, cite it — verbatim, sourced | The actual transcript exchange where the lawyer reveals he's working the other side | The two dated price screenshots, side by side |
| **3. Let the reader convict** (their voice, in their head) | The conclusion forms in the reader, unstated by you | The reader thinks "traitor" — Browder never wrote it | The reader thinks "they waited until I was locked in" — you never wrote it |

Then apply the two restraint rules that keep this from becoming a hit piece:

- **A few details, not a dossier (AN-3, Pattern 8):** "with not too much information… a few details, because you don't have that much of people's attention." Three damning receipts, not thirty. The villain gets the sharpest few, then you get on with it. A 2,000-word villain takedown reads as a vendetta; a three-receipt portrait reads as a verdict.
- **Allow real complexity (Pattern 8 complexity clause):** Browder's good guys sometimes become bad guys; people turn. A villain who is 100% evil with no human texture is a cartoon (AN-4), and a cartoon is *less* persuasive — the reader smells the bias and discounts the whole thing. Render the villain as a real person/system who did a real wrong: the lawyer was once *on Browder's side*; that's what makes the betrayal land. Let the complexity in; it makes the evidence hit harder, not softer.

### Step 4 — Build the moral structure: the good guy earns the bad guy (Pattern 8 + Pattern 7)

A villain with no one to harm is just a target, and a target generates no moral pull. The reader needs to root *for* someone before they can root *against* the adversary. Browder builds the good guys' "humanity and charm and lovability" so that the bad guys' "rotten shittiness" — shown in evidence — has someone to be rotten *to*.

Wire the two poles together:

| Pole | What to establish | Restraint | The link |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Good guy** (root-for) | Who they are, what they wanted, why they're worth rooting for | A few relatable details, not a biography (Pattern 4, AN-3) | The everyman thrown into something vast — established *before* the villain's blow lands (Pattern 7) |
| **Bad guy** (root-against) | Rendered through Step 3 — their own words/acts | Three receipts, not thirty | The villain's evidenced act is a wrong done *to the good guy* the reader now cares about |

The sequence matters: **establish enough caring for the good guy that the villain's evidenced act registers as a wound, not a data point.** This is why the receipt lands — not because the receipt is shocking in the abstract, but because the reader already cares about who it was done to. (For the full version of building the caring before the fall, this stacks with `browder-stakes-architecture`.)

### Step 5 — The hostile-lawyer read-through (the legal-spine gate)

This is the step that separates a villain portrait that ships from one that gets a cease-and-desist. Read the entire portrait *aloud, cold, as the villain's own attorney*, highlighter in hand, hunting for one thing: a sentence they can sue.

| The lawyer hunts for… | What they'd flag | Your defense must be |
|---|---|---|
| **An assertion of fact you can't prove** | "They defrauded their customers." | Either a receipt that proves it, or delete it |
| **An adjective masquerading as fact** | "their dishonest practices" | The documented *act*, with "dishonest" left to the reader |
| **A characterization of motive** | "they did it out of pure greed" | Show the act; never claim to know the inner state (you can't prove a motive) |
| **A quote you can't source** | "an insider told me…" | A sourced, on-record receipt — or cut it |
| **A claim that's true but not yours to make** | restating a charge no court found | Attribute to the record that found it, or drop it |

Two passes:

1. **The sue-ability pass:** every charge against the villain must trace to a receipt from Step 2. If a charge has no receipt, it is removed — not softened, *removed*. (Softening — "allegedly," "it seems" — does not protect you; an unprovable insinuation is still actionable and reads as a smear.)
2. **The opinion-labeling pass:** the *one or two* places (if any) where you offer genuine opinion are clearly framed as opinion ("In my view," "I believe") — distinct from the evidenced facts. Opinion honestly labeled is protected; opinion smuggled in as fact is the trap.

> **The publicity-as-safety logic (Hidden Knowledge #2):** Browder's protection wasn't keeping quiet — it was telling the truth so *provably* and so *loudly* that retaliation became expensive and impunity impossible. A bulletproof, evidenced villain portrait is not just legally safer; it is *strategically stronger*. The better the receipts, the less the villain can do about it. Sloppy = sue-able and weak. Airtight = unassailable and loud.

### Step 6 — The caricature check (final pass: is the villain real?)

Last, read the portrait once more and ask the question that catches the *other* failure mode — not the legal one, the credibility one. A cartoon villain is easy to write and easy for the reader to dismiss. A real one is harder and lands.

- **The complexity check:** Is there any human texture, any specificity, any sign this is a real person/system and not a strawman? Browder's lawyer was once *on his side* — the betrayal has dimension. If your villain is 100% mustache-twirling evil, the reader smells the bias and discounts the evidence along with it.
- **The fairness check:** Would a *fair* observer who saw only your receipts reach your conclusion? If you had to cherry-pick or strip context to make the charge land, that's not evidence — it's manipulation, and it's the dishonest-spine breach. The receipts must mean what you say they mean *in context*.
- **The "let them convict" check:** Re-read for any place you told the reader whom to hate. The villain portrait works when the reader arrives at the verdict themselves. If you find yourself supplying the adjective, delete it and trust the receipt.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Social media** (LinkedIn / X) | One receipt, surgically chosen. A single screenshot, a single quote, a single dated fact — framed neutrally in one line, shown, and the reader convicts in their own head. The accusation NEVER appears in your post text; it appears in the artifact you're holding up. ("Here's what their pricing page said in January. Here's February." Then stop.) At this length, one adjective in your voice = the whole post reads as a rant. Let the screenshot do the work. |
| **Marketing** (campaign / positioning) | The villain is usually the *status quo* or the *incumbent category*, not a named company (naming invites the lawsuit; the category is fair game). Render it through *its own* evidence: the incumbent's own marketing claims vs. their own fine print; the category's own documented failures; the status quo's own numbers. Build investment in the customer (good guy) first; the category's evidenced failure is then a wound done to them. Excavate the buried drama in a dry category (stacks with `narrative-warfare`). |
| **Copywriting** (VSL / landing / email) | Proof-as-narrative against the villain: route every "the old way fails you" claim through a receipt the reader recognizes (the renewal email that raised their rate, the contract clause nobody reads, the dated data). The villain is the thing keeping the reader stuck; the receipt makes it concrete and unsuable. Lead with the emotion the ICP already feels toward it (betrayal by a vendor, fear of being locked in). Honesty spine: the receipts must be real; a fabricated villain-receipt is the worst failure in copy. Stacks with `proof-architecture-builder`. |
| **Ghostwriting** (a client's origin / founder story) | The founder's adversary — the boss who blocked them, the industry that gatekept, the incumbent they left to fight. Render it through that adversary's *documented* conduct (the policy, the email, the public statement, the dated decision), never through the founder's resentment in their own voice. Reconstruct the receipts with the client (the recovered document, the saved email). The founder is the everyman thrown into something vast; the villain is what they were thrown against. Heavy litigation exposure here — apply Step 5 hard. |
| **Content** (investigative / long-form / advocacy) | Native habitat — the full Browder move. Build the accusation on a skeleton of receipts (Pattern 9), run a thread of the villain's own words and documented acts, distill the *interesting* few from the document pile, and let the evidence carry every charge. The reader should finish convinced and the villain unable to point to a single sue-able line. Gather the receipts via `verify` / `deep-research` first; this format demands the most evidence and the least authorial voice. |

## Output Format

Deliver exactly this:

```
ADVERSARY: __________ (named competitor / system / status quo / incumbent)
THE ACCUSATION (one plain sentence — what they did, to whom): __________
THE GOOD GUY (root-for): __________
LITIGATION EXPOSURE: [Low / Medium / High — will they sue or retaliate?]
HONESTY/LEGAL SPINE: every charge below traces to a receipt; zero charges in authorial voice [confirmed]

— RECEIPT INVENTORY (the 3–5 that carry the portrait) —
  1. [Receipt] · Source: [where] · Type: [their words / their act / 3rd-party] · Disputable: [No/Yes] · Damning: [H/M/L]
  2. …
  3. …
  (Backstop receipts held in reserve, not in text: ______)

— THE VILLAIN PORTRAIT (rendered through evidence) —
  [Frame the artifact, neutral voice]
  [Show the receipt — verbatim quote / cited act / screenshot reference]
  [Beat where the reader convicts — the conclusion you do NOT state]
  … (repeat for 3 receipts max; a few details, not a dossier)

— THE MORAL STRUCTURE —
  Good guy established (caring built before the blow): __________
  The villain's evidenced act, as a wound done to the good guy: __________

— HOSTILE-LAWYER READ-THROUGH —
  Every charge traces to a receipt: [confirmed]
  Charges removed for lack of receipt: __________ (or "none")
  Opinion clearly labeled as opinion (if any): __________ (or "none — all evidence")

— CARICATURE CHECK —
  Human texture / complexity present (not a cartoon): __________
  A fair observer of these receipts reaches this verdict: [confirmed]
  No adjective supplied in author voice — reader convicts: [confirmed]

RECOMMENDED LEAD RECEIPT: #__ — because __________
```

### Worked example A — Marketing (the villain is a category, rendered through its own evidence)

**Adversary:** the legacy project-management SaaS the reader is locked into. · **Accusation:** it raises prices the moment you're too embedded to leave, and buries the auto-renewal that traps you. · **Good guy:** the ops lead who chose it in good faith and now can't get out. · **Litigation exposure:** High (named-company adjacent — so we render the *category behavior*, sourced).

**Receipt inventory (top-tier only):**
1. The incumbent's *own* pricing page, archived Jan vs. Nov (Wayback) — 41% increase. Type: their documented act. Disputable: No. Damning: High.
2. The incumbent's *own* Terms of Service, §7.3 — auto-renewal with a 60-day cancellation window that opens 90 days out. Type: their own words. Disputable: No. Damning: High.
3. The incumbent's *own* earnings call transcript — CFO touting "best-in-class net revenue retention driven by contractual stickiness." Type: their own words. Disputable: No. Damning: High.

**The villain portrait (through evidence):**
> They'll tell you it's about value. Here is their own pricing page in January. (screenshot) Here it is in November. (screenshot) Same product. Forty-one percent more.
>
> You might wonder why you didn't just leave when the price jumped. Here's why — section 7.3 of the contract you signed. (quote) The window to cancel opens ninety days before renewal and closes thirty days later. Miss it by a day and you're in for another year.
>
> And in case you think that's an accident, here is their own CFO, on their own earnings call, explaining the strategy to investors: "net revenue retention driven by contractual stickiness."

*Reader's unstated verdict:* "They designed the trap and bragged about it to shareholders." — We never wrote "predatory." The earnings call did. **Note what's absent:** no adjective in our voice, no claim of motive we can't prove (the CFO supplied the motive, on the record), no named company we'd have to defend in court. Three receipts, all theirs, all unsuable. A fair observer reaches the verdict; the incumbent can't point to a single line to sue.

### Worked example B — Long-form / advocacy (the Browder move itself, generalized)

**Adversary:** a certification body charging $4,000 for an industry credential. · **Accusation:** the credential is required by no one, teaches nothing testable, and exists to fund the body's executives. · **Good guy:** the early-career worker who scraped together the fee believing it was the gate to the job. · **Litigation exposure:** High (a body with lawyers and a reputation to defend — assume the highlighter).

**Receipt inventory (top-tier only):**
1. The body's *own* exam blueprint, published — 100% multiple-choice, open-book, no proctoring. Type: their own document. Disputable: No. Damning: High.
2. The body's *own* IRS Form 990 — executive compensation, total program-service expense vs. revenue. Type: their own filing. Disputable: No. Damning: High.
3. Three job postings from the field, on the record, listing the credential as "preferred, not required." Type: third-party record. Disputable: No. Damning: Medium.

**The villain portrait (through evidence):**
> She paid four thousand dollars because the website said it was "the industry standard." So let's look at the exam she paid for. Here is the body's own published blueprint. (cite) One hundred multiple-choice questions. Open book. Unproctored. You cannot fail it if you can read.
>
> Where does the four thousand dollars go? Their own tax filing answers that. (cite Form 990) The three top officers drew a combined [$X]. Program services — the actual teaching — accounted for [Y%] of revenue.
>
> And the job she was told required it? Here are three postings from the field this month. (cite) "Preferred. Not required."

*Reader's unstated verdict:* "It's a tollbooth wearing a graduation gown." — We never called it a scam. Its own exam, its own 990, and three job listings did. The good guy — established first as the worker who believed the website — turns the receipts from data into a wound. **The hostile-lawyer read-through holds:** every charge is the body's own document or a public posting; no motive claimed beyond what the 990 shows in dollars; no adjective in our voice. The publicity-as-safety logic applies — the more provable this is, the less they can do about it.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Bill Browder Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` and § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (rows: **Villain Rendering & Evidence**, **Stakes & Honesty Spine**, **Description Restraint**). Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Accusation in evidence, not voice (AN-4, Pattern 9):** every consequential charge against the villain traces to a receipt — their own words, their own act, or a sourced third-party record. ZERO charges live in your adjectives. Read every villain sentence: is it a thing they did (showable) or a thing you're calling them (assertion)? Every assertion is deleted or converted. The reader supplies the adjective.
- **Survives the hostile lawyer (Step 5, Hidden Knowledge #3):** read cold as the villain's attorney. Every charge has a receipt; every opinion is labeled as opinion; no motive is claimed that can't be proven; no quote is unsourced. If a charge can't trace to a receipt, it's *removed*, not softened. (Litigation exposure High = this gate is non-negotiable.)
- **Not a caricature (Pattern 8, AN-4):** the villain has human texture and specificity — a real person/system who did a real wrong, not a 100%-evil strawman. There is complexity allowed (the good guy who turned, the once-trusted ally). A cartoon villain reads as bias and gets the whole portrait discounted.
- **A few receipts, not a dossier (AN-3, Pattern 8):** 3–5 damning, least-disputable receipts carry the portrait; the rest are backstop, not text. "Not too much information… a few details." A wall of evidence reads as a vendetta; three perfect receipts read as a verdict.
- **The moral structure is wired (Pattern 8, Pattern 7):** there is a good guy the reader roots *for*, established with enough caring that the villain's evidenced act lands as a wound, not a data point. A villain with no victim is a target with no pull.
- **Top-tier receipts preferred (Pattern 9):** the portrait leans on the villain's *own* words and documented acts (unsuable, self-witnessing) over third-party records over — almost never — your own characterization. If the inventory was empty or all bottom-tier, evidence was gathered via `verify` / `deep-research` BEFORE drafting.
- **Honesty/fairness spine intact:** the receipts mean what you say they mean *in context*; nothing is cherry-picked or stripped of context to manufacture a charge. The villain is real, the conduct is true, the evidence is genuine. (A fabricated or distorted receipt is the single most dangerous output this workflow can produce.)
- **The reader convicts (Step 6):** nowhere did you tell the reader whom to hate. The verdict forms in them, from the evidence, as their own conclusion.

## Common Pitfalls

- **The accusation slides into your voice (AN-4 — the cardinal sin).** You start showing the receipts, then a sentence like "this is exactly the kind of dishonest, predatory behavior we've come to expect from them" slips in — and now the whole portrait is a smear a lawyer can sue and a skeptic can dismiss. **Recovery:** run the smear test (Step 1). Highlight every sentence that *characterizes* the villain rather than *shows* what they did. Delete or convert each to the underlying act-in-evidence. Trust the receipt; the reader will supply the word you wanted to write — and the reader's word is unsuable.

- **No receipts — just conviction.** You're certain the adversary is the bad guy, so you write the portrait from that certainty, with vague gestures at "their well-known practices" and "everyone knows." This is the smear the gate exists to block, and it's both legally exposed and persuasively weak. **Recovery:** stop drafting. Run the Step 2 inventory honestly — if the HAVE column is empty or all bottom-tier, route to `verify` / `deep-research` to gather their own words and documented acts. No top-tier receipts means no villain portrait; render one anyway and you've written exactly the actionable smear Browder spent a month of transcript-reading to avoid.

- **The cartoon villain (AN-4, credibility breach).** You flatten the adversary into pure mustache-twirling evil — no texture, no complexity, no humanity — because it's easier and feels more satisfying. The reader smells the bias and discounts your evidence along with it. **Recovery:** find the complexity (Step 6). Was the villain once an ally? Is the system one the reader themselves chose in good faith? Browder's lawyer was *on his side* before he switched — that's what makes the betrayal land. A real villain who did a real wrong is more damning than a cartoon, because the reader believes you.

- **The dossier (AN-3, AN-1 stacking).** You found thirty damning receipts, so you use all thirty — and the portrait reads as a vendetta, an obsessive screed that makes the *writer* look unhinged rather than the villain look guilty. **Recovery:** distill (Step 2, Pattern 9). Pick the 3–5 most damning, least-disputable receipts; hold the rest as backstop. Browder read a month of transcripts and used the *interesting* few. Three perfect receipts convict; thirty mediocre ones acquit by exhaustion.
