# Daniel Pink — Writing & Book Structure

Daniel Pink (Drive, When, The Power of Regret, To Sell Is Human — six NYT bestsellers over 30 years) treats writing as engineering: you are building something that has to work, and you stress-test it like a structure under load. His system runs idea → socialization → 30-40 page proposal-as-test → months of structure hunting → daily word-count production, chapter at a time — and every piece is governed by one question: what is the promise I'm making to the reader, and does every page pay it off?

## Quick Reference

- **Structure before sentences.** Pink cannot write until he sees the skeleton. Months of research go to finding the organizing principle; drafting starts only after a framework survives staring at the wall.
- **The proposal is the test.** If an idea can't withstand a 30-page proposal, it can't withstand a 300-page book. And if you hate writing the proposal, you'll hate writing the book. Kill early — 10 days, not a signed contract.
- **Fresh yet familiar.** The pitch for *When*: "We have lots of how-to books. We need a when-to book." New enough to matter, recognizable enough to want.
- **Socialize ideas.** Talk about the idea and read the room: dead eyes vs. questions vs. "I disagree" — all three are data.
- **Consilience is the generalist's edge.** Different disciplines asking the same question without talking to each other = a book. One obscure paper (green socks) = skepticism; converging piles across fields = include it.
- **Ruthless inclusion.** Three weeks of research can rightfully become one paragraph. The reader's need decides, not your sunk cost.
- **The reader promise.** A book costs $25 and nine hours of someone's life. Win = they think differently AND do different stuff. Useful beats merely interesting.
- **Routine is liberating.** Fixed time, fixed place, daily word count (even 500), no phone, chapter at a time. Consistency over intensity — the tortoise won.
- **Write to figure it out.** When you don't know what you think, drafting IS the thinking. (Charlie Yarnoff: "Sometimes you have to write to figure it out.")
