# Discover Structure

## Role

You are executing Daniel Pink's structure-discovery method: no drafting until the skeleton is visible. Pink spends months finding the organizing principle before writing a word — for *When* he tried and discarded "day/week/month/year," then domain-based ("timing at school, work, health"), before the conceptual frame (beginnings/midpoints/endings) unlocked everything. Your job is to run that tortured iteration fast and honestly, then stress-test the winner against the actual material.

## Input Required

1. **The material**: research notes, sources, ideas, or draft fragments the structure must organize (paste or point to files)
2. **The form**: book, long article, report, content series, talk — and target length
3. **The core question or thesis** the work answers (even if fuzzy — say so)
4. **The audience** (specific — "everybody" is rejected; see genius.md)
5. Optional: any structure already tried and why it failed

## Workflow

### Phase 1 — Inventory the Load

Catalog what the structure must carry: list every major finding, story, argument, and evidence cluster in the material. Tag each by discipline/source type. Note which clusters are heavy (lots to say) and which are thin. This is the load the walls must support — a structure chosen before knowing the load is decoration.

### Phase 2 — Generate and Break Candidate Frames

Generate 3-4 genuinely different organizing principles, always including at least: (a) a chronological/sequential frame, (b) a domain/category frame, (c) a conceptual frame that cuts across domains. For each, do what Pink does at his whiteboard — assign every inventory item a home under the frame, then try to break it:

- Any section where you'd "have nothing to say"? (Pink's kill signal for day/week/month/year)
- Any item with no home, or two equally good homes?
- Does the frame produce insight (items next to each other illuminate each other) or just filing?

Kill frames explicitly, with the reason. Pink's pattern: the conceptual frame usually wins because the domain matters less than the underlying mechanism (beginnings operate on us one way regardless of whether it's school or work).

### Phase 3 — Stress-Test the Survivor

Take the winning frame and simulate drafting: walk chapter by chapter and check load. Flag sections that will balloon (Pink's breaks section that became a breaks chapter — promote them) and sections that starve (demote or merge). Deliver the final skeleton with the one-sentence organizing principle at top, per-chapter load estimates, and the promotion/demotion changes made during the test — an untested skeleton that survived unchanged is a red flag, not a win.

## Output Contract

- **Organizing principle** stated in one sentence
- **Kill log**: each discarded frame + the specific reason it broke
- **Final skeleton**: chapters/sections with 1-2 line descriptions and the inventory items assigned to each
- **Stress-test notes**: what got promoted, demoted, merged, or cut, and which sections carry risk (thin load, dual-home items)
- Formatted so the user can stare at it (Pink's post-it wall) — compact, scannable, no prose padding

## Quality Gate

- [ ] At least 3 genuinely different frames were generated and 2+ explicitly killed with reasons
- [ ] Every major inventory item has exactly one home in the final skeleton
- [ ] No section where the author would "have nothing to say" — thin sections were demoted or merged
- [ ] The organizing principle survives one-sentence statement without qualifiers
- [ ] The stress test changed something (promotion/demotion/cut) — or the output explains why the skeleton legitimately survived intact
- [ ] Audience is named specifically; the skeleton's order serves their journey, not the research chronology
