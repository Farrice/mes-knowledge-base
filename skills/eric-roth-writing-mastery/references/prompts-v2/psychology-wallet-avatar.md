## Role & Activation

You are applying Eric Roth's Character Psychology Wallet technique to audience research and ICP building. Instead of demographics and pain points, build avatars with the depth of a Forrest Gump or Benjamin Button — neuroses, giddiness, telling possessions, recurring phrases, and the things they'd never admit out loud. The wallet is the difference between an avatar you forget and one that "stays in your soul."

## Input Required

- **[BUSINESS/AUDIENCE CONTEXT]**: who the avatar serves or represents (product, service, niche)
- **[EXISTING DEMOGRAPHIC ICP]** (if any): traditional profile for reference/contrast
- **[KNOWN PATTERNS]** (optional): any real customer/client observations already on hand

## Execution Protocol

### The Wallet vs. The Profile
Contrast, to calibrate depth:

| Traditional ICP | Psychology Wallet |
|---|---|
| Age: 35-45 | She's 38 but tells people 36 because there's a difference she can't articulate |
| Income: $80-120K | She checks her bank balance every morning before coffee — not because she's broke, but because controlling the number controls the anxiety |
| Pain: "Needs more leads" | She's terrified the business is actually working and she's the bottleneck |
| Goal: "Scale to 7 figures" | She wants to prove her mother wrong about "the internet job" without ever having to say it out loud |

### Phase 1 — The Physical Wallet
If the avatar emptied their pockets/bag right now, what would be found?
1. **The phone** — 3 most-used apps; the notification they dread; the text they haven't responded to
2. **The worn item** — something carried that they don't need anymore but can't throw away
3. **The crumpled paper** — a receipt, a note, a number; why they still have it
4. **The photo** — who's in it, when it was taken, why it's there
5. **The secret** — something in the wallet/bag nobody else knows about

### Phase 2 — The Psychological Wallet
**Neuroses (3 minimum)**: what they check compulsively; what conversation replay keeps them up; what habit they hide from their spouse/partner.

**Giddiness (3 minimum)**: what secretly delights them that they'd be embarrassed to admit; what YouTube hole they fall into at midnight; what small victory makes them disproportionately happy.

**Anger Triggers (3 minimum)**: what advice makes them viscerally angry; what type of person they judge silently; what phrase makes them clench their jaw.

**The Saying (1)**: the phrase or belief they repeat to themselves like a mantra — their verbal tell, the line they'd say in a crisis.

### Phase 3 — The Voice Test
Write three sentences in this person's actual voice — not about them, *as* them:
1. What they'd text their best friend about their business struggle (informal, real)
2. What they'd post on LinkedIn about the same struggle (polished, performed)
3. What they'd say aloud at 2 AM when nobody's listening (raw, unfiltered)

The gap between these three versions IS the avatar's psychology.

### Phase 4 — The Scene Test
Write a single paragraph of visual prose describing this person's morning (Pattern 7 — what does their world feel and sound like). Include: time they wake and why that time; the first thing they look at; the physical environment (light, sound, temperature); the moment they first think about work. This paragraph should make anyone who reads it say "you're describing *me*."

### Phase 5 — The Telling Detail
Identify the one detail that says more about this person than any demographic data could — the crumpled phone number, the repeated gesture, the specific brand of coffee. The difference between "a stressed entrepreneur" and "someone who alphabetizes their spice rack at midnight because it's the only thing in their life they can control."

## Output Contract

Deliver, in order:
1. Physical Wallet — the literal contents inventory
2. Psychological Wallet — neuroses (3+), giddiness (3+), anger triggers (3+), the saying (1)
3. Voice Test — the three sentences showing the voice gap
4. Morning Scene — the visual prose paragraph
5. Telling Detail — the single most revealing detail
6. Traditional ICP — conventional demographics/pain points, for reference
7. Usage Guide — how to use the wallet when writing content, copy, or campaigns for this avatar

Quality standard: someone should read the wallet and feel uncomfortable — like it describes them too precisely. If the avatar feels generic, the wallet isn't deep enough.

## Quality Gate

- [ ] Does every wallet item name a specific detail (a brand, a phrase, a time, an object) rather than a generalized trait?
- [ ] Does the Voice Test show a genuine gap between the three registers, not three versions of the same sentence?
- [ ] Does the Morning Scene pass the "close your eyes" test — could a reader place themselves physically in it?
- [ ] Is the Telling Detail something demographic data could never surface — i.e., not restated income/age/title?
- [ ] Are neuroses, giddiness, and anger triggers each at their 3-item minimum, not padded with near-duplicates?

## Creative Latitude

This deliverable rewards psychological specificity over statistical plausibility — invented details (the spice rack, the karaoke bar) should feel like they were observed, not generated, even when composited from patterns rather than a single real person (per the Fanciful Real principle: psychologically true, circumstantially chosen for resonance). Push past the first neurosis or giddiness item that comes to mind — the third item in each category is usually the one that surprises. The Telling Detail is the deliverable's centerpiece; do not settle for a detail that merely illustrates a pain point already stated elsewhere in the wallet.

## Deploy When

Building ICPs, personas, or audience avatars that need to feel like real humans a writer could have dinner with — not demographic rows in a spreadsheet — before writing content, copy, or campaigns for that audience.
