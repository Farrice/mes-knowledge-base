# David Placek — Hidden Knowledge Reference

## 1. The Quantity Threshold
Most people generate 50-100 names and get stuck. Lexicon generates 2,000+. The quality curve has a nonlinear inflection point — it doesn't kick in until you push past the comfortable stopping point. Names 1-100 are the obvious ones everybody would find. The gold is in names 500-2,000 — this is where category conventions break down and genuine synchronicity produces unexpected candidates.

## 2. The Body Language Read
Placek reads physical reactions to concepts in conversation, not verbal responses. He watches posture shifts, arm crossing/uncrossing, forward leans, and energy changes in the room. In the live fiber exercise, he didn't listen to what Sean said — he watched Sean's posture lift upward when discussing lightness and said "You feel lighter." When presenting names to clients, the one that makes someone physically lean forward is communicating more than any focus group rating.

## 3. The Category Escape
The first hypothesis on any naming project: "Don't go where the category is." Before exploring any concepts, map the competitive landscape and declare it a no-go zone. If every competitor signals the same thing, that territory is saturated and invisible.
- Fiber brands all say "fiber" → don't say fiber
- Mop brands all say "mop" → you're not a mop, you're a Swiffer
- Plant-based brands want hippy/natural vibes → you need audacity (Impossible)

## 4. The Strategic Question Sequence
Four questions in exact order:
1. How do you define winning? (diagnostic — every stakeholder answers differently)
2. What do you HAVE to win? (asset inventory)
3. What do you NEED to win? (gap analysis)
4. What do you NEED TO SAY? (message architecture)
Most people skip 1-3 and start at 4. The cascade prevents the most common naming mistake: trying to say something without knowing what victory looks like.

## 5. The Competitor Courage Frame
When a name is polarizing internally, don't try to make it more comfortable — reframe it as competitive advantage. The exact phrase that sold BlackBerry: "Your current competitors — who are all big companies — would never have the courage to put BlackBerry on a device." This transforms risk-feeling into advantage-feeling. Deploy this whenever a strong name faces internal resistance.

## 6. The Re-Name Safety Net
Placek has "never seen evidence" that renaming causes lost equity or momentum — provided the launch has enthusiasm and a clear story. The biggest barrier to renaming is psychological (fear of losing equity), not empirical. Windsurf (formerly Kodium) renamed successfully — the rename itself was the launch moment. The practical threshold: if the old name is in the comfort trap, rename.
