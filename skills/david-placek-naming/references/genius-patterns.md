# David Placek — Genius Patterns Reference

## Pattern 1: Ship of Gold Treasure Hunting
**Core**: Map the entire territory before diving. Don't chase the first signal.
**How**: Generate 2,000+ candidates across Greek/Latin roots, mythology, periodic table, aviation, sports medicine, unrelated domains. Three two-person teams with divergent briefs.
**Key Quote**: "A lot of this is like treasure hunting" — inspired by a ship called Ship of Gold where the treasure hunter methodically mapped every shipwreck to find the one with the gold.

## Pattern 2: Surprisingly Familiar
**Core**: The crosshair where easy-to-process meets unexpected-in-context.
**How**: Three-part test: (1) Can they pronounce it on first read? (2) Does it evoke something recognizable? (3) Is it unexpected in this category?
**Examples**: Swiffer on a mop (swift + familiar word, surprising in cleaning). Feather on a fiber supplement (familiar concept, surprising in gut health). Impossible on plant-based meat (familiar word, surprising as a food brand).

## Pattern 3: The Comfort Trap
**Core**: Consensus without distinction = invisible zone. Polarization = energy.
**How**: Plot names on distinctiveness-vs-familiarity axis. If everyone agrees it's fine, it's in the invisible zone. If half the room hates it, ask WHY — that's where the energy is.
**Key Evidence**: Andy Grove (Intel CEO) on Pentium: "This is so polarizing — that means it has energy inside." BlackBerry team initially rejected it; the energy in their resistance was the signal.

## Pattern 4: Two-Person Teams, Three Divergent Briefs
**Core**: Eliminate brainstorming. Use structural divergence instead.
**How**: Team 1 gets the real brief. Team 2 gets a modified brief with a fictional feature. Team 3 works in a completely unrelated category. Combine all three output streams.
**Origin**: 18-month internal audit at Lexicon — best names came from individuals/pairs, never from freelancers or brainstorms.

## Pattern 5: Approximate Thinking
**Core**: Permission to produce imperfect ideas.
**How**: Draw a spectrum: bizarre (left) → approximate (middle) → safe (right). Tell team: "Stay in approximate. Don't perfect it. Don't kill it."
**Effect**: Eliminates self-censoring. People who would never share a "weird" idea will share an "approximate" one.

## Pattern 6: Sound Symbolism Engineering
**Core**: Phonemes carry measurable psychological weight.
**How**: 30+ years of cross-language research. Key mappings:
- K, P, B → reliable, fast, strong (Pentium, BlackBerry)
- Z → attention, cutting-edge (Azure, Febreze)
- X → innovation, the unknown (SpaceX, Lexus)
- V → vibrancy, movement (Vercel, Viagra)
- CVCV pattern → maximum processing fluency (Sonos, Turo)
**Tool**: Lexicon has proprietary software called "Predict" for sound symbolism scoring.

## Pattern 7: Proof-of-Concept Contextualization
**Core**: Never show names on a list. Show them in believable contexts.
**How**: Mock names into Wall Street Journal headlines, celebrity endorsement ads, product packaging. One test: "Is this believable in less than one second?"
**Why**: Stakeholders evaluate names as BRANDS (with context) rather than WORDS (abstract). This eliminates the most common source of name rejection: "it sounds weird."

## Pattern 8: The Courage Culture
**Core**: Encourage = en-courage (give courage to).
**How**: Two precision phrases:
- "I wouldn't have thought of that" (credits the person, validates novelty)
- "I wish we could make that work" (converts criticism into problem-solving)
**Separation**: Speculate phase vs. evaluate phase. Never evaluate during generation. Ideas "live in the system" before being narrowed.
