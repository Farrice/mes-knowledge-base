# Sam Parr Copywriting Mechanics - Genius Context

Load this file when the task needs Sam Parr's copywriting mechanics, not his broader taste acquisition system.

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These fourteen patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist to stamp onto a draft. Absorb them, then rewrite from behavior, not from vocabulary. The test: would Sam Parr recognize this as his own move — copy that changes what a specific reader does next — or as someone reciting "AIDA" and "curiosity gap" like exam terms? If it's the second, rebuild before shipping. This is the recognition test every output from this skill should survive.

Specifically:
- Do NOT label the delivered copy "Attention," "Interest," "Desire," "Action" — that's diagnostic scaffolding for the workflow output, never reader-facing text. Sam's own copy never announces its structure; it just moves.
- Do NOT enumerate which Sam mechanics were applied inside the final copy itself — the workflow Output Schemas ask for that as diagnosis, not as something the reader sees.
- His texture is operator-blunt, not agency-polished: a fourth-wall punch first ("You're reading this not because you want to, but because I want you to"), then earned length. A draft that reads like a copywriting textbook has failed even if every mechanic is technically present.
- Polish is the tell-class failure here specifically: Sam's own worked examples (the AGZ sleep-supplement rewrite, the Tupperware rewrite) are rough and spoken — he interrupts his own pitch mid-demo to say "everything I just said was fake." A draft too clean to have been thought aloud copied his vocabulary, not his behavior-engineering instinct.

## Core Genius

Sam treats copy as social behavior engineering. The important question is not "is this written well?" The important question is "does this move the right reader into the next state?"

That gives the system a concrete sequence:

1. Lock the desired action.
2. Create attention without losing trust.
3. Build interest line by line.
4. Let story create desire before the product arrives.
5. Put proof close to the claim.
6. Answer doubt through detail.
7. Keep rhythm alive.
8. Practice through copywork until the moves become instinct.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern 1: Desired Behavior Before Words

Source anchors: `00:00:41`, `00:01:01`.

Sam frames copy as choosing words that influence behavior. The working move is to name the reader action before touching the line.

Deploy when: copy sounds good but does not drive a next step.

### Pattern 2: AIDA As Reader-State Movement

Source anchors: `00:01:19`, `00:35:17`.

Attention, interest, desire, and action are useful only if the reader actually moves. The system should mark where movement stops.

Deploy when: a draft has a hook but no desire, or desire but no action.

### Pattern 3: Familiar Energy

Source anchors: `00:02:26`, `00:11:50`.

Copy often fails before logic begins because the speaker feels outside the reader's world. Familiar energy makes the message feel socially alive.

Deploy when: outreach, hooks, or ad copy sound like a formal announcement.

### Pattern 4: Headline Gravity

Source anchors: `00:03:01`, `00:04:24`.

The headline is the highest-leverage unit. It decides whether the rest of the work gets a chance.

Deploy when: the first line is a category label, summary, or polite setup.

### Pattern 5: Mind The Gap

Source anchors: `00:05:05`, `00:07:16`, `00:10:13`.

Good copy creates a gap the reader wants resolved. The gap must be clear enough to feel and honest enough to trust.

Deploy when: copy has information but no pull.

### Pattern 6: Interest Beats Length

Source anchors: `00:07:10`, `00:10:00`, `00:24:30`.

The lazy diagnosis is "too long." The better diagnosis is "not interesting enough." Length can stay when each section escalates.

Deploy when: a draft is being cut before checking whether it builds proof, tension, desire, or clarity.

### Pattern 7: Known Phrase, New Turn

Source anchors: `00:14:10`, `00:15:37`, `00:16:20`.

Familiar language lowers processing effort. A fresh turn creates memory.

Deploy when: a hook needs stickiness without becoming obscure.

### Pattern 8: Rhythm Creates Momentum

Source anchors: `00:17:13`, `00:19:52`, `00:20:35`.

Sentence movement is not decoration. Rhythm makes continuation easier than stopping.

Deploy when: the copy is correct but flat.

### Pattern 9: Story Carries Desire

Source anchors: `00:23:14`, `00:24:30`, `00:42:30`.

Story is not an add-on after the pitch. It creates the desire, tension, and buyer role that make the pitch make sense.

Deploy when: the product appears before the reader cares.

### Pattern 10: Copywork Builds Texture

Source anchors: `00:27:40`, `00:29:05`, `00:31:20`, `00:45:15`.

Manual reproduction exposes order, pacing, proof placement, transitions, and payoff. This is the training system behind better instincts.

Deploy when: a writer keeps asking for frameworks but lacks feel for the format.

### Pattern 11: Proof First

Source anchors: `00:33:11`, `00:34:17`, `00:34:46`.

For weak ads, proof may be the best headline. Proof earns belief faster than polished benefit language.

Deploy when: claims are generic or the buyer is skeptical.

### Pattern 12: Objection Through Detail

Source anchors: `00:40:30`, `00:42:16`.

Objections can be answered through specific anecdotes, tests, or lived details rather than defensive rebuttal.

Deploy when: the reader's private doubt is obvious.

### Pattern 13: Humor Fit

Source anchors: `00:43:56`, `00:44:05`, `00:45:15`.

Humor works when it proves humanity, confidence, or shared context. It fails when it feels imported.

Deploy when: personality could raise trust and attention without weakening seriousness.

## Signature Moves

| Move | What It Does |
|---|---|
| Action Lock | Names the behavior the copy must create before rewriting. |
| Reader-State Map | Finds where attention, interest, desire, belief, or action breaks. |
| Gap Opener | Replaces an explanatory hook with a relevant unresolved question. |
| Proof Object Swap | Replaces a benefit claim with a concrete proof asset. |
| Visual Fact Translation | Turns abstract proof into a physical or picturable comparison. |
| Story Delay | Moves the product reveal later so desire has time to build. |
| Objection Detail | Handles doubt by showing the product surviving a real situation. |
| Rhythm Map | Marks sentence movement and removes stalls. |
| Copywork Hour | Runs focused manual reproduction and rule extraction. |

## Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Pass Standard |
|---|---|
| Reader action | The desired behavior is explicit. |
| Pull | The first line creates a real reason to continue. |
| Payoff | Curiosity gaps resolve instead of dangling. |
| Proof | At least one meaningful claim has a proof object or a named proof gap. |
| Desire | Story or situation creates want before the product is pushed. |
| Objection | The biggest likely doubt is answered naturally. |
| Rhythm | The copy has sentence movement without fake short-line theater. |
| Voice | The final copy fits the brand, not Sam Parr as a costume. |
| Behavior delta | The output explains what changed for the reader. |

## Anti-Patterns (Sourced)

Every item below is source-attributed to `extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt` (68,567 bytes, plain transcript, no in-file timestamps) and cross-verified against the timestamped raw captions at `_active/harness/codex-harvest-2026-06-11/extractions/video-context/uf4fR3qcDkU/transcript.txt` (265,316 bytes). See `references/source-ledger.md` for claim-by-claim labels.

- **Leads with the product before the reader wants it.** The weak ad Sam is handed reads "Nightly Rest is within arms reach" (`extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt`) — his fix is to withhold the product entirely and open with a before/after proof image instead; naming the product before desire exists is the single most common failure this skill exists to catch (Pattern 9, Pattern 12 in `copywriting-extraction.md`).
- **Diagnoses "too long" instead of "not interesting enough."** Transcript, verbatim: "No one will read this or watch this. This is too long. No, it's not interesting enough." (`extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt`) — cutting word count without first checking whether each section escalates treats the symptom, not the cause (Pattern 6).
- **Presents improvised numbers as verified fact.** Sam catches his own demo mid-sentence: "that would probably be a pretty good ad. Now everything I just said was fake. I don't know if that's true." (`extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt`) — the architecture is the teachable artifact; any stat carried from a Sam-mechanics pass into shipped copy needs Chain Step 5.5 verification before it goes out.
- **States the reassurance instead of dissolving it with a detail.** Contrast case, verbatim: "He doesn't say they're waterproof. He says, I wore them in the shower." (`extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt`) — naming the claim directly ("waterproof") invites doubt; a lived detail reads as evidence (Pattern 12).
- **Ships the first pass as the final draft.** Transcript: "they, um, write drunk and edit sober" — then, self-quoting an editor the auto-captions render as "David Olga" (near-homophone caption garble; likely David Ogilvy, not independently confirmed by this source — see ledger), "I'm a [ __ ] writer. I'm a great editor." (`extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt`) — the first draft exists to be cut by a third, not published as-is (Pattern 10, "Cut a Third" in `copywriting-extraction.md`).
- **Commits to a joke before it's been tested.** Sam's own analogy for how he handles humor: "we always talk about this like when you meet somebody new and you start like testing the water with jokes to see what you can land, what you can't, what you have to take back." (`extractions/sam-parr/transcript.txt`) — humor deployed at full commitment without a test-read is a trust risk, not a personality flex (Pattern 13).
