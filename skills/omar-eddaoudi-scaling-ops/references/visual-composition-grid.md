# Visual Composition Grid Reference

> Per Omar: "Just take any high-performing ad creative that you have at your disposal and draw these four lines on top of it. What do you see? Almost all of them, specifically the ones from big brands, follow these lines to guide their composition."

## The 4-Line Grid

The 4-line grid divides the canvas into thirds horizontally and vertically — equivalent to the photographic rule of thirds. Apply this BEFORE any pixel placement.

```
┌──────┬──────┬──────┐
│      │      │      │
│   ★  │      │  ★   │  ← Top intersections (eye-pull zones)
│      │      │      │
├──────┼──────┼──────┤
│      │      │      │
│      │  ●   │      │  ← Center (default focal default; often weakest)
│      │      │      │
├──────┼──────┼──────┤
│      │      │      │
│   ★  │      │  ★   │  ← Bottom intersections (eye-pull zones)
│      │      │      │
└──────┴──────┴──────┘

★ = Power points (strongest visual placement)
● = Center (use sparingly; reserved for symmetric hero composition)
```

### How to Apply in Figma / Photoshop

1. Open canvas
2. Add guide lines: 33%, 66% horizontal + 33%, 66% vertical
3. Lock guides
4. Place primary element (focal point) on or near a power-point intersection
5. Align secondary elements along the lines
6. Tertiary elements (proof / badges) outside the power zones

## Focal Point Optics

> Per Omar: "What is the first thing that you notice in this image? It's the product, and that is due to something called in physics the focal point. Essentially in optics, the focal point is exactly where your eye lands when it is focusing on the image it has in front of it."

### Rule of Focal Point

For ecommerce ads selling a physical product: **the focal point MUST be the product.**

If the focal point is anything else (a model's face / a giant headline / an offer badge), the customer will struggle to identify what's being sold.

### Engineering the Focal Point

| Technique | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| **Highest contrast in scene** | Eye lands on the highest-contrast area first |
| **Brightest area** | In dark/muted compositions, the brightest spot becomes focal |
| **Convergence lines** | Lines (real or implied) that point at the product |
| **Negative space framing** | Empty space surrounding the product makes it pop |
| **Hand-holding the product** | Human hands automatically draw eye to held objects |
| **Sharpest focus** | In photo composites, sharpest area = focal point |

### Anti-Pattern: Focal Point Conflict

| Conflict | Symptom | Fix |
|----------|---------|-----|
| Headline competes with product | Customer reads headline first, then doesn't see product | Reduce headline weight OR increase product visual prominence |
| Multiple products in frame | Eye doesn't know where to land | Single product hero; if multi-product, group as one visual mass |
| Loud background | Background pulls focus | Mute background; preserve color saturation only on product |
| Big offer badge | Offer dominates, product becomes secondary | Move offer to tertiary tier (small, corner) or remove entirely |

## Color Contrast Engineering

### Primary + Secondary Palette Strategy

```
PRIMARY (brand colors)
├─ Primary color 1 (e.g., orange)
└─ Primary color 2 (e.g., navy)

SECONDARY (variation engine — NOT brand-breaking)
├─ Achromatic shifts (black / white / gray) — universal pair
├─ Analogous (color-wheel adjacent — e.g., orange + red-orange + yellow-orange)
└─ Complementary inverse (e.g., orange's complement is blue)
```

### When to Deploy Each Secondary Strategy

| Strategy | Use For | Effect |
|----------|---------|--------|
| Achromatic (B/W/Gray + brand color) | Premium aesthetic, editorial feel | High sophistication, low energy |
| Analogous (warm-warm or cool-cool) | Mood/emotion-driven creative | Cohesion, family resemblance |
| Complementary inverse | High-energy, attention-pull | Maximum contrast, scroll-stop power |

### Variation Math

One template + 3 secondary palette shifts = 3 ad variations from one design. Multiply by hook variations and you have a creative variation engine without redesigning each time.

## Visual Hierarchy 3-Element Rule

Every static breaks into three weight-tiers:

### Primary Element (Focal Point)
- **Role**: First thing the customer sees in the first 0.3-1 second
- **Engineering**: Biggest, brightest, highest contrast, on power-point intersection
- **Rule**: For ecommerce, this is almost always the product

### Secondary Element (Context)
- **Role**: Adds meaning to the primary element
- **Engineering**: Smaller than primary, supporting weight, often above or below primary
- **Common forms**: Headline that names what the product does, key benefit callout, mechanism mention

### Tertiary Element (Proof)
- **Role**: Seals the deal after primary + secondary do their work
- **Engineering**: Smallest weight, edge placement, low-saturation
- **Common forms**: Star rating, review count, "as seen in" publications, certifications, testimonials

### The Rules of Hierarchy

1. **Bigger = more attention** — primary > secondary > tertiary in physical size
2. **Brighter / more saturated / higher contrast = eye-pull** — use sparingly per tier
3. **Bold or highlighted text guides eye** — use only on primary OR secondary, never both
4. **Negative space supports hierarchy** — give primary room to breathe

## The Self-Test Hierarchy Audit

> Per Omar: "I want you to really analyze it deeply. What is the first element that pops out? What is the second element that pops out? And what is the third thing that you see when you look at your creative?"

### Audit Procedure

1. Look at your draft for exactly **1 second**, then look away
2. Write down what you remember seeing in order: 1st, 2nd, 3rd
3. Compare to your engineered intent:
   - 1st seen = intended primary?
   - 2nd seen = intended secondary?
   - 3rd seen = intended tertiary?
4. If misaligned → rearrange and re-test
5. Pass condition: order matches intent on three consecutive 1-second tests

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| Headline read first, product second | Headline weight > product weight | Shrink headline or increase product visual mass |
| Offer badge read first | Badge too prominent | Remove or shrink to tertiary |
| Nothing pops out | No clear hierarchy at all | Increase contrast on intended primary |
| Two things compete | Equal weight on multiple elements | Force one to dominate by size or contrast |

## Authority Hijacking Reference

> Per Omar: "There's nothing that makes a person want to buy something more than seeing a person they admire or look up to using that product. Authority is such an important cue because it completely hijacks all the barriers that people have."

### Authority Tier Hierarchy (Conversion Power)

1. **Niche-relevant micro-influencer** (highest conversion) — known to ICP, recognized by your customer
2. **Niche-relevant publication** ("As seen in [Industry Magazine]") — borrowed credibility from category gatekeepers
3. **Niche-relevant practitioner** (dermatologist for skincare, nutritionist for supplements) — expert credibility
4. **Out-of-niche celebrity** (Clooney for skincare) — recognition without conversion power
5. **Generic stock-photo "professional"** (zero conversion power) — avoid

### Selection Criteria

- ICP recognizes them (test: would your avatar know this person's name without context?)
- Category-relevant (do they USE products in your category? have they before?)
- Within budget (micro-influencers often outperform celebrities at 1/100th the cost)
- Authentic-feeling (forced endorsements signal as ads, lose conversion)

### Authority Slot Placement

Place authority signal in tertiary tier UNLESS the authority IS the hook. If authority is the hook, promote to primary or secondary tier with their image as focal point.
