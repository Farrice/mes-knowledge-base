# LENS CARD — Jonathan Franzen (literary novelist of comic distance, compression, and character psychology)
> Compiled 2026-07-27 from genius.md (canonical). FIRING RULE: this card decides WHETHER this lens has something to say about a draft line; when a lens fires, READ the matching genius.md section before treating. Never treat from the card alone.

## Lenses
### The Comic Problem Genesis
The best characters are defined by problems that are simultaneously small AND desperate. Start with the embarrassingly small thing that matters too much, then build the character outward from that specific itch. "I'm looking for a comic problem. It doesn't have to be a big problem... the smaller the problem, the funnier it is."
**Test:** Is the character's central problem an exquisitely observed micro-neurosis — pathetic, desperate, and funny at once — that immediately defines them?

### Comic Distance as Truth Detector
When you can laugh at a character, you've found truth about them. Victim-positioned writing (too close, too protective) produces sentimental, manipulative prose. If you can't find anything funny about your character's situation, you're too close — step back until you can see the absurdity. "Distance is really really critical."
**Test:** Can you find something absurd in the material? If not, you are still too close — this is the clearest failure signature for this expert.

### Shame-to-Comedy Conversion
Take your most shame-laden material. Find the ridiculous angle. The thing that would make you cringe becomes the thing that makes readers feel SEEN. "There's no technical solution to shame levels in a writer... you have to go into the shame and figure out why am I so ashamed" — and the failure reflex is "to just kind of pile on the ugliness."
**Test:** Is difficult material transformed by comic distance into relatable insight — or handled earnestly, piling shame on shame, heavy and manipulative?

### Minimal Detail Maximum Impact
For any scene, identify the 3 details that do the most work. Cut everything else. Trust the reader — their imagination does the heavy lifting. "There's probably a total of less than one page devoted to describing the weather in that book... two sentences are really all you need to establish what you need for a minor character."
**Test:** Minor character in 2 sentences? Scene carried by 2-3 details? Everything else cut?

### Want-Collision Scene Architecture
Every scene is powered by incompatible wants. Before writing any scene, articulate what each character desperately wants in that moment. Design the scene around the collision. "One character wants you to get on a plane. The other character really has no intention of getting on a plane... that's drama basically."
**Test:** Are the wants incompatible, and does the tension arise from what characters DON'T say but desperately want — not from stated conflict?

### The Iron Bridge Page Building
Every page must be FINISHED — publication-ready — before moving to the next. Don't move on until the page behind you is as good as you can make it. The novel is built like an iron bridge, link by link; no "I'll fix it later" trap.
**Test:** Does each page stand as finished work with no reliance on future fixes?

### Organic Plot Development
Know your destination, but discover the route. Hold the destination loosely; let characters' wants determine the path. "I've got to get there, but I have no idea how I'm going to get there. So it's the adventure of trying to... make an unlikely sequence of events seem inevitable." If you're surprised, the reader will be too.
**Test:** Does the journey feel surprising yet, in retrospect, completely inevitable — driven by the internal logic of character wants?

### Flaw as Plot Engine
The gap between "character has a flaw" and "flaw generates the plot" is where psychological precision lives. Articulate the character's misperception (what they consistently get wrong about others) so want-collisions emerge organically. Chain: flaw → misperception → reasonable wrong choice → consequence.
**Test:** Load-bearing test — every plot turn traces causally to the character's core misperception; removing the flaw collapses the narrative. Dual-identification test — the reader simultaneously roots for and cringes at the protagonist.

## Anti-patterns / calibration
All sourced to Franzen's own words (HIW-Franzen 2025-11-26):
- **Victim-positioned protectiveness.** "If you stick close to a character who the author is convinced is a victim, a good person that bad people have done bad things to... you're in trouble. On page one."
- **Outline-visible plotting.** "A book that was too fully planned is likely to read like a book that was too fully planned... that's the last impression you want to give." Sterile, competent, dead on arrival.
- **Cliché tolerance past the first instance.** Hard numeric line: "in my opinion, you get at most one cliche per book. People send me a galley... I read along until I get to the second cliche and I say, 'Thank you.'" This applies to exposed craft machinery too.
- **Trauma-dumping self-focus.** Raw difficulty without transformation is "very much focused on the self... incompatible with forging the kind of bond I want to forge with the reader."
- **Breaking the vivid dream.** Metafictional self-reference "basically to my mind takes you out of the experience."
- **Ornamental description without specificity.** "Merely describing the beauty of nature will not get the job done... I find that kind of writing personally almost always tedious, boring."
- **Calibration — embody, never expose:** Do NOT enumerate which patterns you applied, do NOT label sections by pattern name ("comic distance achieved here"), do NOT explain the framework. Comic distance is the tell of quality, not a decoration to add later. Polish that smooths away the absurdity isn't polish — it's a retreat into safety. If the output over-explains, over-describes, or feels assembled rather than discovered, it has failed on his own terms. The test: would Franzen recognize this as work that trusts the reader the way he insists on trusting the reader — or as vocabulary borrowed from a craft class? If unsure, the answer is no — rewrite.
- **Savant anchors:** every interaction a dance of unspoken incompatible desires · every descriptive word chosen, ample space left for the reader · shame converted to hilarious, relatable insight · each page finished, structurally robust · surprising-yet-inevitable plot from character logic · flaw fully load-bearing.
