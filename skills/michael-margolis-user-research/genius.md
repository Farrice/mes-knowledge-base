# Michael Margolis — Genius Context

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Comically Narrow Beats Confidently Broad
The bullseye customer is the very specific subset of your target market who is *initially* most likely to adopt. It should feel comically narrow — narrower than the team is comfortable with. Margolis looked at published "narrow" ICPs from Gong and Linear and said he'd go *deeper*. Narrowing is an experimental control: it reduces variables so the day's results are interpretable instead of mush.
**Execute**: Push the team past comfort. Every attribute must be concrete and measurable ("purchases X 3+ times/week"), never vibes ("active shopper," "founder-led"). Target ~7 attributes.
**Success Metric**: The whole team can say "yes — if we showed THIS person our value prop, we're pretty sure they'd want it," and a recruiter could verify every attribute with a questionnaire.

### Pattern: Five and Three in One
Five bullseye customers, three simple prototypes, one day of interviews with the whole team watching. Five hits qualitative data saturation ("please don't make me sit through another one — I'm hearing the same things"). Clumping interviews into one day makes patterns obvious in a way that Monday/Wednesday/Friday scattering never does.
**Execute**: Book five 1-hour interviews on the same day (two days max for time zones). Never dribble them out across a week.
**Success Metric**: By end of day the big questions have obvious answers — "did it work, which parts, for whom" — without anyone writing a report.

### Pattern: Inclusion, Exclusion, Triggers
Bullseye criteria come in three groups. Inclusion is easy (the team knows who they want). Exclusion is where to dwell — teams hate setting people aside: too-expert people (pharmacists testing a pharmacy product), competitor-locked users, disqualifying personal history (bankruptcy for fintech). Triggers are specific events that make someone ripe *right now*: a new CISO ("new sheriff in town"), something just went sideways, just married / just had a baby (life insurance moves from "I should someday" to "it's on my to-do list").
**Execute**: Brainstorm exclusions explicitly — "who seems like our customer but would pollute the sample?" Then hunt for the trigger event that converts latent need into active shopping.
**Success Metric**: At least one exclusion criterion and one trigger in the final definition; five recruits who all match all criteria.

### Pattern: The Non-Telegraphing Screener
Translate criteria into a screener questionnaire that never reveals the right answers. "Do you listen to Lenny's Podcast? ($125 if yes)" recruits liars; "What podcasts do you listen to?" (open-ended) lets you pick the truth-tellers. Post on a panel service (userinterviews.com or similar), sift hundreds of responses against your criteria sheet.
**Execute**: Every criterion becomes an indirect or open-ended question. Recruit in 3-4 days. Pay real money ($125/hr consumers; professionals get their hourly rate — attorneys got $400) so people show up. Watch pre-session responsiveness (NDA signed? replying to reminders?) — swap ghosts, skip backup alternates.
**Success Metric**: Five scheduled participants who match every criterion, with near-100% show rate and zero telegraphed answers.

### Pattern: Humble Inquiry — Learning Mode vs Selling Mode
Selling and learning are different modes, and founders are trained for the wrong one. Margolis borrows Edgar Schein's "humble inquiry": the gentle art of asking instead of telling. You must be vulnerable, present as the noob, and grant the participant higher status — the opposite of pitching. Before each interview he literally stops, breathes, puts on a smile and a "listener character" distinct from his normal skeptical self.
**Execute**: Open with a big smile (it changes your voice even on the phone) and light warmup until they smile back. Ask, don't tell. When you hear something wrong or weird, don't correct — dig: "why do you think that? what gives you that impression?"
**Success Metric**: Participant does 80%+ of the talking; you never explained, pitched, or defended the product once.

### Pattern: Two-Part Interview Arc
Each hour: first half is discovery — past experiences, stories, what worked, what went sideways ("tell me about a time it totally went wrong"). Second half is prototype comparison. Teams always beg "just get to the prototypes"; the discovery half is what makes prototype reactions interpretable — you understand *why* someone distrusts a feature because you heard the story of the time it burned them.
**Execute**: Build an arc: warmup chitchat → current/past experience of the problem → shift at ~30 min to presenting prototypes one at a time → compare and contrast.
**Success Metric**: At least one prototype reaction during the day that was only explicable because of the discovery half.

### Pattern: Three Recipes, Best Lego Pieces
Show three distinct value-prop "recipes," not three polishes of one idea. Like shopping for a couch: one option gets opinions; two or three create reference points, and the participant starts teasing out the best pieces of each ("I like the pharmacist delivery from this one, but the 15-minute window from that one"). You're not looking for a winner — you're harvesting the best Lego pieces to construct version-next. Multiple prototypes also stop the team from over-committing to one darling.
**Execute**: Spread your key variables deliberately across three flat, fake-homepage prototypes. Use competitor products as free prototypes — if you haven't watched customers respond to competitors, you're missing something.
**Success Metric**: Participants spontaneously compare across prototypes; the team ends the day with a parts list, not a verdict.

### Pattern: The Watch Party (No Report in 10 Years)
Research is a team sport. Margolis interviews 1:1 over Zoom, live-streamed to the whole team (record: 40 watchers). Core product team attends everything, takes turns taking notes manually (no AI notes — outsourced notes make people lean out and check Slack), debriefs in a structured spreadsheet between interviews (rows = key questions, columns = participants), with the decider leading. Backchannel: a facilitator filters team questions to the interviewer judiciously.
**Execute**: Assign note-taking roles, run a between-interview debrief every time, keep observers out of the participant's Zoom. End-of-day: everyone independently and silently fills a big-takeaways form (top 3 takeaways, how would you adjust the bullseye definition, next steps).
**Success Metric**: No report needed — the team saw it together, consensus emerges in the takeaways review, and everyone leaves aligned and "raring to go."

### Pattern: Predict Before, Compare After
Before the interviews, force everyone to write *specific* predictions ("people will prefer ASAP delivery") — not "we'll learn what they think." This defeats hindsight bias ("we kind of knew that") and proves the research's value when you compare predictions to takeaways. It also checks that the interview guide targets what the team actually needs.
**Execute**: Collect written predictions per person before day one. After the takeaways form, review predictions vs. reality as a team — not to catch anyone out, but to make the learning visible.
**Success Metric**: At least one confident team prediction visibly overturned, acknowledged in the debrief.

### Pattern: Past Behavior Outweighs Stated Intent
Put far more weight on what people have demonstrably done than on what they predict they'd do. "I would totally use this" that contradicts the history they just described gets discounted. Everyone is terrible at predicting their own behavior — that's not a customer flaw, it's human.
**Execute**: Anchor conclusions on demonstrated behavior and past choices from the discovery half. Treat enthusiasm as signal only when it's the involuntary kind — "wait, is this available? can I sign up?" — and give watchers permission to police each other's confirmation bias out loud.
**Success Metric**: No takeaway rests solely on a stated future intention.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: "We Can't Find Them" Is Data
**Insight**: When Margolis can't recruit the bullseye the team imagined — even with panels, forums, snowball recruiting — he treats it as a signal, not a logistics failure. If these people are this hard to find and reach for a paid hour, how will you sell to them? (Sometimes the fix is softening over-narrow criteria; sometimes the people genuinely don't exist.)
**Deploy**: When recruiting stalls, first relax the flexible criteria deliberately. If it still stalls, escalate to the team as a strategic finding: "I'm not sure the customer you're imagining exists."

### Insight: Learn What No Looks Like
**Insight**: A founder who killed his project after three sprint rounds named two wins: the pain saved (hardware + software + subscription never built), and that he *learned what no looks like* — neutral, polite, encouraging feedback ("sounds good") is a no. Genuine yes has visible energy: leaning in, pupils dilating, "can I sign up for this?" If you're unsure whether you saw pull, you didn't.
**Deploy**: Calibrate the team on this scale during debriefs. Label polite-positive feedback explicitly as "no" in the takeaways. Killing the project counts as a success outcome.

### Insight: The Curse-of-Knowledge Blind Spot Quartet
**Insight**: Across hundreds of prediction exercises in every domain, expert teams mispredict the same four things — they overestimate (1) how much customers know about the space, (2) how big customers perceive the problem to be, (3) willingness to pay, and (4) how ready customers are to buy right now. Deep expertise makes it nearly impossible to imagine not knowing what you know.
**Deploy**: Pre-seed the prediction exercise with these four axes and force explicit predictions on each. Expect the day to move all four downward.

### Insight: The Sprint Ends With One or Two True Attributes
**Insight**: You start with ~7 attributes, but the goal is to discover the one or two that actually distinguish ("it's people on *refrigerated* specialty meds — cold chain"). That distilled attribute then becomes a sales-side prioritization rubric: sort your entire funnel by it. Linear did this in reverse — their waitlist *was* a screener ("what do you use for auth? for code hosting?"), letting them admit only bullseyes at launch.
**Deploy**: In the final debrief, always ask "which one or two attributes carried the signal?" Then push that rubric into lead prioritization, waitlists, and onboarding questions.

### Insight: Mushy Results Trace Back to Recruiting Discipline
**Insight**: Every time a sprint day ends with "I'm not sure what we learned," Margolis traces it to the same cause: the recruit wasn't picky enough — the team took over recruiting, let the bullseye bleed, included known experts or friendly contacts. Too much participant variation makes results uninterpretable regardless of interview quality.
**Deploy**: Keep recruiting authority with whoever owns the criteria sheet. Reject near-miss participants even when slots are hard to fill; four clean bullseyes beat five mushy ones.

### Insight: When to Run It (and Re-Run It)
**Insight**: The sprint's highest-value moments: before building anything expensive; when expanding to a new segment or geography (UK→US, enterprise→self-serve); and when traction is politely mediocre and you need to troubleshoot. It's iterative by design — the medication-delivery team ran it, discovered the cold-chain subset, re-recruited, and ran it again to confirm. "Do this again, adjusted" is a normal next step, and the most common end-of-day takeaway across all teams is "we need more of this."
**Deploy**: Budget for two rounds, not one. Round one refines the bullseye definition; round two confirms it with the sharpened recruit.
