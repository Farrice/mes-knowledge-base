# 16 — Creative Concept Engine (`/satori-concept`)

> Given a design brief, generate 3 concept directions using 7 named creative techniques — each direction anchored to a hidden truth (the "one big idea"), and justified for the ONE specific audience it is for. This is the generative flagship: it produces concepts, not critique.

Design is decision-making before it is expression. A concept is the largest decision on the board — the single idea every later choice of type, color, grid, and image will serve or betray. This workflow forces that decision first, and forces it to stand on a hidden truth rather than the obvious thing. If you skip it, you decorate the obvious thing faster; you do not out-think it.

## Pre-Flight Gate

**Use this when**:
- A brief is in hand and you need concept *directions* before any layout, moodboard, or generation
- A design came back generic ("looks fine, says nothing") and you need a real idea underneath it
- You have facts and features but no angle — you're staring at the product, not the truth behind it
- A client / stakeholder needs to choose between distinct thinking directions, not aesthetic variations of one

**Do NOT use this when**:
- The brief itself is unclear — run `/satori-comms-brief` first (this workflow assumes a brief exists) or reduce to a one-sentence brief via `/satori-why-before-what`
- You already have the concept and need to audit its execution — use `/satori-lift-audit` (layout) or `/satori-why-before-what` (element rent test)
- It's a logo specifically — `/satori-logo-concept` runs the verb-not-noun path built for identity
- You need production, not thinking — go straight to `/satori-poster-think` → `/fantastic-posters`, or `/creative-direction`
- The audience is genuinely "everyone" and cannot be narrowed — stop; a concept for everyone lands for no one (see genius.md GP-02, Predictive Empathy). Narrow the audience first.

Concepts sit *above* composition. This is the layer that decides what the design is *about*; LIFT decides whether the layout delivers it. Run this before the audit workflows, not after.

## Execution

### Step 0 — Load the brief and name the ONE audience

Pull the comms brief (from `/satori-comms-brief`) or the raw brief. Then force two decisions before anything else:

1. **Reduce to one sentence** (GP-08): *"A [thing] that [verb] [audience] [outcome/feeling]."* If you can't write it, you're not ready to conceive — stop and fix the brief.
2. **Name the ONE audience — specifically.** Not "consumers," not "professionals." *"Retail equity traders who read the terminal before coffee."* *"Parents of a smoker who don't smoke themselves."* The concept will be pressure-tested against these exact people in Step 4, so name them now with enough detail that you could recognize one in a room.

> *"Great concepts often come from understanding a specific audience deeply enough to speak their language."*

Decision made here: **who this is for and what it must do.** Every technique below is applied *for these people*, not in the abstract.

### Step 1 — Excavate the hidden truth (the default first move)

Before generating any direction, do the one thing amateurs skip: stop designing around the product and find the truth behind it.

> *"stop designing around the obvious thing itself and then to start designing around the hidden truth behind the product."*

Run the four trigger questions and write an answer to each:

> *"ask yourself what the real problem is. What's the consequence? What is the emotional impact? What's the thing people aren't immediately thinking about?"*

- **What's the real problem?** (Not the surface complaint — the thing underneath it.)
- **What's the consequence?** (Where does it lead if nothing changes? Who else does it touch?)
- **What's the emotional impact?** (What does it actually *feel* like, and to whom?)
- **What is nobody thinking about?** (The blind-spot truth the obvious framing hides.)

**Exemplar — the crayons.** The obvious anti-smoking concept designs around the smoker's own health (tar, lungs, statistics). The hidden truth: the smoker's damage lands on the *children they love*. The winning ad turned the cigarettes into crayons — the danger is now to someone else's small hands. Same product, a completely different (and more powerful) truth.

Write the hidden truth as **one sentence**: *"The real thing here isn't ___ ; it's ___."*

**Reality gate (non-negotiable).** The hidden truth must be *true* — verifiable about this product, audience, or category. A hidden truth you invented because it sounds profound is a lie with good production values, and it will read as one. If you cannot name a real hidden truth, do not fabricate one; return to research or `/satori-comms-brief`. (Factual grounding standard applies — label the truth's basis: observed / stated-by-client / researched.)

Decision made here: **the anchor.** All three directions will attach to a hidden truth. Ideally one strong truth feeds all three; if the excavation surfaces two or three distinct truths, note them — different directions may anchor to different truths.

### Step 2 — Load the 7-technique palette

These are the seven generative moves. You do not use all seven per concept — you select 1–2 per direction (Step 3). Read them as the toolkit; the selection guide at the end maps technique to brief type.

---

**T1 — HIDDEN TRUTH** *(already run in Step 1; it is also a standalone lens)*
- **Trigger question**: "What's the real problem / consequence / emotional impact / the thing nobody's thinking about?"
- **Exemplar**: Anti-smoking ad — cigarettes become crayons; the concept designs around the smoker's impact on the children they love, not the smoker's own lungs.
- **Apply**: Take the hidden-truth sentence from Step 1 and make *it* the subject of the design. The product becomes evidence of the truth, not the hero.

**T2 — ONE BIG IDEA / COMMIT**
- **Trigger question**: "What is the single strongest feature or idea here, and what would it look like to push it further than anyone else would?"
- **Exemplar**: A product with a red lens — instead of showing the lens, the red *spreads through the entire system*: photography graded red, product shots bathed red, atmosphere red. One idea, taken to its limit.
- **Apply**: Find the one strongest idea and build the whole system around it. Then push past where a competent designer would stop. Do not hedge with a second theme — commit.

> *"Creativity often comes from taking one strong idea and pushing it further than everybody else would."*

> *"AI is very good at generating options, but human designers are still much better at recognizing the strongest idea and then building an entire system around it."*

This second quote is the load-bearing one for the whole workflow: use AI (or brainstorming) to *generate* candidate directions cheaply, but the human move — the one that earns the fee — is **recognizing** the strongest and committing the whole system to it. Generation is abundant; recognition + commitment is the moat.

**T3 — SPEAK THE AUDIENCE'S LANGUAGE**
- **Trigger question**: "What do these exact people already say, know, or recognize that an outsider wouldn't?"
- **Exemplar**: Bloomberg Businessweek "bull market" cover — a bull rendered so investors read *market sentiment* instantly, while an outsider just sees a bull in a field. It doesn't try to explain itself to everyone.
- **Apply**: Use the audience's own terms, in-jokes, iconography, or shorthand. Accept that outsiders won't fully get it — that exclusivity is the point and the proof of fit.

> *"Great concepts often come from understanding a specific audience deeply enough to speak their language."*

**T4 — LITERAL INTERPRETATION**
- **Trigger question**: "What phrase, expression, or metaphor lives in this audience's head — and what if I made it physically real?"
- **Exemplar**: Take an idiom the audience already carries and render it literally, so meaning arrives pre-installed.
- **Apply**: Pick a phrase/metaphor the audience already owns; execute it as a real object/scene. You borrow meaning that already exists in their mind instead of building it from scratch.

> *"This is where you take a phrase, an expression, a metaphor... and you make it physically real... borrowing meaning that already exists in the audience's mind."*

**T5 — WHAT-IF COMBINATION**
- **Trigger question**: "What if this thing were [an unrelated thing]?"
- **Exemplar**: "What if a webpage was furniture?" → a 404 error page fused with IKEA's "missing a piece" assembly diagram: instantly understood *and* funny, because both references are already loaded.
- **Apply**: When you're stuck, stop thinking about the product; collide it with something unrelated and see what meaning the collision generates.

> *"when you're struggling for ideas, just stop thinking about the product and start asking what if questions."*

**T6 — TINY-DETAIL FLAIR**
- **Trigger question**: "What is the one small detail everyone else overlooks that would do the whole job?"
- **Exemplar**: A sushi restaurant sign where only the "Yo" changes color — the accent hue pulled straight from the food. One tiny change, disproportionate effect.
- **Apply**: Sometimes the concept is a single overlooked micro-move, not a big idea. Know when the design does **not** need a huge concept — restraint is the creative act here.

> *"Sometimes, it comes from noticing tiny details that everybody else just overlooks."*

**T7 — EMOTION OVER INFORMATION**
- **Trigger question**: "Where am I telling people a fact when I could make them feel it?"
- **Exemplar**: A dog-euthanasia poster shows a statistic — pure information. Then a torn page reveals a collar, and it becomes *someone's* dog. A tiny visual change moves the piece from data to grief.
- **Apply**: Facts alone rarely change minds. Find the single move that converts the information into a felt thing — one image, one reveal, one substitution.

> *"Information tells people what to think, whereas emotion makes them feel something."*

---

### Step 3 — Generate 3 concept directions

Produce **three genuinely distinct directions** — distinct in *idea*, not in color/typeface. For each direction, force these decisions in order:

1. **Select 1–2 techniques** from the palette that fit *this brief and this audience*. (Use the Technique-Selection Guide below.) One primary technique per direction is the norm; a second only if it reinforces, never dilutes.
2. **Anchor it to a hidden truth** — name which Step-1 truth this direction is built on. A direction with no hidden truth underneath is decoration; evict it (GP-01).
3. **Generate the concept** — the actual idea, described concretely enough that a designer could start building.
4. **Name the central idea in ONE sentence** (GP-08). If it takes two sentences, you have two ideas or a fuzzy one — sharpen or split.
5. **Commit** (T2). Push the one idea further than the obvious version. Write the "further" move explicitly — what makes this direction go past where a competent designer would stop.
6. **Write a rough execution note** — the primitive/motif, the one hero move, and how it would show up across the system. Not a full layout; a directional sketch in words.

Keep the three directions *conceptually separated* — a good set gives the client a real choice of thinking (e.g., one HIDDEN-TRUTH + EMOTION, one WHAT-IF, one TINY-DETAIL restraint play), not three skins of the same idea. If two directions collapse into the same central sentence, kill one and generate a genuinely different third.

### Step 4 — Pressure-test each direction against the ONE audience

This is the discipline that separates a concept from a clever doodle. For each of the three directions, run the audience gauntlet and write the verdict:

- **The <2-second read**: Would *these exact people* grasp the idea fast, without a caption explaining it? (Genius.md: a stranger names the leverage point in under two seconds; here, the target names the *idea*.)
- **Insider vs. outsider (T3)**: Does it speak their language? An outsider not getting it is fine — *the audience* not getting it is fatal. Name what the insider reads that the outsider misses.
- **Resolve, don't hand over (GP-03)**: Does it give them something to resolve (memorable), or does it hand the meaning over flat (forgettable)? If it hands over, add one degree of controlled resolution.
- **Emotion vs. information (T7)**: Is it making them *feel* the truth, or just *told* it? Facts rarely move people.
- **Next-emotion fit (GP-02)**: What emotion does the audience carry into the next 60 seconds after seeing it? Is that the emotion the brief wants? If the concept is loud but the audience isn't pre-aligned, soften or reframe.

**Kill or revise** any direction that fails the audience read. Do not carry a failing concept forward "because it looks cool" — that is the aesthetic-first auto-reject. Three directions that pass beat five that mostly don't.

### Step 5 — Recommend the strongest and push it further

Now the human move that AI can't do for you: **recognize** the strongest of the three and **commit**.

> *"AI is very good at generating options, but human designers are still much better at recognizing the strongest idea and then building an entire system around it."*

- **Name the winner** and state, in 2–3 sentences, *why it beats the other two for THIS specific audience* — cite the Step-4 verdicts, not taste.
- **Push it further** (T2). The recommended direction should not stop at "good enough." Add the one move that takes it past the obvious execution — the equivalent of letting the red spread through the entire system. Write that move down.
- **Note the trade** for the runners-up: one line each on what that direction would have won and what it cost — so the client's choice (if they overrule you) is informed, not blind.

Decision made here: **the single idea the whole system will commit to.** Hand off to composition (`/satori-lift-audit`, `/satori-grid-select`) or production (`/satori-poster-think` → `/fantastic-posters`) with the concept locked.

### Technique-Selection Guide (which move fits which brief)

| Brief type | Lead technique(s) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Awareness / charity / cause** | T1 Hidden Truth + T7 Emotion over Information | The obvious framing is weak; the power is in the felt consequence nobody's thinking about |
| **Niche B2B / specialist** | T3 Speak the Audience's Language | The audience is small and expert; insider shorthand is the moat — stop explaining to outsiders |
| **Product hero / feature launch** | T2 One Big Idea / Commit | Find the single strongest feature and build the whole system around it, pushed further than rivals |
| **Playful / consumer / DTC** | T5 What-If Combination + T4 Literal Interpretation | Humor and borrowed meaning land fast with broad consumer audiences |
| **Premium / editorial / luxury** | T6 Tiny-Detail Flair | Restraint reads as confidence; one overlooked micro-move outclasses a loud concept |
| **Stuck / blank page** | T5 What-If Combination | Stop thinking about the product; collide it with something unrelated to break the block |
| **Facts aren't landing** | T7 Emotion over Information | Convert the strongest fact into one felt image or reveal |

Default starting move for *any* brief is **T1 (Hidden Truth)** — it's Step 1. The other six are how you dramatize the truth once you've found it.

## Content-Type Adaptations

| Surface | How the method shifts |
|---|---|
| **Poster / print** | Single frozen frame, so the concept must land in one look — favor T1+T7 (hidden truth made felt) or T5 (what-if collision). One hero move; no reliance on scroll or reveal. Hand off via `/satori-poster-think`. |
| **Logo / identity** | Concept must survive at thumbnail and outlive campaigns — favor T2 (commit to one idea) and T6 (tiny detail), never a busy metaphor. Route the *ideation* to `/satori-logo-concept` (verb-not-noun); use this workflow only for the surrounding campaign idea. |
| **UI / product** | The concept lives across states and time, not one frame — T4 (literal interpretation of a metaphor the user already holds, e.g. the IKEA 404) and T3 (speak the user's language) travel best. Test the concept at empty-state, error, and success, not just the hero screen. |
| **Social / feed** | Thumb-stopping in <2 sec against a hostile feed — T5 (what-if) and T7 (emotion) win because they're instantly graspable. T3 insider language earns shares from the in-group. The <2-second read in Step 4 is pass/fail, not advisory. |
| **Packaging** | Physical, held, seen in a shelf-row of competitors — T6 (tiny-detail flair, like the color pulled from the food) and T2 (one committed idea) differentiate without clutter. Concept must read at arm's length AND survive the shelf grid. |
| **Ad creative** | Persuasion under time pressure — T1 hidden truth is the spine, T7 emotion is the delivery, T4 literal makes it memorable. The CTA is *not* the concept; the felt truth that earns the CTA is. Beware information-dump concepts here. |

## Output Requirements

The deliverable is a concept brief containing **exactly three directions** plus a recommendation. Use this artifact spec:

```markdown

**One-sentence brief**: A [thing] that [verb] [audience] [outcome/feeling].
**The ONE audience**: [specific description — recognizable, not "everyone"]
**Hidden truth(s)**: "The real thing here isn't ___ ; it's ___." [basis: observed / client-stated / researched]

---

## Direction A — [evocative name]
- **Central idea (one sentence)**: [...]
- **Technique(s)**: [T# name] (+ [T# name] if used, and why it reinforces)
- **Hidden truth it's built on**: [...]
- **Why it works for THIS audience**: [insider read + felt emotion + next-emotion fit]
- **The "further" move (commit)**: [what pushes it past the obvious version]
- **Rough execution note**: [primitive/motif · hero move · how it shows up across the system]

## Direction B — [name]
[same fields]

## Direction C — [name]
[same fields]

---

## Recommendation
- **Strongest direction**: [A / B / C]
- **Why it beats the other two for this audience**: [2–3 sentences citing the Step-4 verdicts]
- **Push-it-further move**: [the one move that takes the winner past "good enough"]
- **Runner-up trades**: [B: won ___ / cost ___] · [C: won ___ / cost ___]

## Handoff

```

Every direction MUST have all six fields filled. A direction missing its hidden truth or its audience justification is not a direction — it's a decoration, and it does not ship in the set.

## Quality Gate

Guards against these genius.md anti-patterns (auto-reject if present):

- [ ] **Decoration without reason (GP-01)** — Every direction names a real hidden truth it's built on. No "looks cool," no idea that pays no rent.
- [ ] **Invented truth** — The hidden truth is *true* (observed / client-stated / researched), not fabricated to sound deep. Basis is labeled.
- [ ] **Communicating to everyone (GP-02 / T3)** — One specific audience is named, and every direction is justified *for them*. "For everyone" fails the gate.
- [ ] **More-equals-better layering (GP-11 anti-pattern #6)** — Each direction commits to one big idea, pushed further; it does not stack three techniques into mud. Max 2 techniques, second only if reinforcing.
- [ ] **Information over emotion (T7)** — At least the recommended direction makes the audience *feel* the truth, not just read it.
- [ ] **Hands-over meaning (GP-03)** — Concepts give something to resolve; none of the three flatly hands its meaning over.
- [ ] **Aesthetic-first (auto-reject #8)** — No palette/typeface/moodboard was chosen before the concept. Concept precedes expression.
- [ ] **Recognition + commitment (T2 / GP-11)** — A single winner is recommended and pushed further, not three co-equal options dumped on the client to sort out. Generating options is the cheap half; recognizing and committing is the deliverable.
- [ ] **Three distinct ideas** — The three central sentences are genuinely different ideas, not three skins of one.

**Pass criteria**: All nine boxes checked. Any unchecked box = revise before delivery. A set of three that each pass the Step-4 audience gauntlet beats a larger set that mostly doesn't — quantity of options is not the product; a committed, audience-true idea is.
