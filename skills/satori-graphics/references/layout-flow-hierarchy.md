# Layout Flow & Hierarchy — Three-Flow Rule, 3-Key Levels, Directional Hierarchy

> **(v3)** Source: Satori Graphics Layout Course (2026-07-21 expansion, watched — frames in `extractions/satori-graphics/expansion-2026-07-21/frames/`). This is the layout *origination* layer: it commits the journey BEFORE grid selection (GP-05) and BEFORE the LIFT audit (GP-06) verify it.

## The Three-Flow Rule (GP-18)

Every layout carries exactly **one primary journey with three committed anchors**:

| Anchor | Job | Typical carrier |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Hook** | Entry point — where the eye lands in <1 sec | Largest visual mass: hero product, artwork, dominant type |
| **2. Secondary Detail** | The middle stop that carries the eye deeper | Heading/subheading, supporting image, key claim |
| **3. Finisher** | Where the eye *rests* — and absorbs the essential information | Logo, CTA, date/location, price — the thing that must not be missed |

Worked exemplar — Buck Design "Universe '24" (GitHub): bold 3D graphics emanating bottom-right (hook) → white heading (secondary, with a short stop at the tagline) → logo bottom-left (finisher). The traced path is a smooth curve across a triangle of anchors, not a straight scan line.

> *"Not every single person will view this design in exactly the same way… But it provides a clear, streamlined path that helps most viewers absorb the message quickly."* — Satori

**Rules of use:**
- Three anchors, not four, not two. More = competing paths (chaos); fewer = a poster with no journey.
- Write the three anchors down before opening the canvas. This is the layout's one-sentence brief (GP-08 applied to space).
- Micro-routes (movement L3) are permitted *inside* the main path, never instead of it.

### The Thumbnail / Step-Back Test (verification habit)

> *"Literally take a step back. Look at your design from a distance or shrink it down to a thumbnail size. If the visual path still feels balanced and easy to follow, you've done something right… If it feels chaotic or your eye is bouncing around the place, something is probably off. So try adjusting the balance of contrast, spacing or alignment."*

Protocol: shrink to ~120px or view from 3m → trace the path with a finger → does it hit Hook → Secondary → Finisher calmly? Fix levers, in order: **contrast → spacing → alignment**. Satori frames this as a *trained habit* ("train you to see flow on every single project and not just relying on gut instinct") — run it on every layout, not just failing ones.

## The 3-Key Levels of Size Hierarchy

Size is the fastest hierarchy lever ("our eyes pick up on the fact of size instantly"). Structure it in the same three levels:

- **Hook = largest visual.** Product brief → the product IS the hook (Yakult bottle, front and center, red circle behind it adding emphasis — the switched-on designer's double move).
- **Secondary detail = slightly smaller.**
- **Finisher = smallest, but essential.** The viewer finishes here and absorbs the must-know info.

**The exception that proves the rule**: on an events poster (ATTISM fashion-week invitation), the hook is artistic typography — but the most *important* information is the date/location, living at finisher scale. Largest ≠ most important; largest = entry. The essential info rides the *end* of the journey, not the top of the size order.

Combine with F/Z-pattern reading flow for full control of start-to-finish attention.

## Directional Hierarchy — the Path of Importance (GP-19)

> *"The goal isn't to create movement. It's actually to create a path of importance. Each directional cue should serve the hierarchy by leading from the most dominant focal point to the next in order of relevance."*

Hierarchy decides **what is seen first**; direction decides **where the eye goes next**. The triad (from the source graphic): **Line of sight · Flow of shapes · Balance of weight.**

| Tool | Move |
|---|---|
| **Gaze lines** | Frame text close to the direction the subject's face/eyes point — the reader's eye follows the model's |
| **Angled shapes** | Backgrounds/shapes pointing inward funnel focus to the layout's center |
| **Curves & diagonals** | Create natural momentum that keeps the eye traveling |
| **Light & shadow** | Illumination nudges attention from one element to the next |

Proof exemplar — Dazed cover: the model's face is deliberately amber-lit (hierarchy → first fixation), her face points up toward the masthead — which is **black, not white**. The designer refused the "make it stand out" default because hierarchy had already guaranteed the journey. Salience defaults lose to engineered paths.

## How this layer composes with the existing machinery

```
GP-13 comms problem → GP-16 concept → [THREE-FLOW: commit 3 anchors] → GP-05 grid
   → [DIRECTIONAL HIERARCHY: wire the path] → GP-04 movement level (2–6 as brief demands)
   → GP-20 contrast stack (make the anchors win) → GP-06 LIFT audit → GP-17 perception gap
```

- Three-flow is the *decision*; the movement ladder is the *mechanics*; LIFT is the *audit*. Don't run LIFT on a layout whose three anchors were never committed — there's nothing to audit against.
- The finisher anchor is the terminal beat of `/satori-perception-gap` Step 1 — a CTA-blind gap usually means the finisher was never committed as an anchor.
- Temporal flow (movement L6) sets *when/how long* per anchor: punch (hook) → linger (secondary) → release (finisher). "Something like 99% of designers don't consider this temporal flow" — the cheapest edge in the stack.
