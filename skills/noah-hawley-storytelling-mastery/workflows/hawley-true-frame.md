# True-Story Frame

*Fargo* opens with white text on black: "This is a true story." It isn't — the Coens made it up, Hawley inherited it. But the part of the brain that reads those words leans in differently. It stops watching and starts *remembering*. This workflow builds that frame deliberately: a wrapper that borrows the weight of the real so a made-up thing lands like something the audience half-recalls happening.

## Pre-Flight
Read these files before executing:
1. `skills/noah-hawley-storytelling-mastery/genius.md`
2. `skills/noah-hawley-storytelling-mastery/references/hidden-knowledge.md`

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: Before executing, run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` — confirm you can answer the question the piece is exploring, where it ends, and what assumption you're taking for granted. The frame is a tool laid over a story; if the story underneath has no spine, no frame will save it.

## Input Required
- The piece being framed (logline, draft, post, campaign concept — whatever already exists or is planned)
- The theme/question underneath it (what is the work actually answering?)
- The feeling the frame should produce — recognition, dread, irony, intimacy, the uncanny sense of having heard this before
- Any real events, places, or figures the piece sits near (so we can echo deliberately, not stumble into a real tragedy)
- The platform and audience (a screen card, an essay's first line, and a LinkedIn opener carry the frame differently)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Choose the frame
Three doors. Pick one — combining them dilutes the leverage.

| Frame | What it does | Best when |
|-------|-------------|-----------|
| **False-documentary card** | A declarative claim of truth laid over fiction ("This is a true story." "Names changed at their request."). The audience reads it as fact and lowers their guard. | The piece wants the *posture* of testimony — confession, record, reportage. |
| **Real-event echo** | The story rhymes with something the audience knows happened — a private plane down in a field, a town that emptied overnight — without naming it. The plot is invented; the *shape* is borrowed. | You want resonance, not assertion. The audience supplies the realness from their own memory. |
| **Memory-trigger** | A sensory or situational detail engineered to feel like the reader's own recollection ("the soap dispenser that wouldn't see her"). Not a claim about the world — a claim about *their* world. | First-person, intimate, or content pieces where the reader is the protagonist. |

State the frame in one sentence and name which door. Then write the actual wrapper text — the card, the opening echo, the trigger detail. This is the thing the audience meets first.

### Step 2: Calibrate the cognitive leverage
The frame is a lever. Measure how much weight it's pulling, and decide whether the audience is meant to *believe* it or *feel the irony* of it. Hawley's *Fargo* card works precisely because most viewers know it's a Coen invention — the leverage is ironic, a wink that paradoxically makes the violence land heavier. A memoir's "true story" frame is the opposite: believed, and the weight is trust.

Answer on the page:
- **How much does the frame borrow?** A whisper of recognition, or the full authority of documented fact? Over-borrow and the reveal (if there is one) feels like a cheat. Under-borrow and the frame does nothing.
- **Believe or feel the irony?** Mark it. *Believed* frames must not collapse when scrutinized; *ironic* frames must let the audience in on the game (the wink is load-bearing).
- **What does the borrowed weight pay off?** The frame must serve the theme, not decorate it. *Fargo*'s "true story" amplifies "decency versus cynicism" — these things happen, in ordinary towns, to ordinary people. If the frame's only job is to seem cool, broom it.

Withhold here, too. The strongest version states the frame once and never mentions it again — let the audience carry it. The frame that keeps reminding you it's true is the sad music before the sad moment.

### Step 3: ETHICS CHECK — echo, don't exploit or deceive
This is a hard gate, not a courtesy. The move is to tap recognition. It is *never* to ride a real person's grief for borrowed gravity, or to mislead a reader about something consequential.

Run all four. Any **FAIL** halts deployment until reworked:

| Check | PASS looks like | FAIL looks like |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Real-tragedy proximity** | Echoes a *type* of event; no identifiable victim, family, or community can read themselves into it. | A recognizable real death/disaster repurposed for effect. |
| **Consequential deception** | The "truth" claim is genre convention the audience can read as such, or clearly fiction by context. | A reader could act on the false claim where it matters — health, money, safety, reputation, a real person's record. |
| **Consent & dignity** | Real figures, if echoed, are treated with the dignity their reality demands. | A living person fictionalized into something defamatory or humiliating. |
| **Reveal honesty** | If the frame is believed, there is an honest off-ramp (context, disclosure, or convention) so no one is left genuinely deceived. | The deception is the product and it costs the audience something. |

Write a one-line **Ethics Note** stating which frame you chose and why it taps recognition without crossing into exploitation or harmful deception. If the echo sits near a specific real tragedy, name it and explain the distance you've kept. Flag — do not bury — anything marginal.

### Step 4: Deploy — placement and payoff
Decide where the frame lives and how it cashes out.

- **Entry point**: the card before the cold open, the first line of the essay, the opening clause of the post. The frame must arrive *before* the story, so the audience reads the story through it.
- **Recurrence**: ideally zero. State it once. If it must recur, it should escalate the irony or deepen the belief, never just repeat.
- **Payoff**: where the borrowed weight discharges. The frame promised "this happened"; the ending should make the audience feel the weight of it having happened — the recognition closing into a small gut-drop. Tie the payoff to the theme from Step 2.

Mark the deployment so the executor knows exactly what text goes where.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | The on-screen card or cold open. Believed-or-ironic is set by genre and the audience's prior knowledge; the card never recurs. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The opening line as memory-trigger or real-event echo. The reader should think "I remember something like this" before they know it's an argument. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn/X) | The first line *is* the frame — a memory-trigger that makes the reader the protagonist. No card, no preamble; the recognition has to fire in one scroll. |
| Brand / campaign | The real-event echo or testimonial posture ("a true story from a customer"). Ethics gate is strictest here — a believed frame in advertising can become a consequential deception fast. |
| Memoir / personal | Believed frame, trust-weighted. The leverage is intimacy; the ethics check centers on real people named or echoed and their dignity. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- The frame serves the theme — kill it if its only job is to seem real or seem cool (white-hat-at-noon: a clever frame that betrays what the work is about).
- Believed-vs-ironic is decided and consistent; the wrapper text doesn't promise one and behave like the other.
- The frame states itself once and trusts the audience to carry it — no reminding the reader it's true (that's emotionality wearing a documentary coat).
- All four ethics checks logged PASS, or the piece is reworked. No real tragedy ridden for gravity; nothing consequential left genuinely deceiving.
- The payoff makes the borrowed weight *land* — recognition closes into feeling, not just a tidy callback.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Frame as costume, story naked underneath.** "This is a true story" over a thin plot just dresses up nothing. Recovery: run the Decision Framework first — the frame amplifies a spine; it cannot manufacture one.
- **Over-borrowing the realness.** Insisting on the truth claim until it feels like a defendant protesting too much. Recovery: state it once, then go silent; let the audience supply the conviction.
- **Sliding from echo into exploitation.** A "type of event" quietly becomes a specific real family's tragedy. Recovery: name the real event you're near, measure the distance, abstract until no one can read themselves into the victim's seat.
- **Confusing the modes.** Treating an ironic frame as if the audience believes it (so the wink never lands) or a believed frame as ironic (so trust quietly breaks). Recovery: mark Believed/Ironic in Step 2 and audit the wrapper text against it before deploy.
