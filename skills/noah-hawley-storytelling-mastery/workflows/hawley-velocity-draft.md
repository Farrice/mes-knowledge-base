# Velocity Draft

Hawley writes a novel in roughly 100 pages over three months, not 25 pages he loses by spring. The number that matters isn't speed — it's enough "there there" that you could walk away for a month and the draft would still tell you its own voice. This workflow does two things at once: it gets the piece down before the voice evaporates, and it builds the operator's cadence so the deep mode and the fast mode never poison each other.

If you can name the question and the ending, the velocity sprint has a spine to run down. If you can't, you'll write 25 pages and lose them.

## Input Required
- The piece you're drafting (essay, edition, post, script) and its working question + suspected ending.
- How much time you actually have in one sitting (the real window, not the ideal one).
- The operator's two media and their rough volume (e.g. Parallax long-form biweekly + LinkedIn 4×/week).
- Your current input diet — what's actually going in right now (reading, films, conversations), honestly.
- Where the felt stakes are too high (the piece you keep not-starting because it has to be perfect).

## Workflow

### Step 1: The velocity sprint — get enough "there there"
The goal of a first sitting is not a finished thing. It's a draft with enough voice, structure, and intent that a future you returning cold could "see exactly what you were doing." Hawley's tell for failure is the 25-page novel start — technically real, but too thin to survive the gap, so it dies. Sprint for survivability, not polish.

Before you write a sentence, plant the three anchors that let a draft survive an absence:

```
THE VOICE — one line of the actual register, written in voice, not described
THE QUESTION — what the piece is genuinely asking (you don't hold the answer)
THE LANDING — where it ends (the destination is fixed; the middle is a blob)
```

Then run the piece down in one pass, fastest-path first. Don't edit upstream; "I see it and so I write it." When you hit a hole, leave a bracket and keep moving — `[bridge from the café scene to the turn]` survives a gap; a perfect paragraph and three blank ones does not. The test at the end of the sitting isn't "is it good." It's the survival test below.

| Survival test | Pass condition |
|---------------|----------------|
| Voice legible | A month from now, the draft sounds like *you* on first read |
| Direction legible | The question and landing are visible without re-thinking them |
| Skeleton complete | Every section is at least bracketed start-to-end — no blob that swallows the spine |

If it fails the survival test, you have a fragment, not a draft. Add the missing anchors before you stop, even rough.

### Step 2: Lower the stakes to ship
"I coulda been a contender" was shot on a Tuesday between 4 and 8 — nobody on that set knew it was *the* scene, which is exactly why they could make it. Paralysis comes from treating today's piece as The Piece. The fix is to demote it: this is a Tuesday, not a monument. Playfulness isn't the mood; it's the productivity mechanism.

Reframe the stakes out loud before the sprint locks you up:

```
What it feels like: "This is THE [essay/post/edition] — it has to land."
The Tuesday reframe: "This is a Tuesday between 4 and 8. I'll take another shot if I don't like it."
The escape hatch (HK-8): "If we don't like what we did, I'll find a way to redo it." — name the literal redo path
The kid-on-the-floor move: the playful thing I'd try if no one were watching: _______________
```

Kids building a couch-fort don't think they're making art, which is why they can. If the piece is frozen, the stake is too high — find the redo path and the play move, and the draft unsticks. Struggle theater (HK-7) is not the work; ease that looks like indifference often is.

### Step 3: Dual-media batching plan — context-switch, don't blend
Hawley writes novels *between* shows, never alongside — solitary-slow and collaborative-fast are different brains, and he refuses to run them at once. The operator's failure mode is blending: drafting a deep essay in the same headspace as firing off posts, so both come out half-pressured. Batch the modes into separate blocks and switch contexts cleanly between them.

Map the two media onto two distinct working modes, then schedule them so they don't bleed:

| | Deep mode (long-form) | Fast mode (short-form) |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Parallax edition, essay | LinkedIn / X post |
| Brain | solitary, slow, voice-led | public, quick, reactive |
| Block | protected, no notifications, long | short, batched, lower ceremony |
| Cadence | sprint in one sitting (Step 1) | batch several at once, then release on schedule |
| Switch rule | never start a post mid-essay; finish the sprint, *then* change rooms | never deepen a post into an essay in the fast block — capture the seed, move it to deep mode |

Lay it on the actual week. The point is the *separation*, not the volume: a clean deep block beats two contaminated ones. When a fast-mode idea wants to become a deep piece, don't widen the post — drop the seed in the input bank (Step 4) and protect the next deep block for it.

### Step 4: The input ritual — the "second breakfast" intake
"When you have a lot of output you need a lot of input" (HK-6). Hawley's ritual: drop the kids, go to a café for "second breakfast," carry a little book of ideas or a magazine, and wait for the "oh, that's interesting" that opens a part of the brain that wasn't open before. The intake comes *before* the work — it's what produces the thought that lets the mind start thinking about the stories.

Design the ritual so input is a fixed habit, not a hope:

```
THE RITUAL
  When: _______________ (a fixed time before the deep block — the "second breakfast" slot)
  Where: _______________ (a place that means "intake," distinct from the writing place)
  What's in the bag: _______________ (the book of ideas / the magazine / the saved reads)
  The signal you're hunting: the "oh, that's interesting" — the line that opens a new room

THE INPUT BANK
  Where seeds live: _______________ (the inbox/file the fast-mode seeds and the "interesting" go)
  Feed rate: input should be proportional to output — heavier output weeks need heavier intake

THE EMPTY-DAY RULE (HK-6)
  "I left it all on the page yesterday, nothing went in." Sometimes the honest answer is
  you're not writing today — and that has to be okay. Refill input; don't grind a dry well.
```

The ritual is the upstream of the work, not a break from it. A week of heavy output with no intake is the empty-day problem arriving on schedule. When the bank is full, the sprint in Step 1 has fuel; when it's dry, no amount of stakes-lowering will produce the "interesting."

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Long-form essay / Substack | The deep mode itself. Sprint the whole edition in one protected sitting (Step 1); the survival test is the gate. Input ritual matters most here — the "interesting" is the seed of the question. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | The fast mode. Batch several at once in a low-ceremony block; never deepen a post mid-block. The Tuesday reframe (Step 2) is the whole game — a post is always a Tuesday. |
| Screenplay / TV | Hawley's native split: solitary novel-brain vs. collaborative room-brain. Keep the script sprint separate from notes/meetings; the survival anchors are voice, the question, and the locked ending. |
| Brand / campaign cadence | The two media become two production tracks (deep brand assets vs. fast reactive social). Batch and context-switch the same way; the input bank feeds both so the voice stays one voice across them. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **Survival, not polish.** The draft passes the Step 1 survival test — voice and direction legible cold, skeleton complete. A pretty fragment that dies in a month is a fail, not a head start.
- **Stakes demoted.** A real Tuesday reframe and a named redo path exist. If the piece is still being treated as a monument, it'll freeze; lower it before shipping the plan.
- **Modes separated, not blended.** Deep and fast have distinct blocks and a switch rule. If the plan runs both brains at once, it's contaminated — re-batch.
- **Input is a ritual, not a wish.** A fixed when/where/what feeds the work upstream, and the empty-day rule is acknowledged. Output planned with no intake plan is the empty well scheduled in advance.
- **Machinery invisible.** The plan executes the moves; it doesn't lecture the operator on which pattern is which.

## Common Pitfalls
- **The 25-page death.** Drafting deep and polished on page one, running out of voice before the skeleton closes — so the gap kills it. Recovery: in Step 1, bracket the holes and reach the ending in one pass; complete the skeleton before perfecting any section.
- **Treating the post like a monument.** The Tuesday piece carries The Piece pressure, so it never starts. Recovery: run Step 2 first — name the redo path and the play move; the stakes were the block, not the page.
- **Blending the two brains.** Drafting the essay while half-watching the feed, posting from the essay headspace — both come out half-pressured. Recovery: in Step 3, separate the blocks physically and enforce the switch rule; capture fast-mode seeds, don't widen them mid-block.
- **Output with no input.** Heavy publishing weeks with no intake, then the well runs dry and stakes-lowering can't fake an idea. Recovery: in Step 4, fix the ritual *before* the output block, keep the bank full, and honor the empty-day rule rather than grinding.
