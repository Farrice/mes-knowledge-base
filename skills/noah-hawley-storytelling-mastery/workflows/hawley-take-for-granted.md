# Take-for-Granted Audit

Hawley's literal first question on any project is "what am I taking for granted in approaching this?" For *Lucy in the Sky* the answer was the movie theater itself — the rectangle nobody questions — which became the instrument that carried the feeling of space. This is that question run on purpose: find the thing being treated as a given, make it a variable, and let the variable do the work.

## Input Required
- The project: what is being built (story, format, offer, positioning, brand, campaign).
- The category it lives in, and the 2–3 examples a reasonable person would compare it to.
- The intended audience and where they encounter it (the medium / channel / container).
- What "winning" looks like — the end state the project is trying to produce.
- Any hard constraints (budget, scope, channel, format) already in play.

---

## Workflow
### Step 1: List everything being taken for granted
Write down the givens — the things nobody on the project has questioned because the category hands them over pre-decided. Push past the obvious. The assumption that became Hawley's tool was not "the story" — it was the *rectangle of the screen*, an element so structural it's invisible. Work the same layers.

| Layer | What's assumed fixed here? | Who decided it (you / category / nobody)? |
|-------|----------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Medium / container** | (the screen is a rectangle; an email is text top-to-bottom; a Substack is weekly) | |
| **Format / shape** | (a detective story runs forward; a pitch is a deck; an offer is a tiered table) | |
| **Frame / POV** | (the narrator is reliable; the brand speaks *to* the buyer; the case is "solved") | |
| **Genre / category default** | (crime ends with a shootout; SaaS landing pages have a hero + 3 features) | |
| **Convention of the field** | (consultants charge by hour; courses are video modules) | |

Aim for 6–10 entries. If a row feels too obvious to write, write it anyway — the *Lucy in the Sky* move came from the most obvious-seeming given in the room.

### Step 2: Pick the one assumption that, if made variable, changes the identity of the thing
Not every given is load-bearing. The test: **if you turned this from fixed to variable, would the thing become a different thing?** *Memento* didn't change a detail of the detective genre — it reversed the structure, and "by changing the structure you're changing the whole identity." A flipped feature gives you a tweak; a flipped *identity* gives you a category of one.

Score the candidates:

| Candidate assumption | If variable, what changes? | Identity shift (1–5) | Carries the intended feeling/outcome? |
|----------------------|----------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------------------|
| | | | |

Choose the highest identity-shift candidate that also serves the end state from your inputs. A flip that's clever but doesn't carry the feeling or the outcome is the white-hat-at-noon twist — cut it.

### Step 3: Convert the assumption into a tool / move
Now make the variable *do work*. Hawley didn't just notice the theater was a rectangle — he assigned the rectangle a job: full screen + surround sound for the bigness of space, shrinking image + front-pulled sound for the smallness of ordinary life. The audience may never consciously notice. Specify your move at that level of concreteness.

Fill in:
- **The given (before):** the assumption in its fixed, default form.
- **The variable (after):** what it becomes when you stop treating it as fixed.
- **The job it now does:** the specific feeling, meaning, or differentiation it carries that it couldn't carry as a given.
- **How it shows up, concretely:** the actual mechanic on the page / screen / page-load / offer — not the concept, the execution. (Constraints from your inputs are fuel here, not walls — the single location, the one channel, the small budget is the forcing function. See Pattern 14.)

### Step 4: Stress-test — most-advanced-yet-acceptable
A flip can be different enough to register and still too strange to receive. Run the calibration: is the result advanced enough that it *lands as new*, and familiar enough that the audience has the receptors to take it in?

- **Register check:** Would the target audience recognize this as fresh, or shrug at it as ordinary? (If ordinary, the flip wasn't load-bearing — return to Step 2.)
- **Receive check:** Does the audience still have a handle to grab — enough of the inherited shape to orient — or is it so far out it has no receptors? (If unreceivable, dial back toward the convention until one anchor returns.)
- **Invisibility check:** Does the tool do its work without announcing itself? The best version, the audience "might not even be aware of." If you have to explain the flip for it to land, it's not a tool yet.

If it fails the register check, the assumption wasn't the load-bearing one — go back to Step 2. If it fails the receive check, you've flipped too far — keep one familiar anchor.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | Audit medium + structure + frame. The flip is usually structural (reversed timeline, unreliable container, the screen-as-instrument) — the kind that changes the show's identity, not a single scene. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | Audit the *series* shape and the reader's assumed posture. The given is often "weekly topic-of-the-week" or "I explain, you learn" — flip the frame (the reader is the protagonist, the season has a destination) per Translation 1. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | Audit the post's default shape (hook → list → CTA) and the assumed relationship (writer instructs reader). The highest-leverage flip is usually the frame: implicate the reader instead of informing them. |
| Brand / positioning / offer | This is the home use (Translation 6). Audit the *category default* every competitor accepts — the format the market hands you — and make it the differentiator. The constraint (no team, one channel) is the wedge, not the apology. |
| Campaign / multi-deliverable | Audit the assumed container per touchpoint. The flip should be a single identity-level move that every deliverable inherits, so the campaign reads as one coherent show, not five clever one-offs. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- The chosen assumption is genuinely *load-bearing* — flipping it changes the identity of the thing, not just a surface detail (rules out the cosmetic tweak masquerading as a flip).
- The tool is specified as **execution**, not concept — there's an actual mechanic on the page/screen/offer a stranger could build from, the way the theater move names "shrink the image, pull the sound forward."
- The flip survives both the register check (lands as new) and the receive check (keeps one familiar anchor) — most-advanced-yet-acceptable, per Pattern 12.
- The tool does its work without being announced; the audience "might not even be aware of it." If the flip needs explaining to land, it isn't a tool yet.
- The constraints from the inputs were used as fuel, not treated as walls (Pattern 14) — at least one limit became part of the wedge.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Auditing the content instead of the container.** Listing plot points or features is plot-listing in disguise. The givens you want are the structural ones nobody questions — the rectangle, not the movie. Recovery: re-run Step 1 and add a row for the most invisible, most obvious thing in the room.
- **Picking the clever flip over the load-bearing one.** A flip that's interesting but leaves the thing's identity intact is a tweak; it'll fail the register check downstream. Recovery: score on *identity shift* in Step 2, not on cleverness, and demote anything below a 4.
- **Stopping at the noticing.** Naming the assumption is half the move; assigning it a *job* is the other half. "The theater is a rectangle" is an observation — "use its size for the bigness of space" is the tool. Recovery: don't leave Step 3 until "the job it now does" and "concrete execution" are both filled with specifics.
- **Flipping so far the audience has no receptors.** Difference without a handle reads as noise, not innovation. Recovery: keep one inherited anchor (Step 4 receive check) so the audience can orient before the new move pays off.
