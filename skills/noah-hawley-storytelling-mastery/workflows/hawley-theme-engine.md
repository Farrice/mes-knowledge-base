# Theme Engine

Two men meet in an ER, one civilized and one its opposite — who are they? That is how a season starts, not "and then this happens." A writers' room defaults to plot because plot is the only language everyone shares, and the list-making part of the brain is the wrong part for the job. This workflow drags a brief back into the writing brain: find the question, name the theme as the question, and populate every beat from the theme outward — then kill the great twist that doesn't serve it.

## Input Required
- The brief — story premise, content angle, or existing plot/section list (raw is fine)
- The format and length it ships in (TV season, essay, post, campaign)
- Any beats, characters, or sections already locked
- Any "great twist" or set piece the author is already in love with
- The feeling the audience should leave with (one phrase, not a moral)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Find the Question / Image-Seed
Don't start from "what's the plot." Start from the unanswered thing under the brief. A question, or an image that has a question buried in it — the woman driving home with a man stuck in her windshield, who is he? You are looking for a seed whose answer you genuinely don't know yet. If you already know the answer, what you have is a lesson, and a lesson isn't a story.

Pull three candidate seeds out of the raw brief, then pressure-test each:

| Seed candidate | Stated as a question | Do I already know the answer? |
|---|---|---|
| (image or premise 1) | | yes = lesson, discard / no = keep |
| (image or premise 2) | | |
| (image or premise 3) | | |

Keep the one you can't answer. That one will pull the work.

### Step 2: State the Theme as a Question
Theme is not a moral and not a noun. It is the answer the whole work is reaching for, phrased as a question. *Fargo* is "decency versus cynicism — how can decency win?" Not "decency matters." The question form keeps it alive; the moral form kills it.

Convert the surviving seed into the theme-question, then sanity-check it against HK-2 — the theme must be **total for you and invisible to the audience**. If a character could say it aloud or the music could underline it, it's a message, not a theme. Rewrite until it disappears.

```
THEME (as a question): "How can ___ survive in a world of ___?"
The work will answer this — but never say it.
Audience-felt version: they should leave feeling ___ without ever hearing the question.
```

### Step 3: Theme-Break the Beats (Angles, Not Dominoes)
Now break — but redirect every beat away from "what happens next" toward "how does this relate to the theme?" (HK-1: someone has to drag the room from mechanics to meaning.) Each character, section, or beat is an **angle on the question**, not a domino that knocks the next one over. Populate from the theme outward, then let plot emerge from people who embody it.

For each element, answer: *which face of the question does this one turn toward?*

| Beat / character / section | Angle on the theme-question | Plot only? (flag) |
|---|---|---|
| (beat 1) | (the angle it takes — e.g. "decency that costs everything") | |
| (beat 2) | (e.g. "cynicism that looks like wisdom") | |
| (beat 3) | (e.g. "the bystander who has to choose") | |
| (beat 4) | (e.g. "decency that wins by refusing the fight") | |

Any row where the only honest answer is "it just moves the story forward" gets flagged. A beat that can't name its angle isn't broken yet — it's listed. Send it back through the theme before it earns a place.

### Step 4: The Anti-Theme Cut
Every brief has a beloved turn — the clever reversal, the set piece, the "great movie twist." Hold each one against the theme-question and ask whether it answers the question or betrays it. Reality rarely ends with white hat versus black hat in the town square at noon; a twist that delivers that tidy showdown is satisfying *and* a lie about how the world resolves. The biscuit scene exists because Hawley refused the expected one-more-set-piece kill and let the violence be withheld and inverted instead.

Run the love-list through the cut:

| Beloved twist / set piece | Serves the theme? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| (the clever turn) | (how — or how it betrays) | KEEP / CUT / INVERT |

For anything you can't bear to cut, try **invert** before **keep**: what if the protagonist refuses to play that scene, and it becomes its opposite? That is usually where the real beat was hiding.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV season | Theme-break the whole season as angles; the late-middle lull (HK-5) gets a structural answer — a timeline jump or location move — not a service C-story. Pyramid narrows in film; long-form holds room to the end. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The seed is the question the essay is actually answering, not its topic. Sections are angles on it; cut the neat takeaway that resolves the question into advice. The reader should feel the question, never read it. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | One question, one angle, one withheld answer. The post that states the lesson has spent the tension; leave the question live in the last line so the reader finishes it. |
| Brand / campaign | The theme-question is the brand's real conflict, not its tagline. Each asset is an angle; the "clever" ad concept that wins applause but contradicts the question is the white-hat-at-noon twist — cut it. |

## Output Format
```
SEED (the question/image): __________

THEME (as a question): "How can ___ ?"
  • Total for author / invisible to audience: [confirmed / needs rewrite]
  • Audience-felt residue: __________

THEME-BROKEN MAP (angles on the question):
  1. [beat] → [angle] 
  2. [beat] → [angle]
  3. [beat] → [angle]
  4. [beat] → [angle]
  (flagged plot-only beats sent back: ____)

THE CUT LIST:
  • CUT: [twist that betrays the theme] — why: ____
  • INVERT: [set piece] → [its refused/opposite version]
  • KEEP: [turn that serves the theme] — angle it carries: ____

ENDING CHECK: does the locked ending deepen the question rather than close it? [yes / no — fix]
```

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- The theme is a **question**, not a moral or a noun, and nowhere on the page is it stated aloud or underlined (HK-2).
- Every kept beat names its angle on the question; no row's only justification is "it moves the plot forward" (Pattern 1, HK-1).
- The seed's answer was genuinely unknown at the start — if you already knew it, you wrote a lesson and need a new seed (Pattern 2).
- At least one beloved twist faced the cut, and the white-hat-at-noon turn was killed or inverted rather than kept.
- The ending deepens the question instead of resolving it; if it closes the question into advice, it fails Ending Inevitability.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Stating the theme as a moral** ("decency matters"). It goes inert. Recovery: rephrase as the question the work answers and check that no character could say it.
- **Theme-painting after a plot list.** Breaking plot first, then bolting a theme on top, just decorates the outline. Recovery: throw out the sequence and re-break from the theme outward — angles, not dominoes.
- **Sparing the clever twist.** The turn that gets applause is often the one that betrays the question. Recovery: run the cut honestly; try INVERT (refuse the scene) before KEEP.
- **An ending that resolves instead of deepens.** A tidy answer flattens the work to a lesson. Recovery: re-read the ending against the seed — it should leave the question more alive, not answered.
