# Adaptation Mode

Hawley adapted the Coens, Marvel, Kathy Reichs, and Ridley Scott and somehow kept a reputation as television's most original voice. The trick is that he never re-watches the source. He sits with the feelings *Alien* left in him and builds a different story that produces those same feelings. This workflow runs that engine on whatever you're adapting — close the source, name what it did to you, and rebuild the doing from the ground up.

## Input Required
- **The source** — the film, book, essay, talk, or long-form piece being adapted (title + your honest first-person memory of it; do NOT plan to re-consume it).
- **The target format** — what it's becoming (TV pilot, screenplay, Substack edition, LinkedIn carousel, ad, video script).
- **The fidelity contract** — who must still recognize the source's *feeling*, and what (if anything) is contractually fixed (IP constraints, named characters, brand promise).
- **Your own swatch** — a sentence on how the source made *you* feel, before any analysis.

---

## Workflow

### Step 1: Name the Paint Swatch (do NOT re-consume the source)
Close the source. Re-reading or re-watching pulls you toward copying its plot — the exact trap. Instead, reconstruct from memory the emotions it produced in you, as discrete colors on a swatch. Hawley on *Alien*: not "what happened" but "what feelings did that movie produce in me."

Fill the swatch from felt memory, not from a synopsis:

| Slot | Prompt | Your answer |
|------|--------|-------------|
| The dominant feeling | What's the one emotion that outlasted the credits? | |
| The undertow | What's the quieter feeling running beneath it? | |
| The bodily note | Where did it land — gut, chest, throat, scalp? | |
| The unresolved | What did it refuse to settle for you? | |
| The moment it turned | The single beat where the feeling crystallized | |

If you reach for the plot to answer any row, stop — that row isn't a feeling yet. A swatch is what survives when the events are gone.

### Step 2: Reverse-Engineer the Recipe (ingredients in proportion)
The source produced those feelings through a *mixture* held in specific proportions — Hawley reads the Coens as comedy + violence + philosophy + Judaism, and the gray zone between them is where the work lives. Name the source's ingredients and, critically, their ratio. A recipe is not a list; it's a balance.

Fill in:

```
SOURCE RECIPE
- Ingredient A: ____________  (~__% — dominant)
- Ingredient B: ____________  (~__% )
- Ingredient C: ____________  (~__% )
- Ingredient D: ____________  (~__% — the trace that makes it specific)
THE GRAY ZONE: where do two ingredients collide to make the unease/charge? __________
THE FORBIDDEN MOVE: the obvious thing this source REFUSES to do (its restraint) __________
```

The proportions are the fingerprint. Comedy at 60% is a farce; the same comedy at 15% inside dread is *Fargo*. Get the ratio wrong and you've adapted a different source.

### Step 3: Build a Genuinely Different Story Native to the Target
Now invent. Do not transpose the source's events into the new format — induce the swatch through a story the source's author would not recognize as theirs, but would recognize the *feeling* in. Let the target format's physics dictate the mechanics: an essay does its build over paragraphs; a carousel does it in the turn between slides; a 44-minute episode does it in the cut.

Run these sub-questions:
- **What am I taking for granted about adapting this?** The default move (compress the plot, keep the famous beats) is the rectangle-theater assumption — name it, then refuse it.
- **What's the native tension→release in the new format?** Where does the build live, and where's the release that delivers the dominant feeling?
- **What do I withhold?** Find the source's loudest beat and *don't* play it — induce its feeling sideways, the way the soap dispenser carries what the confession set up.
- **What's my different story?** A premise with new characters/events/structure that mixes the recipe's ingredients in the source's proportions.

### Step 4: Fidelity-of-Feeling Check
The single test that separates an adaptation from a copy: **would someone who loves the source recognize its feeling without recognizing its plot?** Two failure poles — recognize the plot (you copied) or recognize neither (you drifted). You want feeling-yes, plot-no.

Run it against the swatch from Step 1, color by color. If the dominant feeling didn't survive the format jump, the adaptation failed at the heart and no surface fidelity rescues it. Re-mix the recipe proportions and rebuild Step 3 before delivering.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | Recipe ratio becomes the season's tonal architecture; the "different story" is a new premise that answers the source's buried question. Hold the gray zone across episodes, not within one scene. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The swatch becomes the edition's emotional arc; the recipe becomes the argument's ingredients. Build the dominant feeling across paragraphs; withhold the source's thesis and let the reader arrive at the feeling. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | The whole adaptation collapses to one tension→release unit. Pick ONE swatch color and induce it; the recipe shows you which single ingredient carries the post. The turn between hook and last line is your only format physics. |
| Brand / campaign | The recipe becomes the voice's generative logic — the ingredients in proportion that make every touchpoint feel "the same at its heart." Adapt the founder's origin story or category myth into the brand's feeling, never its literal events. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **No re-consumption residue.** If any swatch row or recipe ingredient is actually a plot point in disguise, the source is leaking through — rewrite it as a feeling.
- **The new story is genuinely different.** If a fan would recognize the source's *plot* in your scaffold, you copied. Recognition must land on feeling only (rubric: Adaptation Fidelity-of-Feeling).
- **The ratio is named, not just the ingredients.** A list of ingredients without proportions is not a recipe and will reproduce the wrong tone.
- **Something is withheld.** The source's loudest beat should be the one you don't play. If you kept every famous moment, you transposed instead of reconstructed (rubric: Restraint / Imagination Trigger).
- **The dominant feeling survived the format jump.** Verify against Step 1's top color specifically — surface fidelity on the minor colors does not rescue a lost dominant.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Re-watching/re-reading "for accuracy."** This is the failure that defines the workflow. The source's plot will overwrite your swatch and you'll end up compressing events. Recovery: close the source, work from felt memory only; if you genuinely can't remember the feeling, that absence is the answer — it didn't move you, so there's nothing to adapt.
- **Right ingredients, wrong proportions.** You name comedy + violence + philosophy but mix them as a 60% farce when the source was 15% comedy inside dread. Recovery: return to the gray zone — find where two ingredients collide and tune the ratio until that collision feels like the source again.
- **Format transposition disguised as adaptation.** Turning an essay into a carousel by summarizing its sections, slide per section. That's a copy in a new container. Recovery: ask what the carousel's *turn-between-slides* can do that the essay's slow build did — induce the feeling through the new format's native move, not a reformatted outline.
- **Keeping the famous beat.** Adapting *Fargo* by keeping the woodchipper. The recognizable moment is exactly what marks it a copy. Recovery: identify the source's loudest beat and deliberately withhold it; induce its feeling through a quieter, original moment the way the working soap dispenser carried the whole season's question.
