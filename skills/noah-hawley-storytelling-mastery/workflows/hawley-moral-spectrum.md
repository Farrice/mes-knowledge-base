# Moral Spectrum

Stop sorting your cast into a white hat and a black hat in the town square at noon. Reality doesn't end there, and neither does Hawley. The most dangerous figure on the page is rarely the villain — it's the ordinary person at the fence who, given the right room and the right night, reveals a capacity for harm the villain never had to discover, because the villain was born to it. This workflow places every character on the spectrum, then builds the unremarkable middle that becomes the moral engine.

## Input Required
- The story/world and its core **theme-question** (e.g. *Fargo*: "how can decency win against cynicism?"). If you only have a premise, name the question first.
- The **ensemble** — every character/voice/position you're casting (3+). Rough sketches are fine.
- The **payoff the audience is being trained to want** (the violence, the comeuppance, the win) — what does the genre promise them?
- The **format and length** you're building for (informs how much middle you can develop).

## Workflow

### Step 1: Place the Spectrum
Lay every character on a single axis from a clear moral pole to its opposite. Don't stop at two poles — the whole point is the gradient between them. The "good end" and "bad end" anchor the line; the interesting work is everyone in between, especially anyone who looks like they don't belong on a moral axis at all (the bystander, the climber, the kid).

For each character, fill:

| Character | Position (good ↔ evil) | Their moral logic (how *they* justify it) | What the audience first reads them as |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| | | | |

The justification column is load-bearing. A villain who knows they're a villain is flat. Everyone on this line believes they're the reasonable one — name the belief.

### Step 2: Build the Ordinary Middle
Find or design the unremarkable person in the center — and make *their ordinariness* the engine. This is the *Fargo* S2 move: the audience roots against the crime family, so the family sends the kid who "just wants to prove he's a man," and suddenly the violence the audience was hungry for becomes unbearable. Banality, not malice, is what implicates the viewer.

Pressure-test the middle character with these sub-questions:
- What does this person *want* that is completely ordinary? (To be respected. To not be a coward. To keep their job. To be a man.)
- What small, reasonable-feeling step pulls them toward the worse end? (Not a choice to be evil — a choice to belong, to comply, to not make a scene.)
- At what moment does the audience realize this ordinary want has put them past the villain? When does "I get it" curdle into "oh no, I get it"?
- If you removed this character, does the spectrum collapse into hero-vs-villain flatness? (If no, the middle isn't doing its job — rebuild it.)

Write the middle character's arc as one sentence: *"An ordinary person who only wanted ______ ends up ______, and the audience watched it happen one understandable step at a time."*

### Step 3: Map Audience Allegiance (and the Indictment)
Allegiance is not fixed — it moves, and the moves are the story. Chart where the audience's sympathy sits across the arc, and mark the moment the rooting turns back on them. The strongest version of this map has at least one beat where the audience gets exactly what it wanted and hates having wanted it ("why did I want this?").

| Story beat | Who the audience roots for | Who they root against | The shift / the indictment |
|------------|----------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------------|
| Open | | | (establish the easy alignment) |
| Mid | | | (complicate it — sympathy bleeds across the line) |
| Turn | | | (the payoff arrives and implicates the viewer) |
| Land | | | (allegiance settles somewhere uncomfortable) |

Hold the gray zone — don't resolve it. The audience should not be told whether to feel vindicated or sick. The unease is the deliverable.

### Step 4: Anchor Every Position to the Theme-Question
Each character on the spectrum is an *answer* the work is testing against the theme-question. Decency, cynicism, complicity, ambition, fear — each pole and each midpoint argues a different reply. Tie them back so the spectrum stops being a character chart and becomes a thematic argument.

For each character: *"On the question of [theme-question], this character's life argues that the answer is ______ — and the story either confirms or breaks that argument by the end."*

Then check coherence: any character who doesn't argue *something* on the theme-question is service creep. They're on the page to fill a slot, not to test the question. Broom them or rebuild them until they carry weight.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | Full ensemble across the season; the middle character gets a B-arc that crosses the main story at the turn. Allegiance map = the season's emotional spine, beat by episode. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | Cast *positions/voices* on the spectrum (e.g. the cynic, the true believer, the person quietly going along). The "ordinary middle" is often the reader — set them up to agree, then implicate. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn/X) | Compress to three positions max: the obvious villain, the obvious hero, and the fence-sitter who is secretly you. The post's turn is the indictment in Step 3 — "we both did this." |
| Brand / campaign | Map the market's moral roles (the bad actor everyone names, the white-hat the category promises, the ordinary buyer who enables the bad outcome by default). The campaign's power move is making the audience see their own complicity, then offering the better step. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **No flat poles.** The villain has a justification, the hero has a flaw, and there is real gradient between them. If the map reads as hero-vs-villain, the middle isn't engineered.
- **The middle is ordinary, not edgy.** The engine character's danger comes from a relatable want (belonging, respect, not being a coward), not from being secretly sinister. Edgy is cheap; ordinary is unbearable.
- **At least one genuine indictment.** Somewhere the audience gets what it wanted and is made to ask "why did I want this?" If the allegiance map never turns on the viewer, you've written a chart, not a mirror.
- **Gray zone held, not resolved.** The output does not tell the audience whether to feel vindicated or sick. Score against the Moral Complexity rubric anchor (10): the audience is made complicit, not seated outside it.
- **Every position argues the theme.** Each character is a live answer to the theme-question. Anyone who isn't gets broomed (Coherence Over Continuity).

## Common Pitfalls
- **Symmetric "shades of gray" with no engine.** A spectrum where everyone is equally morally murky is mush, not complexity. Recovery: find the one ordinary character whose specific, relatable want drives the worst outcome — that asymmetry is what makes the gradient mean something.
- **The villain does the implicating.** If the audience's discomfort comes from the obvious bad guy, you've spent the easy currency. Recovery: route the indictment through the fence-sitter the audience already excused — complicity lands hardest from someone we let off the hook.
- **Stating the moral instead of building the mirror.** Telling the reader "we are all complicit" is emotionality — the sad music before the sad beat. Recovery: build the wanting in Step 3 first, deliver the payoff, and let the recognition arrive without narration.
- **Allegiance treated as fixed.** A map with one row (root for the hero, against the villain, the end) misses the whole move. Recovery: force a beat where sympathy crosses the line, then a turn where the rooting indicts.
