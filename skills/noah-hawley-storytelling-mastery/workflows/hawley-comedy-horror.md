# Comedy-Horror Engine

A laugh and a flinch run on the same engine: tension, tension, tension, release. Build the suspense once, then choose what comes out the other end — a punchline or the thing in the basement. The richest version doesn't choose, it holds the ambiguity, the way Chigurh's Prince Valiant haircut is hysterical and unsettling at the same time and scarier for it.

## Input Required
- The raw scene / beat / hook (a paragraph, a logline, or "here's the moment I'm trying to land")
- What the audience is supposed to **fear or want** in this moment (if you can't name it, Step 1 will)
- The register you're after: comic, dread, or undecided (undecided is allowed — it often becomes the gray zone)
- The format and length budget (one scene, one post, one 6-second hook?)
- One detail of vulnerability already present, if any (a person alone, a thing that doesn't work, a door left open)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Locate the tension source
Before anything is built, name the live wire. A unit without a fear or a want underneath it has nothing to release — it's just events. Two questions do the work:

| Question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| What does the audience **fear** here? (loss, exposure, the thing arriving) | |
| What does the audience **want** here? (the kill, the kiss, the confirmation, the punchline) | |
| Whose **vulnerability** carries it? (who is exposed, and for how long?) | |

Fear and want are often the same coin. In *Fargo* the audience wants the violent payoff *because* they've been trained that violence is progress — and the fear is that it'll actually arrive. Find the coin. If you named a fear but no want (or vice versa), keep digging until both are on the table.

### Step 2: Build — linger, withhold, trigger the imagination
The build is not "more stuff happens." The build is *staying* on the vulnerability long enough that the audience's own imagination does the work. *Paranormal Activity* cost $15,000 and worked not by showing the threat but by showing how vulnerable a sleeping person is eight hours a day — a drawer slides in the corner of frame and the adrenaline spikes, because the imagination already supplied the monster.

Fill this:

```
THE VULNERABILITY WE STAY ON:  ____  (the exposed person / object / silence)
WHAT WE WITHHOLD:              ____  (the threat we never show, the punchline we delay, the score we cut)
THE SMALL TRIGGER:             ____  (the drawer that slides — the tiny thing the imagination amplifies)
HOW LONG WE HOLD:              ____  (longer = bigger release; too long = they shift and you've lost them)
```

Rule of the build: underwrite the obvious. If you find yourself explaining the danger, showing the monster early, or scoring the moment before it lands, you're spending the imagination you should be triggering. Show the vulnerability, not the threat.

### Step 3: Choose the release — and decide the gray zone
The build is register-neutral. The *release* is where comedy and horror split — same staircase, different door at the top. Decide which door:

| Release | What comes out | When it's right |
|---|---|---|
| **Punchline** | The tension breaks into laughter | The fear was secretly absurd; the want gets subverted into a joke |
| **The thing in the basement** | The tension breaks into dread | The fear arrives, or worse, *almost* arrives and we're left with it |
| **The gray zone (hold both)** | The audience laughs and flinches at once and isn't sure which is correct | The unease is the point — Chigurh's haircut, the Coen register |

Then the real decision: **do you resolve the register, or hold it?** Resolving tells the audience whether to laugh or flinch. Holding refuses to. The held version is harder and usually better — the ambiguity is what makes a thing stay with someone. If you're holding the gray zone, do *not* let the music, the framing, or a character's reaction tell the audience how to feel. Leave them productively unsure.

### Step 4: Test note — you can't feel your own suspense
Here's the honest limit, straight from Hawley: you can make yourself feel grief, joy, adrenaline — but never *your own* suspense, because suspense needs not-knowing and you know how it pays off. So you cannot self-validate timing. You have to instrument it.

Write the test note that ships *with* the unit:

```
TEST WITH:        a real reader who does NOT know the payoff
WATCH FOR:        behavior, not opinion — do they lean in, or shift and check their phone?
THE TIMING DIAL:  hold a beat longer → bigger reaction; one beat too long → "they're shifting, I've lost them"
THE GRAY-ZONE CHECK: ask nothing. If they laugh AND look unsettled, the ambiguity landed.
```

Behavioral signal beats stated opinion every time. A focus-group survey can be rigged; a body leaning forward cannot.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | The full build — linger on screen time, withhold the reverse shot, save the score for the release (the soap-dispenser move). Hold the gray zone with framing, not dialogue. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The "vulnerability we stay on" is a paragraph the reader sits inside before the turn. Withhold the thesis; let a small concrete detail trigger the dread or the laugh. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | Compress the whole machine into 2–4 lines: one line of vulnerability, one beat of withholding, the release in the last line. The hook *is* the tension; the last line is the basement door. |
| Brand / campaign | The fear/want is the customer's. Build tension on their vulnerability (the silent failure, the thing that doesn't work for them), release into relief — and decide if a wink (comic) or a chill (dread) sells harder. |
| Comedy bit / sketch | Run it as horror with the funny side played (Key & Peele). Same suspense build; the punchline is just the release the dread version would have made a scream. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **One machine, not two.** Did you build a single tension→release unit, or did you write a "funny part" and a "scary part" bolted together? If they're separate, it failed.
- **Withholding is present.** Name what you withheld (threat, score, punchline, explanation). If you showed and explained everything, you spent the imagination instead of triggering it.
- **The gray-zone call is explicit.** You either held the ambiguity on purpose or resolved it on purpose — never by accident. If you resolved it, the music/framing/reaction is *not* telling the audience what to feel against your wishes.
- **No emotionality.** The release is delivered through an action or object, not through a label or a cue that arrives before the moment (no sad music before the sad beat).
- **The test note is real.** It names a not-knowing proxy and watches behavior, not opinion — because you cannot feel your own suspense.

## Common Pitfalls
- **You showed the monster.** The threat appeared early and the imagination had nothing left to do. → Cut to the vulnerability; let a small trigger (the sliding drawer) carry it, and keep the threat offscreen longer than feels comfortable.
- **You resolved the gray zone without meaning to.** A reaction shot or a music cue told the audience to laugh, killing the dread (or vice versa). → Strip the cue. If you're holding both, nothing on the page may adjudicate the feeling for the viewer.
- **You scored the build instead of the release.** Emphasis spent early, so the payoff has nowhere to detonate. → Leave the build dry; save the one note for the moment it breaks (the soap-dispenser detonation).
- **You self-certified the timing.** You decided it "lands" from inside your own head, where you already know the payoff. → You can't feel your own suspense. Ship the test note; instrument a real reader's body, not their survey.
