# Tone-Embodying Pitch

Hawley once pitched a 70s-anti-hero crime show by riffing about a *segue* until the executives realized the segue was the pitch — the wrapper had the same tone as the work. An irreverent comedy earns an irreverent outline; a slow-dread thriller earns an outline that makes the reader's skin prickle before plot enters. The container is not packaging for the content. It is the content's first scene.

## Input Required
- **The work** being pitched/outlined/introduced (logline, premise, or the piece itself).
- **The recipient** and their default expectation of this artifact type (an exec expecting bullet points, a reader expecting a TLDR, a client expecting a deck).
- **The tone** you believe the work has, in your own words — even rough.
- **The container format** (cold-open intro, one-page outline, pitch email, deck, video VO, landing-page hero).
- **The reveal target** — the moment you want the recipient to realize the container *was* the work.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Name the tone precisely
"Funny" and "dark" are not tones — they are genres. Push until you have a register a reader could *feel*, then locate it on the one dial that matters: where the tension sits and how it releases. The richest tones live in the gray zone where the recipient isn't sure whether to laugh or flinch.

Fill this in before writing a single line of container:

| Probe | Answer |
|-------|--------|
| If the work were a room, what does it feel like to stand in it? | |
| What is the tension, and what is the release (punchline / dread / catharsis)? | |
| What gray-zone ambiguity do I refuse to resolve for the reader? | |
| One word a stranger should land on after the first sentence (not the genre — the *feeling*) | |
| What would betray this tone instantly? (the off-note that proves I found the right note) | |

If the tone answers are generic, you will write a generic container. Sharpen here or stop.

### Step 2: Design the container to *reproduce* that tone, not describe it
Now build the artifact so its own reading experience runs the same tension→release machine as the work. Do not write *about* the tone. Stage it. The reader's pulse, confusion, smirk, or held breath while consuming the container should match the pulse the finished work produces.

Translate the named tone into container mechanics:

| If the tone is… | The container does… |
|------------------|--------------------|
| Irreverent / fast | Breaks its own format, jokes at its own expense, refuses the expected outline structure |
| Slow dread | Withholds; under-explains; lets a small detail sit too long; leaves the threat offscreen |
| Deadpan absurd | States the impossible flatly, no wink; lets the reader supply the laugh |
| Tender beneath violence | Opens hard, then turns on an ordinary act (breaking bread, a working soap dispenser) |
| Cosmic / vertiginous | Shifts scale mid-paragraph — intimate, then suddenly vast — then back |

Underwrite the obvious. If the work is scary, the most scared the reader should be is in the sentence where nothing visibly happens. Where would you normally add emphasis, music, an adjective? Cut it there, and save it for one detonation.

### Step 3: Engineer the reveal — the container *was* the pitch
The container should run as a complete experience first, and only late should the recipient realize they were already inside the work. The segue story is the model: the executives thought Hawley was warming up; the warm-up was the show. Place the reveal so it lands as recognition, not as a label you stamped on it.

Choose the reveal mechanic:

| Mechanic | How it lands |
|----------|-------------|
| **The turn** | Run the container straight, then one line reframes everything they just read as the work itself |
| **The withheld name** | Never call it a pitch; let them name it ("wait — *this* is the show") |
| **The dry-then-score** | Keep the whole container restrained, then release one charged beat that *is* the work's signature moment |
| **The mirror** | Make them want one thing through the container, then deliver it so they feel the work's actual stakes |

Never write "as you can see, this captures the tone." Naming the machinery kills it. Let the reveal be theirs to have.

### Step 4: Coherence check — every beat in the work's tone, nothing serving the format
Walk the container beat by beat. Each one either reproduces the tone or it is service creep — a line that exists because pitches "are supposed to" have it (a stats slide, a comp-titles list, a polite sign-off) and quietly breaks the spell. Broom anything present only to satisfy the artifact convention rather than the work.

For each beat, answer: *does this beat feel like the work, or like a pitch about the work?* If the latter, cut it or rewrite it in tone. One off-key beat tells the recipient the rest was performance.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | The pitch is a cold open — let them watch a scene that is the tone, then reveal it's the pitch. Outline reads in the show's voice, not a beat-list register. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The intro *is* the essay's nervous system. The reader should be inside the piece's tone in sentence one; the "this is what I'll argue" reveal arrives only after the feeling already landed. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn/X) | The hook is the entire container — three lines that reproduce the post's tone, with the turn doing the reveal. No "here's a thread about." The format *is* the demonstration. |
| Brand / campaign | The deck, the landing hero, the proposal email each run the brand's tone as experience. The client should feel the brand before they reach the strategy slide; the strategy then reads as inevitable. |
| Cold pitch email | The email embodies the work's register so completely that the ask feels like the next beat, not a separate plea. The reveal: they realize they already want it. |

## Output Format
```
WORK: [logline / premise]
RECIPIENT + DEFAULT EXPECTATION: [who, and what artifact they expect]

TONE (named precisely):
- Standing-in-the-room feel: [...]
- Tension → release: [...] → [...]
- Held ambiguity (do not resolve): [...]
- One-word target feeling: [...]
- The betraying off-note: [...]

THE CONTAINER (reads as the work — no meta-language):
[the actual pitch / outline / intro, written entirely in the work's tone.
 The reader should feel the tone here before any content is explained.]

THE REVEAL:
- Mechanic: [turn / withheld name / dry-then-score / mirror]
- The exact line or beat where the recipient realizes the container WAS the work:
  [...]

COHERENCE PASS:
- Beats that reproduce the tone: [list]
- Service-creep beats broomed: [what was cut and why]
```

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **Felt before told.** A stranger reading only the first sentence lands on the target feeling without being told the genre. If the tone is announced rather than experienced, rewrite.
- **No meta-language.** The container never says "this captures," "the tone is," "as you can see." The machinery stays off the page (Anti-Pattern: naming the machinery).
- **The reveal is recognition, not a label.** The recipient *discovers* the container was the work; you did not point at it.
- **Tonal architecture holds.** Every beat rides the same tension→release machine as the work; the gray zone is preserved, not resolved (Rubric: Tonal Architecture).
- **No service creep.** No beat exists to satisfy pitch convention (stats slide, comp list, polite outro) at the cost of the tone (Rubric: Coherence over Service).

## Common Pitfalls
- **Describing the tone instead of reproducing it.** "It's a darkly funny crime thriller" is a comp-titles sentence, not a tone. → Go back to Step 1; if you can't write a line that *makes* the reader feel it, you haven't named the tone yet.
- **A polished pitch with no work inside it.** The container is well-made but reads like every other pitch — the reveal has nothing to detonate against. → Cut the convention beats (Step 4) and let one charged moment carry the work's signature.
- **Resolving the ambiguity to seem clear.** Over-explaining the tone to avoid confusing the recipient flattens the gray zone that made the work alive. → Trust the reader; withhold; the unease is the point.
- **The reveal arrives as a stamp.** Ending with "and that's the pitch / that's the tone" announces the trick and kills it. → Move the reveal earlier and let the recipient name it themselves (Step 3, withheld-name mechanic).
