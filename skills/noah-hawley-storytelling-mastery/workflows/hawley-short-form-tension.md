# Short-Form Tension

Most posts release in the hook and then spend four lines explaining the thing they already gave away. This builds the opposite: one tension→release unit where the hook withholds, the body tightens, and the last line lands — then turns so the reader catches their own reflection in it. The register can be a laugh or a flinch; the machine underneath is the same.

## Input Required
- **The raw observation or claim** — what you noticed, believe, or want to say (one sentence is fine; messy is fine).
- **The reader** — who's scrolling past, and what they currently assume about this topic.
- **The turn** — the thing the reader is doing that they don't see yet. (If you can't name it, the post stays at "instruction" and won't land.)
- **Register lean** — comic, dread-adjacent (stakes/fear/loss), or "either, pick for me."
- **Platform** — LinkedIn (a little more room) or X (compress hard).

## Workflow

### Step 1: Build the post as ONE tension→release unit
Lay the post on a single spine: **hook = tension, body = build, last line = release.** The hook opens a gap and refuses to close it. The body tightens the gap line by line — each line should make the reader need the next one. The last line is the only release in the whole post.

Diagnose the failure modes before you draft:

| Failure | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Released in the hook** | The first line already states the payoff ("Here is the lesson: be consistent.") | Move the payoff to the last line. Open with the tension that *earns* it. |
| **Never built** | A list of true-but-flat statements; no line needs the next | Stack the lines so each one raises the stakes or narrows the gap |
| **Two releases** | A "punchline" mid-post, then another at the end | One release. Cut the first; let pressure ride to the floor. |

Draft the spine as three anchors before writing any connective tissue:
- **HOOK (the gap):** _______ ← what's withheld; what tension opens
- **BUILD (the squeeze):** _______ → _______ → _______ ← each line tightens
- **RELEASE (the last line):** _______ ← the only payoff, placed last

### Step 2: Choose the register — comic or dread-adjacent — on the same machine
The spine doesn't change with register; the *pressure* changes. Comic rides toward a recognition that lands as a laugh. Dread-adjacent rides toward stakes — what's quietly being lost, feared, or risked. Same tension→release, different temperature at the floor.

| Register | The tension is built from | The release feels like |
|---|---|---|
| **Comic** | An absurdity the reader recognizes but hasn't said out loud | A laugh that's also a confession |
| **Dread-adjacent** | A cost the reader is paying without noticing | A flinch — the quiet "oh, that's me" |

Hold the gray zone where you can. The strongest short-form sits where the reader isn't sure whether to laugh or wince — that ambiguity is what makes them stop scrolling and read it twice. Don't resolve which one they're supposed to feel.

### Step 3: Add the self-implicating turn
Set the reader up to want a clean conclusion — a villain to blame, a tactic to copy, a side to be on. Then turn it so the conclusion includes them. The move: **make them want the answer, then make the answer a mirror.**

The shape that works (execute it, don't print the label):
- The reader assumes the problem is *out there* (other people, the algorithm, the market).
- You let them ride that assumption for most of the post.
- The last line folds it back: the thing you were diagnosing is the thing you're both doing.

The "so have I" beat is load-bearing. Complicity beats instruction. "You've been doing X" is a finger-point; "You've been doing X — so have I, and here's why we both keep choosing it" is a mirror. Implicate yourself or the turn reads as superior.

Pressure-test the turn:
- Does the reader see themselves, or just agree with you? (Agreement = no turn.)
- Did you earn the turn, or spring it? (The build has to make the turn feel inevitable, not clever.)

### Step 4: Withhold pass
Cut the over-explanation. A post that explains everything triggers nothing — the reader needs a space to fill, or there's no work for their mind to do. This is where most drafts die: the writer, nervous the point won't land, says it three times.

Run the subtractions:
- **Delete the line that explains the turn.** If the last line lands, the explanation underneath it is "the sad music before the sad moment." Trust it.
- **Delete the throat-clear.** Anything before the actual tension is warm-up. Open on the gap.
- **Cut to one stakes-line, not three.** Show the vulnerability once. Repetition signals the writer doesn't trust the reader.
- **Leave the implication unstated.** The reader finishing your sentence in their own head beats you finishing it for them.

Final read: if you can remove a line and the tension *increases*, the line was insulation. Cut it.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| LinkedIn | Hook gets a hard line break (it lives above the "see more" fold and must open the gap there). Build can run 4–7 short lines; one idea per line. Last line stands alone with whitespace above it. |
| X (single) | One spine, ruthlessly compressed. Hook and turn often collapse into two lines. No build paragraph — the build *is* the line breaks. |
| X (thread) | Each tweet is a micro tension→release, but the *thread* is one spine: first tweet = the gap, last tweet = the turn. Don't release per-tweet or the thread has no floor. |
| Substack note / short post | More room to build the dread or the absurdity, but the same single-release rule. Resist the long-form instinct to add a "takeaway" — the turn is the takeaway. |

## Output Format
```
PLATFORM: [LinkedIn / X / note]   REGISTER: [comic / dread-adjacent / gray]
THE GAP (hook withholds): [one line]
THE TURN (last line implicates): [one line]

— FINISHED POST —
[hook]

[build line]
[build line]
[build line]

[release / turn — last line, standing alone]

[full post, same spine, laugh at the floor]

— VARIANT B (dread-leaning) —
[full post, same spine, flinch at the floor]

WITHHOLD NOTE: [what you cut and why — the line you were tempted to add]
```

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **Single release.** The payoff lands once, on the last line — not in the hook, not mid-post. If the first line gives away the ending, rebuild from the gap.
- **The turn is a mirror, not a wag.** The reader recognizes themselves; you've implicated yourself too ("so have I"). Pure instruction or finger-pointing fails the Moral Mirror.
- **Built tension, not stacked facts.** Each line needs the next. If any line could be deleted without the reader noticing, the build is insulation.
- **Withheld the explanation.** No line restates the turn. The space is left for the reader to close.
- **Register held, ambiguity intact.** Comic and dread variants ride the same spine; where it's gray, you didn't resolve whether to laugh or flinch.

## Common Pitfalls
- **The hook is the thesis.** Stating the conclusion first feels safe and kills the tension. Recovery: take your strongest line, move it to the very end, and ask what gap would make a reader *need* to reach it.
- **The turn points outward.** "People who do X are the problem" satisfies the reader's ego and skips the mirror. Recovery: add the "so have I" beat — fold yourself into the diagnosis, then the post indicts instead of flatters.
- **Over-explained release.** The writer doesn't trust the last line and tacks on a sentence telling the reader what it meant. Recovery: delete it; if the post collapses without the explanation, the build was too weak — fix the build, not the explanation.
- **Resolved the gray zone.** Telling the reader it's a joke (or that it's serious) flattens the unease that made them stop. Recovery: cut the tonal signpost and let the last line do both at once.
