# Voice Bible

Eight writers in a room only speak plot, so Hawley breaks them by theme instead. A brand with eight makers has the same problem one altitude up: how do a designer, a copywriter, the operator, and an AI agent all sound like one voice without a style guide flattening them into mannequins. The fix is the same fix — teach the engine that generates the voice, not the phrases the voice happens to use. A style guide tells a stranger which words to copy. A voice bible tells a stranger which feelings to induce and lets them find their own words.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: Before executing, run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` — confirm you can answer the question this voice exists to answer, the ending state it always drives a reader toward, and the assumption being taken for granted (usually: "a voice = a list of words and rules"). If the voice has no question underneath it, you have a style sheet, not a voice — stop and find the question first.

## Input Required
- **The voice to codify** — a brand, a person, a publication, or a persona. Name whose voice this is and who will use the bible (human makers, AI agents, or both).
- **3–8 samples in the voice** — real pieces that unmistakably sound like it. The more channels represented (long-form, post, email, reply), the better the extraction.
- **The job to be done** — what makers will produce with this bible (newsletter editions, ad copy, support replies, social posts). The bible is built to generate *that*.
- **One counter-sample** — a piece that is competent but does *not* sound like the voice. The gap between the two is where the engine hides.
- *(Optional)* Any non-negotiables already known — words it must never use, claims it can't make, lines it won't cross.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Extract the generative logic
Don't start with how it sounds. Start with why it sounds that way. Read the samples for the spirit, not the surface — the recipe of ingredients in their proportions, the way you'd extract the Coens' recipe (comedy, violence, philosophy, Judaism) rather than transcribe their dialogue. Answer these before touching vocabulary:

| Engine component | The question to answer | What good looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **The core question** | What question is this voice always, invisibly, answering? | A real question, not a slogan. "Why do smart people stay stuck?" not "we empower founders." |
| **The feelings induced** | What "paint swatch" of emotions does a reader feel by the end? Name 3–5. | Felt states a reader carries out the door — *seen, slightly unsettled, more capable* — not adjectives the brand uses about itself. |
| **The recipe (proportions)** | What are the 3–5 ingredients, and in what ratio? | "60% lived concrete detail, 25% dry wit, 15% one hard truth." Proportion is the voice; same ingredients in a different ratio is a different voice. |
| **The relationship to the reader** | Who is the reader to this voice — student, peer, accomplice, suspect? | Determines posture before a single word. The Moral Mirror voice treats the reader as an accomplice; the explainer voice treats them as a student. |

Run the counter-sample against this. The competent-but-off piece usually has the right ingredients in the wrong proportion, or answers no question at all. Name the divergence — that gap is the engine made visible.

### Step 2: Surface markers second — and explicitly subordinate
Only now describe how it sounds, and label this section as downstream of Step 1. Markers are evidence of the engine, never the engine. Capture them so a maker can self-check, not so they can imitate:

- **Vocabulary & register** — words it reaches for, words it avoids, formality level, sentence length tendency.
- **Rhythm** — does it run long then snap short? Build then release? Where does it leave space (Hawley's "score where music doesn't go" — where does this voice *withhold*)?
- **Moves it makes** — its tension→release shape, its signature turn, how it opens, how it closes.
- **What it never does** — the bright lines. Often more defining than what it does. (The voice that "never explains the joke," "never opens with a definition," "never resolves the ambiguity for the reader.")

Write one sentence at the top of this section in this spirit: *These are the residue of the engine above. A maker who nails Step 1 will produce most of these naturally; a maker who copies these without Step 1 will sound like a forgery.*

### Step 3: Write rules a stranger could write a new episode from
Convert the engine into generative instructions — Hawley's Catch-22 assignment, "write a new chapter in the voice of the book." Each rule must be *productive* (tells a stranger how to make a choice) not *descriptive* (tells them what the voice looks like). Test every rule: could a maker who has never read the samples use it to generate a fresh piece?

- Lead with **the question to answer** and **the feelings to induce** — every rule serves those.
- State the recipe proportions as a working ratio the maker can dial against.
- Give 2–3 **decision rules** for live choices: "When tempted to explain, withhold and trust the reader to fill it." "When a line is clever but answers no question, cut it." "Score the small released moment, not the big obvious one."
- Add the **bright lines** from Step 2 as hard constraints.
- Keep it tight enough to hold in the head while writing. A bible nobody can carry while drafting is a binder, not a bible.

### Step 4: Channel-test with a fresh maker
Prove the bible generates voice, not mimicry. Have a *new* maker (a different persona, a fresh agent, or yourself cold) write one short piece in the target channel using only the bible — no access to the original samples. Then judge:

| Test | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| **Engine match** | Does it sound like the voice *because it answers the same question and induces the same feelings* — not because it reused phrases? |
| **Novelty** | Is it a genuinely new piece (new topic, new angle), or a paraphrase of a sample? A bible that only reproduces samples failed. |
| **Forgery test** | Would a reader of the original voice recognize this as a new episode, or feel the uncanny "someone is doing an impression" wrongness? |
| **Proportion** | Does the recipe ratio hold, or did one ingredient swamp the rest? |

If it passes only by copying phrases, Step 1 is too thin — the engine wasn't actually extracted, so the maker fell back on surface. Return to Step 1, deepen the question and the feelings, and re-test.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Brand / campaign voice (many makers) | The primary case. Recipe proportions matter most — this is what keeps eight makers coherent without flattening them. Channel-test with each maker type that will actually use it. |
| AI-agent voice spec | Front-load the question + feelings + bright lines as the system's standing instruction; agents over-rely on surface markers, so make the engine the first thing they read and the markers a self-check at the end. |
| Newsletter / Substack persona | Treat the voice as the showrunner's voice across a season; the recipe holds across editions even as topics change. Pair with `/hawley-content-season` for the arc. |
| Personal / founder voice | Extract from real writing, not aspiration. The counter-sample is often the founder's own "professional" mode that sounds like nobody. The bible recovers the engine of how they actually talk. |

## Output Format
```
# Voice Bible: [Voice Name]
Built for: [makers / agents who will use this] · Job to be done: [what they'll produce]

## PART 1 — THE ENGINE (generative logic — this is the voice)
The question this voice answers: [a real question]
Feelings it induces (the paint swatch): [3–5 felt states a reader leaves with]
The recipe (ingredients × proportion): [e.g. 60% X / 25% Y / 15% Z]
The reader is: [student / peer / accomplice / suspect — and why]
What competent-but-off looks like: [the counter-sample's divergence]

## PART 2 — SURFACE MARKERS (downstream of Part 1 — evidence, not engine)
> These are the residue of the engine above. Nail Part 1 and most appear naturally.
Vocabulary & register: [...]
Rhythm & where it withholds: [...]
Signature moves (open / turn / close): [...]
What it never does (bright lines): [...]

## PART 3 — GENERATIVE RULES (write a new episode from these)
1. Answer the question: [...]
2. Induce the feelings: [...]
3. Hold the recipe ratio: [...]
4. Decision rules (live choices): [2–3]
5. Hard constraints: [bright lines]

## PART 4 — CHANNEL TEST
New maker: [persona/agent] · Piece written from bible only: [link/inline]
Engine match: [PASS/FAIL — sounds like voice because of engine, not phrases?]
Novelty: [PASS/FAIL — new piece, not paraphrase?]

```

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (esp. *Adaptation Fidelity-of-Feeling* and *Coherence over Service*). Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- **Engine precedes surface.** Part 1 is the question and the feelings, not vocabulary. If the bible opens with a word list, it's a style sheet — rebuild.
- **Rules are generative, not descriptive.** Every rule passes "could a stranger write a fresh piece from this?" Descriptions of how the voice *looks* don't count.
- **Proportion is stated, not just ingredients.** The recipe carries a ratio. Same ingredients in a different ratio is a different voice.
- **The channel test actually ran** with a maker who never saw the samples — and passed by engine, not by phrase-reuse. A bible delivered without a passed channel test is unverified.
- **No naming the machinery on the page.** The bible teaches makers to write in voice; it does not lecture them about "Pattern 15" or "generative logic theory." Show the engine through the rules, don't label it.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Style guide in bible clothing.** You captured vocabulary, rhythm, and dos/don'ts but never named the question or the feelings — makers will produce competent mimicry that sounds hollow. Recovery: go back to the samples and ask what question they're all secretly answering; that's the missing engine.
- **The bible only reproduces samples.** The channel test produces paraphrases of existing pieces, not new episodes. The engine wasn't extracted, so makers fall back on copying. Recovery: deepen Part 1 — a richer question and sharper feelings give the maker room to invent on-voice.
- **Ingredients without proportion.** You listed "wit, concreteness, vulnerability" but not the ratio, so every maker dials a different mix and the brand fractures. Recovery: count it in the real samples and state the ratio as a working dial.
- **Surface markers smuggled above the engine.** The vocabulary section reads first and the question gets buried, so agents (which over-weight whatever they read first) imitate phrasing and ignore the logic. Recovery: physically reorder — engine on top, markers explicitly labeled as downstream evidence.
