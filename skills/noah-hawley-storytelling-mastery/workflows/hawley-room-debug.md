# Room Debug

A piece can be technically clean and still feel wrong, and the writer rarely knows where the wrong lives. The showrunner's move is not to rewrite on instinct but to find the single broken joint — the missing theme spine, the ending that stops instead of lands, the music laid over every beat — and fix that, not everything. This is the diagnostic you run on a draft that "doesn't feel right" before you touch a word of it.

## Pre-Flight
Read these files before executing:
1. `skills/noah-hawley-storytelling-mastery/genius.md`
2. `skills/noah-hawley-storytelling-mastery/references/genius-patterns.md`
3. `skills/noah-hawley-storytelling-mastery/references/hidden-knowledge.md` (HK-5: plot hijacks story in act three — the late-piece failure that masquerades as competence)

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: Before executing, run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` — confirm you can answer, for the piece under the lens, the question it's answering, where it ends, and the assumption it took for granted. If the piece itself can't answer 1–3, that absence *is* the diagnosis; skip to Step 3.

## Input Required
- The **piece** itself — the draft, script, essay, post, or sequence that feels off.
- The **felt symptom** in the writer's own words — "it drags," "it's flat," "the ending doesn't land," "it feels generic," "I'm bored by page 80." The vaguer the better; the symptom is rarely where the cause lives.
- The **intended question and ending** if known — what the piece is supposed to be about and where it's meant to arrive. ("Don't know" is a valid answer and a strong signal.)
- The **format and stage** — screenplay / season / essay / post run, and whether this is an early draft or a near-final.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Run the six framework questions against the piece
Hold the draft up to the Decision Framework and answer each question *from the evidence on the page*, not from what the writer intended. A piece feels off when one of these has no answer or a weak one. Score each PASS / WEAK / FAIL, and write the specific evidence — the scene, line, or beat that proves it.

| # | Question | What a FAIL looks like on the page | Verdict + evidence |
|---|----------|-----------------------------------|--------------------|
| 1 | **What is the question?** | You can name the plot but not the theme as a question; the piece states a moral instead of asking something. | ... |
| 2 | **Where does it end?** | The ending stops the story rather than giving it meaning; it could end three beats earlier or later with no loss. | ... |
| 3 | **What's taken for granted?** | The piece accepts the format's default (the rectangle, the genre shape) and reads generic because nothing became a tool. | ... |
| 4 | **Tension → release?** | The build never accumulates, or every beat releases and resets; the engine is flat. | ... |
| 5 | **What's withheld?** | Everything is shown, scored, explained; no space for the audience to do work. | ... |
| 6 | **Does every element serve *this* story?** | Characters, subplots, or beats are present to service a cast/asset/calendar, not the story being told now. | ... |

The fastest read: the piece almost always fails *one* of these hardest, and the others are symptoms downstream of it. Note which FAIL feels load-bearing.

### Step 2: Run the Anti-Pattern scan
Now pass the piece against the eight named failure modes. These are the *fingerprints* of the framework breaks above — a piece that fails "What is the question?" usually shows plot-listing; a piece that fails "What's withheld?" usually shows emotionality. Mark each present / absent with the exact location.

| Anti-Pattern | Tell | Present? Where? |
|--------------|------|-----------------|
| **Plot-listing as story** | "This happens, then this happens"; beats sequenced, nothing underneath. | ... |
| **Emotionality** | The piece tells you what to feel — sad music before the sad beat, the emotion named aloud. | ... |
| **Service creep** | A subplot/character/section exists because something would be "wasted," not because the story needs it. | ... |
| **White-hat-at-noon twist** | A clever genre turn that betrays the theme and how reality actually resolves. | ... |
| **Resolved ambiguity** | The piece collapses the gray zone — tells the audience whether to laugh or flinch, whether he's crazy or powered. | ... |
| **Over-scored / over-full** | Every beat emphasized, every silence filled; the 42-minute show with 38 minutes of music. | ... |
| **Plot hijack in act three** (HK-5) | Late beats exist only to pay off earlier setups; the stakes went from *character* ("is she okay?") to *plot* ("the car is chasing him"). Interest died here first for the writer, then the reader. | ... |
| **Named machinery** | The theme is stated on the page; a character announces what the piece is about. | ... |

Pay special attention to the act-three hijack on near-final drafts: a piece that "drags in the back half" is almost never a pacing problem — it's character stakes that quietly became plot stakes.

### Step 3: Locate the primary failure
A draft with three flagged anti-patterns does not have three problems. It has one root and two symptoms. Trace the flags back to the single broken joint — the one fix that, made, dissolves the others. Use this collapse map; the primary is almost always one of six.

| Primary failure | Diagnostic signature (from Steps 1–2) | Downstream symptoms it explains |
|-----------------|----------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **No theme spine** | Q1 FAIL; plot-listing + named machinery present. | Generic feel, inert scenes, "clever" twists that don't connect. |
| **Weak ending** | Q2 FAIL; the destination was found last, not first. | A middle that wanders, stakes that don't build, an arrival that just stops. |
| **Flat tone** | Q3/Q4 FAIL; single register, no gray zone, format taken for granted. | "It drags," nothing surprises, the piece reads the way its format always reads. |
| **Emotionality** | Q5 FAIL; over-scored, emotion named, nothing withheld. | Feels manipulative or hollow; the audience is told, not moved. |
| **Service creep** | Q6 FAIL; an element present for the cast/asset/calendar. | A subplot the reader skips; a "C-story you don't care about." |
| **Plot hijack in act three** | HK-5 flag; character stakes became plot stakes late. | Back-half drag, a finale that's mechanically satisfying but emotionally dead. |

Name **one primary** and **up to two secondaries**. If you can't choose a primary, you haven't traced deeply enough — the symptom you most want to fix is usually the one furthest downstream.

### Step 4: Prescribe the fix and route to the workflow
The diagnosis is worthless without the next action. For the primary failure, write the *specific* move (not "improve the theme" — the actual question to ask, the actual beat to cut) and route to the workflow that executes it. Secondaries get a one-line note; resist fixing them first, since the primary fix often closes them on its own.

| Primary failure | The fix move | Route to |
|-----------------|--------------|----------|
| No theme spine | Name the theme as a question the piece answers; rebuild every beat from it outward; cut what can't connect. | `/hawley-theme-engine` |
| Weak ending | Lock the destination first; rebuild the middle backward from it so the arrival is the only possible one. | `/hawley-ending-first` |
| Flat tone | Map the tension→release machine; place a gray zone; modulate register part to part. | `/hawley-tonal-arc` |
| Emotionality | Cut the score from the confession; find the one soap-dispenser detonation; leave the rest dry. | `/hawley-tonal-arc` (withhold map) |
| Service creep | Broom the element that serves the asset, not the story; coherence over continuity. | `/hawley-coherence-cut` |
| Plot hijack in act three | Re-anchor the back half to character stakes — "is she okay?" not "what's the solution"; refuse the expected set piece. | `/hawley-imagination` (refuse-the-scene) + `/hawley-ending-first` |

End with the **one sentence** the writer should hold while they fix it — the felt symptom restated as its true cause, so they're aiming at the joint, not the bruise.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | Run Step 1 against the script and Step 3 against the act structure; the act-three hijack (HK-5) is the most common near-final failure — check whether the climax is paying off plot or testing character. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | The "question" is the through-line the piece answers; a flat essay usually FAILs Q1 (no spine) or Q5 (every point spelled out). The ending that "just stops" is the Substack version of the weak-ending failure. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | One unit, so the scan narrows: a post that feels off is almost always emotionality (the lesson stated) or no spine (a list with no question under it). Diagnose, route to `/hawley-theme-engine` or the withhold move, skip the structural maps. |
| Brand / campaign | "Off" usually means service creep (a deliverable that serves the calendar) or flat tone across touchpoints. Run Step 2 hardest on the asset-service line; route to `/hawley-coherence-cut`. |
| Series / run (multi-part) | A run that sags is the season-level act-three hijack — the middle parts service the schedule instead of building. Diagnose the run as one piece, route to `/hawley-tonal-arc`. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering. The diagnostic itself must not commit the failures it hunts — no stating the piece's theme for it, no over-explaining the fix.
- **One primary, not a list.** The diagnosis names a single root cause with traced evidence. A flat list of every flaw means Step 3 wasn't run — collapse the symptoms back to the joint.
- **The symptom is not the diagnosis.** "It drags" is the felt symptom; the diagnosis is the cause underneath it (usually the act-three hijack or a missing spine). If the diagnosis just restates the symptom, go deeper.
- **Every flag has a location.** No anti-pattern is marked present without the exact scene/line/beat it lives on. Unlocated flags are guesses.
- **The prescription routes.** Each primary failure ends with a specific move *and* a named workflow. A diagnosis with no route is incomplete.
- **The fix is a move, not a category.** "Improve the theme" fails; "name the question this answers, then cut the twist in act two that doesn't connect to it" passes.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Fixing all the flags at once.** Three anti-patterns flagged, three rewrites prescribed — and the writer fixes symptoms while the root persists. → Step 3: trace to one primary; the secondaries usually close when the joint is repaired.
- **Trusting the writer's symptom as the location.** "The ending doesn't land" sends you to the ending, but the ending fails because the *spine* was never set, so the destination had nothing to arrive at. → Run Step 1 fully before believing the symptom; the cause is usually upstream of the complaint.
- **Mistaking back-half drag for pacing.** Cutting scenes to "tighten" a sagging third act treats the symptom; the real failure is character stakes that became plot stakes (HK-5). → Re-anchor to "is the character okay?" before cutting a frame.
- **Diagnosing without the page.** Reasoning about what *probably* feels off instead of reading the draft for evidence. → Every verdict in Step 1 cites a specific beat; no on-the-page evidence means no verdict.
