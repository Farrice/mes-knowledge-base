# Moral Mirror

You build the audience up to crave a payoff — the takedown, the tidy win, the violence that reads as progress — and then you hand it to them in a way that turns it back on them. Not catharsis. The kid with cerebral palsy walks into the gunfight Fargo S2 trained you to cheer for, and suddenly you don't want anything bad to happen to anyone. The work runs on a single question the audience never hears: *why did I want this?*

## Input Required
- The payoff / climax / conclusion the piece is building toward (the thing the audience will get).
- The audience and what they've been *trained* to want here — the genre default they'll reach for (violence-as-progress, the takedown, the clean victory, the comeuppance).
- The antagonist / opposing force / "villain" of the piece — whoever sits on the far side of the wanted payoff.
- The theme as a question (what is this really asking — e.g. "can decency win?", "is this win actually a win?").
- Format and length (screenplay scene, essay close, post turn, campaign climax).

## Workflow

### Step 1: Name What They're Trained to Want
Find the desire already loaded in the audience before you write a word. They arrive pre-trained — violence reads as story, the takedown reads as justice, the tidy win reads as earned. Your job is not to invent the craving; it's to locate the one already sitting in them and aim it.

Answer these before building anything:

| Question | Your answer |
|----------|-------------|
| What outcome will the audience be rooting for by the climax? | |
| What genre default trained them to root for it? (violence = progress / villain = deserves it / win = closure) | |
| What does *getting* it appear to confirm about them? (they're on the right side; justice is simple) | |
| What's the unexamined assumption underneath that desire? | |

If the wanted thing is morally clean — nobody pays a real cost, nobody is humanized on the far side — you don't have a mirror yet, you have a payoff. Keep going.

### Step 2: Engineer the Craving
Now *build* the desire so it's live and unembarrassed at the moment of delivery. The mirror only stings if the wanting was real. A lukewarm want produces a lukewarm indictment.

Stack the case so the audience leans in: give the antagonist enough menace, enough earned grievance against them, enough threat to what the audience loves, that the payoff feels not just wanted but *owed*. Tension, tension, tension — hold the release. Withhold the obvious so the imagination does the wanting for you; the audience should be ahead of the page, hungry for the thing you haven't given yet. Do not score the want. Let them arrive at it themselves so they own it.

Build prompt — fill before drafting the delivery:
```
By the moment of confrontation, the audience wants ___________ to happen to ___________.
They want it because ___________ (the grievance / threat you stacked).
The wanting peaks at this exact beat: ___________.
I withheld ___________ so their imagination supplies the desire instead of me stating it.
```

### Step 3: Design the Indicting Delivery
Give them the thing — but at the instant of delivery, humanize the far side so the wanted outcome becomes unbearable. The move is not to deny the payoff (that's a cheat, a bait-and-switch). You deliver it, or deliver right up to its edge, while flipping the antagonist into a person the audience can no longer want destroyed.

The Fargo S2 mechanic: the crime family the audience has rooted against sends "the kid with cerebral palsy who just wants to prove he's a man" — and the trained desire collapses in real time. The confrontation arrives exactly as built; what changes is who's standing in it. Alternatively, refuse the scene the way the Fargo S5 biscuit beat does — the violence the season trained you to expect is withheld and inverted, and the release becomes catharsis instead of a kill, the most complicated emotion through the simplest act.

Design choices:

| Lever | Indicting move |
|-------|----------------|
| Who arrives at the payoff | Swap the abstract villain for a specific, sympathetic human at the exact moment of confrontation. |
| Timing of the humanization | Reveal it *as* the wanted thing lands, not before (early reveal = preachy; late = the want never indicts). |
| The audience's position | They got what they asked for — keep it; let the cost be theirs to carry, not the character's to lecture about. |
| Register | Hold the gray zone — don't tell them whether to feel triumph or shame. The unresolved feeling is the point. |

Leave the confession dry. Do not put a sad chord under the realization. The withheld emphasis is what makes the small released moment detonate.

### Step 4: The Mirror Check
Read the ending back as the audience and ask the only question that matters: do they end *satisfied*, or *complicit and self-questioning*? Satisfied means you delivered catharsis and the mirror failed. The target is the audience quietly asking *why did I want this?* — implicating themselves, not the character.

Run the check:
- [ ] The audience genuinely wanted the payoff (Step 2 landed — the want was real, not nominal).
- [ ] They got it, or got right to its edge (no bait-and-switch; you didn't cheat them out of it).
- [ ] The far side was humanized at the moment of delivery (the want became unbearable in real time).
- [ ] No music, no narration, no line of dialogue told them how to feel about it.
- [ ] The residue is a question turned on the audience, not a moral turned on the character.

If it ends satisfied, the want was too clean or the humanization came too early. Return to Step 1 — usually the desire wasn't actually built, so there was nothing to indict.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Screenplay / TV | The confrontation scene plays out; the sympathetic figure occupies it. Stage the humanization in action, not dialogue. Cut the score under the realization — score elsewhere, or not at all. |
| Long-form essay / Substack | Build the reader's certainty (the obvious villain, the clean verdict), then turn the last movement so the reader recognizes their own appetite for the verdict. The close indicts the reader, not the subject. |
| Short-form (LinkedIn / X) | One tension→release unit. Set up the wanted conclusion in the build, then the final line turns it: "You've been waiting for me to say they deserved it. So was I." Complicity over instruction. Withhold — don't over-explain the turn. |
| Brand / campaign | Engineer the audience's craving for the competitor's defeat or the easy win the category promises, then deliver a climax that implicates the desire itself — positioning the brand as the one that refused the cheap catharsis. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` and the § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix any violation before delivering.
- The audience ends complicit and self-questioning, not satisfied — Moral Complexity rubric ≥8, and you can name the anchor (the want was real, then made unbearable).
- The payoff was actually delivered or refused-and-inverted, not bait-and-switched — no cheat that lets the audience off the hook.
- The humanization lands *at* the moment of confrontation, never early (early = "emotionality," telling them what to feel).
- Nothing on the page tells the audience how to feel about what they wanted — no score under the realization, no stated moral, gray zone held.
- The residue is a question turned on the audience, not a lesson turned on a character.

## Common Pitfalls
- **The want was never built.** You jumped to the indictment with no desire to indict, so the turn lands on nothing. Recovery: go back to Step 2 and stack the grievance until the audience genuinely *owns* the craving — a real want is the whole mechanism.
- **Bait-and-switch instead of mirror.** You denied the payoff to seem clever, and the audience feels tricked, not implicated. Recovery: deliver the thing (or refuse it the way the biscuit scene does — inverted into catharsis), then let the *getting* be the cost.
- **Humanization too early.** You revealed the sympathetic far side before the want peaked, so the audience never wanted the bad thing in the first place — preachy, not complicit. Recovery: hold the humanization to the exact instant of delivery.
- **Emotionality under the turn.** A sad chord or a stated moral tells the audience the realization is coming and what to feel about it, killing the self-implication. Recovery: dry-then-score — strip the emphasis off the mirror beat and let the small released detail carry it.
