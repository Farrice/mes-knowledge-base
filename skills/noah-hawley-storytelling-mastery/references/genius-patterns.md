# Genius Patterns

16 executable patterns from Noah Hawley's storytelling and showrunning practice. Each is grounded in his own words (the *How I Write* interview + published work). Execute the move; don't name it on the page.

---

## Pattern 1: Theme-First Breaking
**Execute**: Before breaking plot, name the theme as a question. When a room (or your own outline) defaults to "what happens next," redirect: "let's talk about how [theme] runs through this." Populate the story from the theme outward, then let plot emerge from characters who embody the theme.

**Why**: "The only common language [a room] speaks is plot" — which uses "the list-making part of your brain, the outline part, not the writing part."

**Success Metric**: Can every major beat answer "how does this relate to the theme?" Cut the "great movie twist" that can't.

---

## Pattern 2: The Question-Image Seed
**Execute**: Start from a question, or an image that contains a question. "Two men meet in an ER, one civilized and one its opposite — who are they?" Let the work *become the answer*. Don't pre-decide the answer; discover it through the writing.

**Success Metric**: Can you state the seed as a question whose answer you genuinely don't know yet? If you already know the answer, it's a lesson, not a story.

---

## Pattern 3: Ending-Determines-Meaning
**Execute**: Name the ending before building the middle. Treat the middle as "a great big blob" to discover; treat the destination as fixed. If you can't control the ending (open-ended commercial pressure), restructure so you can — Hawley made *Fargo* an anthology precisely to own every ending.

**Why**: "The ending of a story is what gives the story meaning. If you don't know how it ends, how do you know what story you're telling?" Counter-example: *Lost* with no end date → "characters literally trapped in a cage."

**Success Metric**: Does the ending feel like the only possible destination — and does it deepen the question rather than resolve it?

---

## Pattern 4: The Comedy-Horror Engine
**Execute**: Treat tone as one machine — "tension, tension, tension, release" — regardless of whether the register is comic or dreadful. Build tension; choose your release (punchline or the thing in the basement). Ride the gray zone between farce and horror where the audience isn't sure which they're feeling.

**Why**: "Comedy and horror are the same… if you rewatch *Key & Peele*, it's just horror, they're playing the funny side." Chigurh's haircut "is hysterical and yet unsettling."

**Success Metric**: Can the same scene make a viewer laugh and flinch? Is the ambiguity held, not resolved?

---

## Pattern 5: The Moral Mirror
**Execute**: Engineer the audience to *want* a payoff (audiences are "trained that violence is story, is progress"), then deliver it so the wanting indicts them. Make the antagonist sympathetic at the moment of confrontation so the desired violence becomes unbearable.

**Why**: *Fargo* S2 — root against the crime family, then "they send the kid with cerebral palsy… I don't want anything bad to happen to him." "Why did I want this?"

**Success Metric**: Does the payoff make the audience complicit and self-questioning, rather than satisfied?

---

## Pattern 6: What Am I Taking for Granted?
**Execute**: Before solving, name the assumption everyone accepts — the medium, the format, the frame, the genre default — and turn it into a tool. The movie theater is a rectangle (so use its size as emotion). A detective story runs forward (so run it backward — *Memento* "changes the whole identity").

**Why**: "My first question is always: what am I taking for granted in approaching the story?"

**Success Metric**: Did you find a treated-as-fixed element and make it a variable that carries meaning?

---

## Pattern 7: Imagination Over Explanation
**Execute**: Underwrite the obvious. Trigger the audience's imagination instead of showing the threat. Linger; leave space; show vulnerability rather than danger. The unseen does more than the seen.

**Why**: Horror gets "the biggest emotion for the least money because it relies on your imagination." *Paranormal Activity* worked by "showing how vulnerable she is eight hours of the day," not by trying to scare.

**Success Metric**: What did you withhold? If you showed/explained/scored everything, you've spent imagination you should have triggered.

---

## Pattern 8: Emotional-Reconstruction Adaptation
**Execute**: Never copy the source. Don't re-watch/re-read it. Reconstruct the *feelings* it produced — a "paint swatch of emotions" — and induce those same feelings through a genuinely different story.

**Why**: "I don't rewatch *Alien*… what feelings did that movie produce in me… how do I produce those same feelings by telling them a different story." "Take the spirit of a thing, not to copy the thing."

**Success Metric**: Could someone who loves the source recognize its *feeling* in your version without recognizing its plot?

---

## Pattern 9: Coherence Over Continuity
**Execute**: Serve the story being told now, never the asset/cast/franchise. When inherited elements (returning characters, beloved features) don't fit, pause them, reposition them, or broom them. Refuse to manufacture a "C-story you don't care about" to service an actor.

**Why**: "The best thing for the story is to tell a great story." Servicing the whole cast is "a prison of your own creation."

**Success Metric**: Is every element present because the story needs it — or because something would be wasted otherwise?

---

## Pattern 10: The Tone-Embodying Container
**Execute**: Make the wrapper — pitch, outline, intro, package — embody the tone of the work itself. An irreverent comedy gets an irreverent outline. Hawley pitched a 70s-anti-hero crime show by riffing about the *segue* until the executives realized "the segue is the pitch" — "the pitch had the same tone."

**Why**: "Whenever you ask someone to interact with your story, you want the experience to feel like the story. It's all writing."

**Success Metric**: Does the container give a stranger the tone of the work before they hear a word of the content?

---

## Pattern 11: Velocity Drafting
**Execute**: On a first draft, sprint. Get down enough that you can return after a long gap and "see exactly what you were doing — the voice, the characters, the structural conceits." For Hawley a novel start is ~100–110 pages in a ~3-month window; 25 pages is "I lost it." Separate the modes: TV is collaborative and immediate; novels are solitary and slow. Don't multitask the two — context-switch between them.

**Why**: "I see it and so I write it." Enough "there there" to survive a 3-year absence from the manuscript.

**Success Metric**: If you walked away for months, would the draft still tell you its voice and direction?

---

## Pattern 12: Most-Advanced-Yet-Acceptable Calibration
**Execute**: Read the moment you're working in. Push past where the audience is, but not so far they lack receptors. Hawley made *Fargo* S4 maximalist (11 episodes, 55+ min) for "peak TV" 2019, then S5 "concentrated… no episode longer than 44 minutes… verb-driven" for 2024 attention. Match the work's density to the era — and to where the era is *heading*.

**Why**: "I loved all the experimentation. Now I just want a great story well told." Novelty must be "most advanced yet acceptable."

**Success Metric**: Is this different enough to register and familiar enough to receive? Is the density right for the moment?

---

## Pattern 13: Playful Power Negotiation
**Execute**: Treat notes and constraints as creative problems, not fights. The reframe: "how do I get what I want while making them think I'm giving them what they want?" Distinguish a real note ("that's confusing" / "I see what you're going for, you didn't get there") from a story-substitution ("I'd do it differently" = "that's just a different story"). Be "easy to work with" *and* get it on screen. Never the junkyard dog.

**Why**: Going to war means "networks say life's too short to work with those people, and you never make anything again." Multilingual fluency (creative + business) is the professional skill.

**Success Metric**: Did you protect what's meaningful while conceding what isn't — and keep the relationship?

---

## Pattern 14: Constraint-as-Innovation
**Execute**: Use limits — budget, location, single set, format — as engines, not excuses. Don't "take the constraint for granted." If you can only afford one location, make the one location urgent; if a mid-season lull threatens, jump the timeline (Fargo S1 ep8 leaps a year; S2 ep7 puts Ed & Peggy on the run, moving the show).

**Why**: "Constraints on a storyteller often lead to real innovation." Horror is inventive *because* it's low-budget and single-location.

**Success Metric**: Did the constraint produce a move you wouldn't have made without it?

---

## Pattern 15: Originality-Through-Adaptation
**Execute**: Bring full original-author intensity to inherited material. Identify the source's "recipe" of ingredients (for the Coens: comedy, violence, philosophy, Judaism) and re-mix the proportions to make something "the same at its heart" but new. The skill is the Catch-22 assignment: "write a new chapter in the voice of the book" — channel the spirit, don't copy the thing.

**Why**: "I built a career adapting IP, but a reputation as an original storyteller… I bring the same originality to a Fargo as if I'd made it up, because in many ways I have."

**Success Metric**: Is your version unmistakably yours *and* unmistakably faithful to the source's spirit?

---

## Pattern 16: Score Where Music Doesn't Go
**Execute**: Find where to *withhold* the emphasis so the released emphasis detonates. Leave the confession dry; score the soap dispenser. When mixing tones, remember "if you score the comedy, you kill the drama; score the drama, you kill the comedy" — so often the answer is where music *doesn't* go. Build complexity of emotion (catharsis: "from sadness comes happiness") rather than single, signaled feelings. Reject "emotionality" — the sad music before the sad moment.

**Why**: In the *Fargo* S3 soap scene, "because there was no music until that point, the impact was so high." "People just use too much music — a 42-minute show with 38 minutes of music."

**Success Metric**: Did you withhold the obvious emphasis and let a small released moment carry the weight?
