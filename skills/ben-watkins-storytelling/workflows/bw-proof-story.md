# Proof Story (Ben Watkins) — Make It Undeniable

Watkins screen-tested for three big shows in one year and booked none of them. His wife told him to make the short film he'd been talking about — something to remember LA by. He wrote it, produced it, paid for it, and finished it only because by the time he saw how hard it was, he was already too far in. It won the American Black Film Festival. Then the executive producer who had said *"I think he's too young"* changed his mind and cast Watkins on *The Young and the Restless* — not because Watkins argued harder, but because he had *seen the performance in the short.* That is the whole move here: he didn't wait to be picked, he *"got to be [his] first investor… show people how much [he] believe[d] in [him]self,"* and let the decision-maker watch the proof work instead of imagining it. The show has a name for the mechanism — *"removing the imagination gap, the space between what someone has to picture and what they can actually see. And the smaller the gap, the harder it is to say no."* You hand this workflow a result — a number, a built thing, a win, a case — and it hands you back a story where the decision-maker *watches the proof work* instead of squinting at your claim. Polish is the tell; a real thing on the table doesn't need it.

## Input Required
- The **result / proof point / asset** — a number, a built artifact, a win, a client case, a before/after, a demo
- The **claim it replaces** — the thing you *would have asked them to imagine* without it
- The **decision-maker** — one person (investor, client, buyer, hiring manager), described by their interior: what they'd have to risk to say yes, and what they fear
- The **one action** the proof-story should make undeniable (fund it, sign, buy, book, greenlight)

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Isolate the seeable proof
Strip the win down to the one thing they can *actually see*, not the story around it yet. Sort what you have — a number ("60K views 7x'd our revenue"), a built thing (the app, the demo, the film), a win (the festival, the sold-out run), a case (the client's before/after). The test is Watkins's résumé line inverted: *"I don't want you to just see my resume. I want you to see me."* A résumé is a claim; the built film winning the festival is a thing on the table. Name the seeable artifact in one line — if it's still abstract ("we're really good at this"), it's a claim, and no story can rescue a claim. Go make the thing first.

| What you have | Is it seeable or a claim? | The one seeable artifact |
|---|---|---|
| (number / built thing / win / case) | SEEABLE / CLAIM | (name it concretely) |

### Step 2 — Build the story around it so it's *felt*, not listed
A proof point listed is a fact; a proof point *storied* is a feeling — and *"people buy based on emotion, not logic."* Fire the 3 C's around the artifact so they don't read the result, they live it: *"Can you get content, character, and circumstances working at the same time?"* — **Character** (whose stakes rode on this — you, the client, the user at 2am), **Content** (the artifact itself, doing its work), **Circumstance** (what was on the line when it landed). The Titanic test applies unchanged: pull the three apart and the number goes flat; fire them together and the win becomes the most-remembered thing in the room. (Deep C-work — building each C out — lives in `/bw-three-c`; here you fire them fast around the artifact you already have.) Don't recite the metric — put a person in front of it, in a moment that mattered, and let the metric *happen to them.*

| C | For this proof | 
|---|---|
| **Character** — whose stakes rode on it | |
| **Content** — the artifact doing its work | |
| **Circumstance** — what was on the line when it landed | |

### Step 3 — Frame it to remove the imagination gap
Now aim the story at the exact space between what they'd *have to picture* and what they can *see*. The show's name for the mechanism: *"It's removing the imagination gap, the space between what someone has to picture and what they can actually see. And the smaller the gap, the harder it is to say no."* Walk the draft and hunt every place you're still asking them to imagine — every "it could," "we would," "picture a world where" — and replace it with the seeable artifact working. Because, as the show puts it, *"nobody wants to take a risk on something they have to imagine... they want to see it working."* The lower the picturing they have to do, the harder the no. Every claim becomes a demonstration.

| Where I'm asking them to imagine | Replace with (the thing they can see working) |
|---|---|
| | |

### Step 4 — Land the greenlight-yourself frame
Close on the frame that reframes the whole proof — you didn't wait for permission, you *manufactured the evidence.* The show's line, its synthesis of Watkins's *"be your first investor"*: *"the most successful people, they do not wait for that person to greenlight them. They greenlight themselves first until they create enough proof so that they become impossible to ignore."* The rule the story should make them feel without you saying it — the show's closing charge: *"don't wait for someone to greenlight you. Be your first yes. Create the proof first because the opportunity follows the proof, not the other way around."* The proof-story's quiet argument is *this already worked once, without you* — and that inverts the risk: they're not betting on a hope, they're joining a thing already in motion. Then subtract, in Watkins's own words: *"there's details you need to keep the story moving forward. And then there are details you withhold because you want people to lean in and ask."* Withhold one part of the win so they ask — the question is a bigger buy than the monologue. (The full withhold sort — momentum vs. seed — is owned by `/bw-subtraction`; pull one seed here.)

**OUTPUT**: the proof-story — the win told as a lived, seeable thing that closes the imagination gap and makes the yes feel like joining, not risking.

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- **They see it working, they don't imagine it.** The proof is a seeable artifact doing its work, not a claim dressed as one. If a decision-maker would still have to *picture* whether you could pull it off, the gap isn't closed — *"nobody wants to take a risk on something they have to imagine."*
- **The proof is felt, not listed.** The 3 C's fire *together* around the artifact — a character with stakes, the result doing its work, a circumstance on the line — so the win lands as a feeling, not a bullet. A metric with no person in front of it is a fact.
- **The greenlight-yourself frame is felt, not stated.** The story makes them feel *this already worked without you* and inverts the risk — you never have to say "greenlight yourself." If the line appears on the page, cut it; the proof should argue it silently.
- **It closes on emotion, the number confirms.** You can name the single emotion the proof-story causes; the metric verifies the decision the feeling already made. If it rests on the stat alone, rebuild — *"I don't want you to just see my resume. I want you to see me."*
- **Subtraction happened.** One part of the win is withheld so they lean in and ask; nothing survives that's neither momentum nor seed. Total clarity on the win removes the reason to reach for you.
- **The machinery is invisible.** No "here's the proof / here's the imagination gap" labels in the delivered story. The reader only feels the risk drain out of the yes.
- **Flow Gate** (references/flow-and-cadence.md § 4): contour varies, every sentence hands off (known-new), loops engineered not asserted, survives read-aloud, compression kept causality — one fail = revise.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Listing the proof instead of storying it.** Dumping the stat ("we did $200K, 10,000 users, 4.9 stars") and hoping the number closes. *Recovery*: run Step 2 — put one person's stakes in front of the metric and let it happen to them; *"people buy based on emotion, not logic."*
- **Proof that's still a claim.** Calling "we're the best at this" a proof point when it's a thing they'd have to imagine. *Recovery*: Step 1 is a gate — if there's no seeable artifact, no story rescues it. Go build or surface the thing they can watch work first (*"be your first investor"*).
- **Leaving imagination gaps open.** The draft still says "picture what this could do" somewhere. *Recovery*: sweep for every "imagine / could / would" and swap in the artifact working — *"the smaller the gap, the harder it is to say no."*
- **Announcing the greenlight frame as a lesson.** Ending on "so remember, greenlight yourself" turns a felt argument into a lecture and breaks the spell. *Recovery*: cut the moral; let the story's *this-already-worked* do the arguing, and withhold one detail so they ask instead.
