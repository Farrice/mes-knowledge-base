# Story Anatomy (Ben Watkins) — The Front Door

*"For me, there are four key pillars to a good story. And the first one is get their attention."* Watkins didn't learn that in a classroom — he learned it broke, working a 9-to-5 with two kids, when he maxed two credit cards to fly 3,000 miles to New York in winter for a "general meeting," a no-stakes hello that 95% of the time "lead to nothing." The casting director was floored — *"Hold on, you flew all the way to New York for a general?"* — and invented a scene on the spot she never meant to run. The commitment was the story; the improbability was the hook; the emotion sealed a deal no résumé would have. This is the front door of the whole system: you hand it a raw message and it hands you back a finished piece where all four pillars are firing and none of them are showing.

Confirm you can name the north star, the *one* emotion, and how you grip them in the first beat. If the output can't answer those three, stop and return to them before producing. No promise you can name = you have material, not a story.

## Input Required
- The **raw message / topic** — the thing you want to land (an idea, an offer, a lesson, a launch, a take)
- The **audience** — one person, described by their interior (their exact fear, the thought they had this morning), not a demographic
- The **surface** — where it runs (Reel, LinkedIn, Substack, a live pitch, a VSL, a brand campaign)
- The **one action** you want them to take after — and, if known, *where they are in their story* when they meet this (first domain, or dark night of the soul)

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Lock the North Star
Before a single line, answer Watkins's four messaging questions and pin them as the filter for every cut downstream: *"what are you trying to say? Why are you saying this? Who is this for? And what are you promising the audience?"* The fourth is the one everyone skips and the one that matters most. Write all four as one-liners.

| Question | Your lock |
|---|---|
| What are you saying? | (the one idea) |
| Why are you saying it? | (the purpose to infect them with) |
| Who is this for? | (the one interior state you're mirroring) |
| What are you promising? | (the payoff Pillar 4 has to earn) |

If you can't name the promise, stop — you don't have a story yet, you have material.

### Step 2 — Grip them in the first beat (Pillar 1)
Open the piece so prediction fails inside the first sentence. Pick the mechanism — or stack all three, which is what the best hooks do: **Surprise** (*"The human brain is engineered to relax based on familiarity and pattern recognition. And as soon as you break the pattern, people perk up."*), **Emotion** (*"if I'm the cause of that emotion, then they're focused on me"*), **Recognition** (describe their exact thought so precisely they feel named — *"a slogan comes out and says just do it and they think it's saying that right to them"*). And in the same breath, open the curiosity gap: a live question the piece will spend its whole length answering. The loop opens at the hook, not the ending. Write the actual opening line — no placeholder, no label.

### Step 3 — Fire the 3 C's together (Pillar 2)
*"Can you get content, character, and circumstances working at the same time?"* Not in sequence — **coincident**. The Titanic test: two people you care about (character) professing their love (content) *while* the ship sinks (circumstance). Pull them apart and each deflates into a fact. Build the body so all three overlap in the same beats. The fastest way to install circumstance right after the hook is one conjunction — the *but* (but-and-therefore is Trey Parker & Matt Stone's engine, not Watkins's): a celebration becomes a live question the instant you add the stakes. The show's Costco example does it in one line — a couple screaming *"WE DID IT"* in the snow, then: "But now we're going to need your help to stay here." Beat-level test only here: does this beat carry a C forward (character, content, or circumstance)? If a beat carries none, it isn't earning its place — but hold the keep/withhold/dead-weight sort for Step 6. (Full cut: `/bw-subtraction`.)

### Step 4 — Teach the unknown inside the familiar (Pillar 3)
Dissolve a real lesson *inside* the story, never bolted beside it as a module. *"Take a world people are familiar with and show them parts of it that they didn't know were there."* The campfire rule: the caveman telling you about the bear that attacked him is teaching you how heavy bears are, how many claws, how to survive — *"in the context of a really cool story. You lean in when that happens."* Name the one thing they'll walk away knowing that they didn't before. Gut-check: do they leave *fuller*, or entertained-then-hungry?

### Step 5 — Plant the question, promise the payoff (Pillar 4)
Close the loop you opened — but as a *question*, not a cliffhanger. *"I actually don't approach it as cliffhangers. I actually approach it as questions."* And the half everyone drops: *"everything I've done up to that point before I ask the question has to also be promising them that the way that gets answered, they're going to really appreciate."* A gap with no promised payoff reads as manipulation and they bail. Make the last line land the promise or hand them the next step.

### Step 6 — Subtraction pass
Now cut. Asked what separates a good storyteller from a master, Watkins's answer: *"It's the ability to know what to leave out."* Sort every remaining detail into two buckets: **momentum** (keep — it moves the story forward) or **seed** (withhold — *"you want people to lean in and ask, make them ask"*). Anything that's neither is dead weight — delete it. Total clarity is the mistake; it removes the reason to lean in. Then strip every label so the machinery goes invisible — the reader should feel pulled and never once see the engine.

**OUTPUT**: the finished piece, machinery invisible — plus one line naming the single emotion it causes.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Short-form social (Reels / LinkedIn / X)** | Hook does all three Pillar-1 moves in the first line and opens the loop in the same breath — at this length you have one sentence to break the pattern. The 3 C's collapse into 2-3 beats; the teach is a single hidden reveal; the question is the last line. Subtract hard — one off-pillar line is a third of the post. Stack `/bw-hook-forge` for the open. |
| **Long-form (Substack / YouTube / essay)** | North Star governs the through-line; the loop opened at the hook runs the entire piece. The 3 C's sustain across sections (character we track, content unfolding, circumstance escalating via but/therefore). Teach carries the substance load — reveal several hidden rooms. Cut the interesting paragraph that proves a *different* promise. Stack `/parallax` or `stanton-produce` to architect the arc. |
| **Live pitch / sales / VSL** | The pillars become the emotional arc of the *sell*: grip cold/tired/skeptical in the first ten seconds, make them *feel* the outcome (not read the résumé), teach the hidden mechanism, leave a question they carry out of the room. KPI is behavioral — *"the first thing they should do leaving that room is call their boss or call their wife or their husband."* Truncate on buy-in; don't overexplain. Stack `copy-engine` for the written VSL blocks inside the arc. |
| **Brand / campaign** | The four pillars aim at brand *association*, not a single post — a recurring world with characters, stakes, and a governing why the audience wants to belong to. North Star = the one thing the brand is *for*; subtract every association that muddies it. The open question becomes the ongoing reason to return. Stack `/bw-north-star` to lock the governing purpose first. |

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- **The machinery is invisible.** No "here's the hook / here's the emotional beat" labels anywhere in the finished piece. If a beat is announced, the spell is broken — rewrite it so the reader only *feels* pulled.
- **It closes on emotion, not logic.** You can name the single emotion the piece causes, and the facts merely confirm the decision the feeling already made. If it rests on features and clarity, it fails — *"I don't want you to just see my resume. I want you to see me."*
- **The loop opened at the hook.** A live question is open by the end of the first beat and everything before it promises the payoff is worth it — not a cliffhanger dangled at the end.
- **The 3 C's are simultaneous.** Character AND content AND circumstance fire in the *same* beats (Titanic), not a character beat then later a stakes beat.
- **Subtraction happened.** At least one seed is withheld to make them lean in; nothing survives that's neither momentum nor seed. Total clarity = a leak.
- **Recognition, not personalization.** The audience feels the line was written *about them specifically* — their exact interior mirrored, no name token pasted on.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Enumerating the pillars in the output.** Stamping "Pillar 1… Pillar 2…" in the finished piece announces the machinery and kills the lean-in. *Recovery*: the pillars are your build sheet, not the reader's — strip every label; the piece should read like one seamless story.
- **Betting on logic to close.** Stacking features, price, and clarity and hoping the decision makes itself. *Recovery*: return to Step 1, name the *one emotion*, and rebuild the body of the piece to cause it — facts inform, emotion closes.
- **Skipping subtraction because it "all feels important."** The founder's instinct is total clarity. *Recovery*: run Step 6 honestly — sort every detail into momentum or seed, delete the rest, and withhold one thing so *"you want people to lean in and ask."*
- **A cliffhanger with no promise.** Withholding a beat and hoping they stick around reads as manipulation. *Recovery*: convert it to a *question* the audience must close in their own head, and make everything prior promise the answer pays off.
