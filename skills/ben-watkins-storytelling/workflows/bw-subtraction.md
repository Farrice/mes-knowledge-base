# The Master's Cut (Ben Watkins)
Broke, Watkins took two credit cards that were nearly maxed, combined them, and bought a one-way bet: a plane ticket to New York for a *general meeting* — no role, no audition, just a casting director putting a face to a name. He didn't hedge it with backups or a fuller plan. He cut the bet down to the one thing that could move his career and flew. That's the same instinct he reaches for when you ask what separates a good storyteller from a master. He doesn't reach for a richer vocabulary or a bigger box of images — he goes the other way. *"It's the ability to know what to leave out."* Then he pulls up Twain to twist the knife: *"I'm sorry this letter is so long. I didn't have time to make it shorter. That is what separates good storytellers from master storytellers."* The amateur adds — more backstory, more proof, more clarity, because it all *feels* important. The master cuts, and then withholds one more thing on purpose. This workflow takes a bloated story, pitch, script, or post and runs it through the two-bucket sort he actually uses in the room, so that what you *don't* say does as much work as what you do.

## Input Required
- The bloated piece, raw — the over-written pitch, script pages, essay, post, VSL, or deck
- The north star if you have it: what are you saying, why, who for, and **what are you promising the audience** (Watkins's four messaging questions — if unknown, Step 1 forces it)
- Where this lands (feed / live pitch / long-form / sales page) — the format sets how aggressive the withholding can be
- Any detail you're already attached to and would fight to keep (it gets the hardest look)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Lock the North Star — the promise governs the cut
Before you delete a word, answer Watkins's four: *"what are you trying to say? Why are you saying this? Who is this for? And what are you promising the audience?"* Write them down. The fourth is the one people skip and the one that decides every cut — *"When I go to tell a story, I have to have a bigger... purpose for telling that story. that helps become what I would call it a north star."* And then, in his words, he makes every downstream decision based on that. You cannot tell momentum from dead weight until you know what the piece is *for*. If the north star isn't locked, run `/bw-north-star` first; otherwise fill the four fields inline. Produce the lock now:

```
SAYING: __________   WHY: __________
FOR (one person): __________   PROMISING: __________
```

Every sort in Step 3 gets filtered through **PROMISING**. A detail that doesn't move the audience toward that promise is not momentum, no matter how true or how dear.

### Step 2: Inventory every detail
Break the piece down to its atoms. List every discrete detail, beat, fact, line, backstory chunk, proof point, and aside as its own row — one detail per line, no bundling. Don't judge yet; strip it bare so nothing hides inside a paragraph. The bloat lives in the details that felt too important to question, so surface all of them:

```
D1: __________
D2: __________
D3: __________
… (keep going until the piece is fully atomized)
```

### Step 3: Sort every detail — momentum / seed / dead weight
Now the two-bucket move that is the whole method — with a third bucket for the trash. Watkins: *"there's details you need to keep the story moving forward. And then there are details you withhold because you want people to lean in and ask, make them ask."* Run each detail through the sort:

| Bucket | What it is | The test | What you do with it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MOMENTUM** | Carries the story toward the promise | Cut it and the story stalls or the promise breaks | **Keep** — tight, no padding |
| **SEED** | A detail so good it makes them lean in and ask | If revealed now it's a fact; if withheld it becomes a question | **Withhold** — rewrite so it provokes the ask, then you get to show off when they do |
| **DEAD WEIGHT** | Neither moves the story nor makes them ask | It's here because it "felt important," not because it earns | **Cut** — no ceremony |

Sort every atom into the table. Be honest about the third column — the resume-reader's failure is that everything looks like momentum:

| Detail | Bucket | If SEED: where will they ask? / If DEAD: why it doesn't earn |
|---|---|---|
| D1 | | |
| D2 | | |
| D3 | | |

The trap to name out loud: **total clarity is a mistake.** The founder's instinct is to give, in Watkins's world, all the clarity — every backstory, every spec, *"I don't want you to just see my resume. I want you to see me."* But over-clarity is a leak; it removes the reason to lean in and ask. A detail you're tempted to over-explain is usually a **seed** you're spending too early. Move it to the SEED column and let it pull.

### Step 4: Rewrite — cut the weight, plant the seeds, keep momentum tight
Produce the tightened version now. Three moves, in order:
1. **Delete** every DEAD WEIGHT row. Don't soften them, don't relocate them — cut. This is the Twain work: slower and harder than adding, which is exactly why the amateur skips it.
2. **Plant** the SEEDs. For each, rewrite the surrounding line so the detail is *implied but unrevealed* — a specific, loaded gap the audience wants closed. The seed is not a vague tease; it's a precise withholding that makes one question unavoidable. When they ask, you *"get to show off"* — and you never rambled to get there.
3. **Tighten** the MOMENTUM. Keep only what carries the promise forward, and compress it. Momentum kept loose is just slower dead weight.

The machinery stays invisible: do **not** label "here's the seed" or "curiosity gap" in the output. The reader should feel pulled to ask and never see the wire. Deliver the cut piece plus a **Seed Map** the operator can defend the cut with.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Short-form social (Reels / LinkedIn / X) | The most brutal cut on the roster — one seed, planted at or near the hook, everything else is momentum or gone. At this length a single dead-weight line is a third of the piece. Withhold the number, the name, the outcome — the 7x'd-revenue result stated *before* the how — so the whole scroll leans toward one unanswered question. Total clarity here = instant scroll-past. |
| Long-form (Substack / YouTube / essay) | Sort section by section, not just line by line — whole passages become dead weight. Plant 2-4 seeds staggered so a new question opens as the last resolves, keeping the reader leaning the entire runtime. The paragraph you love that proves a *different* promise is dead weight no matter how good; cut or bend it. Pair with `stanton-clamp-audit` to verify each kept section still turns the story. |
| Live pitch / sales / VSL | Subtraction here is also a control move — pair with the pitch room. Withhold the detail deliberately so *they* ask, then per Watkins *"if you decide you want to come back and ask more... then we're having a conversation. Now I'm winning even more."* The seed you don't reveal becomes their question, and the asker has already bought. Structure the cut so each withheld seed is a modular section you can be pulled back into — stack with `/bw-pitch-room` for truncate-on-buy-in. |
| Brand / campaign | Cut the "comprehensive" asset that says everything and lands nothing. The campaign holds one promise; every asset either carries momentum toward it or withholds a seed that makes the audience seek the next asset. A brand that over-clarifies gives the audience no reason to come back — plant the gap that pulls them across the funnel. |

## Output Format
```
NORTH STAR
  Saying: ______  Why: ______  For: ______  Promising: ______

THE CUT (tightened version)
  ______________________________________________
  (dead weight deleted · seeds planted as gaps · momentum tight — no labels)

SEED MAP
  | Seed (withheld detail) | Where the audience will ask | What you show off when they do |
  |------------------------|-----------------------------|--------------------------------|
  | ______                 | ______                      | ______                         |
  | ______                 | ______                      | ______                         |

CUT LOG (what got deleted and why it didn't earn)
  • DEAD WEIGHT removed: ______ — didn't move the promise or make them ask
  • DEAD WEIGHT removed: ______ — ______
  • Beloved detail verdict: ______ (kept as momentum / re-planted as seed / cut)

WORD COUNT: before ____ → after ____   (the master didn't have time to make it shorter — you did)
```

**Worked micro-example (the general-meeting bet — how a filled cut reads):**
```
NORTH STAR
  Saying: Bet on yourself before anyone else will  Why: to sell the "start before ready" ethos
  For: the broke operator waiting for permission  Promising: proof that the leap is the move

THE CUT (tightened version)
  I took two credit cards that were nearly maxed, combined them, and bought a plane ticket
  to New York. Not for a role. For a meeting where nothing was promised. My wife thought I'd
  lost it. I got on the plane anyway.

SEED MAP
  | Seed (withheld detail) | Where they ask | What you show off |
  | what the meeting led to | after "nothing promised" | the career it actually unlocked |
```
Note the momentum kept (the bet, the stakes) and the seed withheld (the outcome — never stated, so they lean in and ask). The dead weight cut: the itinerary, the airline, how much the tickets cost.

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` ("Total clarity," "The resume-read," "Over-explaining past the sale") + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (Subtraction Discipline: *"what's left out does as much work as what's in"*). Flag and fix before delivering.
- **What's left out does real work.** At least one deliberate seed is withheld so the audience must lean in and ask. Zero seeds = you clarified, you didn't subtract — total clarity is a mistake, redo Step 3.
- **Every kept detail is momentum toward the promise.** If a line neither carries the story forward nor makes them ask, it's dead weight that survived the cut. Delete it.
- **The seeds are precise, not vague teases.** A seed opens one specific, unavoidable question the audience wants closed — not a generic "keep reading." A murky withhold reads as manipulation, not a promise.
- **The machinery is invisible.** No "here's the hook / here's the seed / curiosity gap" labels in the delivered piece. The reader feels pulled to ask and never sees the wire.
- **The cut is shorter and tighter than the input.** If the word count didn't drop, you added or relocated instead of cutting. The Twain test: it took longer to make it shorter.
- **The beloved detail got a verdict.** Kept only if it's genuine momentum or a stronger seed than what it displaced — never because you love it.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Relocating instead of cutting.** Dead weight gets shuffled to a later paragraph and called "restructuring." Recovery: if a detail failed the momentum *and* seed tests in Step 3, delete it — moving it just hides the bloat downstream.
- **Over-clarifying the seed.** You plant a seed then immediately answer it two lines later because withholding felt risky. Recovery: hold the reveal until they ask; a seed you resolve yourself is a fact you spent early, and *"now I'm winning even more"* only happens when *they* reach for it.
- **Cutting momentum with the weight.** Aggressive trimming strips a load-bearing beat and the promise collapses. Recovery: re-run the momentum test — cut it, and if the story stalls or the promise breaks, it was momentum; restore it and cut something adjacent instead.
- **Sorting without the north star.** Without a locked promise, every detail looks equally important and nothing gets cut. Recovery: return to Step 1 — you can't tell momentum from dead weight until you name what the piece is *for*. Stack with `compression-sprint` or `michael-connelly` momentum work if the piece still drags after the sort, and `prose-check` before delivery.
