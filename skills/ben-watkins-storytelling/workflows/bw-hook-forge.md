# Hook Forge (Ben Watkins)
Watkins once maxed two credit cards to get himself into a general meeting for a show that didn't exist yet — he knows the exact price of failing to grab a room in the first ten seconds. When he broke down what stops a scroll, he didn't reach for a formula, he reached for physiology: *"The human brain is engineered to relax based on familiarity and pattern recognition. And as soon as you break the pattern, people perk up."* The show's own feed proof is a number nobody expects to hear bragged about — the host applying that surprise mechanism to the algorithm: *"When you think viral, you think a million or 2 million or 3 million. For us, it was 60K. And that 7x our revenue."* That's a hook — a contrarian truth that breaks the pattern, lands a jolt, and makes the right person feel it was said straight at them. This workflow forges a bank of them, because the three ways Watkins gets attention — surprise, emotion, and *"the most powerful word in the universe is someone's name"* — are the only three doors in.

## Input Required
- The topic / message / one idea the hooks are pointing at
- The surface (Reel · LinkedIn · X · Substack · YouTube title · live pitch opener · VSL cold open · ad)
- The audience — one person, not a demographic: their exact fear, the thought they had this morning, the dream they're avoiding, where they are in their own story
- The single emotion you need them to feel in the first beat
- Any true, specific, surprising fact you already hold (a number, a reversal, a "for us it was 60K")

## Workflow

### Step 1: Lock the North Star (one line, no drafting past it)
State the four in a single breath: what you're saying · why · who it's for · what you're promising. Every hook below gets filtered through the promise — a hook that grabs attention the payoff can't honor is a lie that collapses on the second beat. Write it: `Saying ___ · Why ___ · For [one person] ___ · Promising ___.` If the promise is fuzzy, stop and sharpen it; you can't withhold toward a payoff you can't name.

### Step 2: Generate SURPRISE hooks (break the pattern) — 4 to 5
Find the thing the audience assumes is true, then say the opposite of it — *truthfully*. The show's own feed break — the host applying Watkins's surprise principle to the algorithm: viral means millions, but *"For us, it was 60K. And that 7x our revenue."* Mine three veins:

| Surprise vein | The move | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrarian truth** | Say the opposite of the consensus, backed by a real fact/number | Point to the exact word where their prediction fails |
| **Pattern-break** | Set the expected frame, then snap it ("Everyone told me to ___. I did the opposite.") | Is it *true*, or clickbait that dies on beat two? |
| **Visual / auditory interrupt** | Open on the image or sound that shouldn't be there (for Reels/video/VSL) | *(host)* *"surprise doesn't just come from what you say. [It] can also come from what people see."* |

Every surprise hook must clear one bar: point to the word or frame that makes them go *wait, what?* If you can't point to it, there isn't one — it's a statement, not a hook.

### Step 3: Generate EMOTION hooks (be the cause) — 3 to 4
Don't report a feeling — *cause* one in the first beat. *"When they feel that emotion, if I'm the cause of that emotion, then they're focused on me."* Use the show's field test to build these: **write the hook whose comment section you can already picture.** Ask *"If this video takes off, what are people going to write in the comments?"* — if you can already hear *"this is so true"* / *"I completely disagree"* / *"this made me cry"*, the emotion is live and the hook is doing its job. Name the one emotion per hook (frustration, awe, fear, vindication, grief). If you can't name it, you're hoping, not causing — cut it.

### Step 4: Generate RECOGNITION hooks (call them by their name) — 3 to 4
Mirror the reader's exact interior so precisely they feel named without a name appearing. This is the one everyone botches. The host's gloss on Watkins's mechanism nails the definition: *"It's not actually about saying their name. It's about describing someone's thoughts or fears or frustrations, their dreams or experiences so accurately that they immediately recognize themselves in what you're saying or you're showing."* Watkins's own proof is *Just Do It* — *"Think about how many people woke up every day with some sort of plan to start some sort of New Year's resolution or change some sort of habit or behavior and then a slogan comes out and says just do it and they think it's saying that right to them. That's how easy it is to call people by their name."* Write the thought they had this morning back to them, then get to the value fast while leaving the loop open. Precision test: the line should feel *wrong* to the person it isn't for. Vague recognition names no one. **For recognition beyond the hook line** — full mirror copy that sustains the interior match across a whole piece — route to `/bw-call-by-name`.

### Step 5: State the curiosity gap each hook opens (the loop lives at the top, not the end)
For every hook in the bank, name the live question it forces open — *"I need to establish a question between myself and the audience that they have to get that answered."* Not a cliffhanger: *"I actually don't approach it as cliffhangers. I actually approach it as questions."* The gap must promise its own payoff — everything in the hook has to signal the answer is worth staying for. An engineered loop = promise + withhold + stake; merely *mentioning* the mechanic ("the toolkit that kills the three reasons") withholds nothing and yields confusion, not curiosity (CS-02). A hook with no open question isn't a hook; it's a headline that already resolved.

### Step 6: Star the ones that do ALL THREE
Mark the hooks that surprise, land an emotion, *and* make the reader feel named — the savant tier. The show's own finding: *"the best performing hooks almost always did all of the three things that Ben mentioned. They surprised people. They made people feel something. And by the end of the hook, people knew that they were talking to them."* Lead your surface with a starred hook; hold the single-mechanism ones as backups and A/B variants. Sharpen mechanics with `luke-iha-vicious-hooks` / `vicious-hook`, novelty-feel with `kallaway-illusion`, and scroll-stop retention with `seena-rez` — but the anatomy stays Watkins's.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Short-form social (Reels / LinkedIn / X)** | Hook is line one and it lives or dies alone — favor the starred all-three hooks. Recognition carries most on LinkedIn (mirror the interior state); surprise + visual interrupt carry on Reels (the image that shouldn't be there in frame one). Under char caps, cuts remove details, never the connective tissue between surviving sentences — re-read aloud after each cut pass. Stack with `hook-forge` / `diandra-headline-engineer` for the scroll-stop headline layer. |
| **Long-form (Substack / YouTube / essay)** | The hook is the title *and* the first paragraph, and the open loop has to survive a longer runtime — promise a payoff big enough to hold 8+ minutes. Surprise in the title, recognition in the opening line, the question by the end of paragraph one. |
| **Live pitch / sales / VSL** | The hook is the cold open before you introduce yourself — a contrarian truth or a mirrored fear that reorganizes a tired, skeptical room. *"If I flip the script, I just became the best part of your day."* Open the loop, then truncate on buy-in; don't over-explain the hook past the moment it lands. |
| **Brand / campaign** | The hook is the tagline / campaign line doing *Just Do It* work — recognition at scale, one line that makes every person in the segment feel personally addressed. It must survive repetition, so the surprise is structural (a reframe of the category), not a one-time shock. |

## Quality Gate
> 🛡️ **Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + `§ Expert-Specific Quality Rubric` (Attention Grip, Recognition, The Open Loop). Flag and fix before delivering.
- **The machinery is invisible.** No hook is labeled "surprise hook" or "here's the emotional beat" in what ships — the reader feels pulled, never sees the engineering. Naming it on the page breaks the spell.
- **Every hook opens a real question**, and the hook itself promises the payoff is worth it — no cliffhanger that withholds without earning trust.
- **Recognition mirrors an interior state**, not a name token or a demographic label — the line would feel *wrong* to the person it isn't for.
- **Surprise is true.** Every pattern-break survives beat two; no clickbait that collapses. If there's a number, it's real.
- **At least 3 hooks do all three** and are starred; the bank has 10–14 total, each tagged to its mechanism(s) and its open question.
- **Every hook serves the north star's promise** — a hook that grabs attention the payoff can't honor is cut, however loud.
- **Loops are engineered, not asserted.** Every open question = promise + withhold + stake, primed with something real first — a mechanic-mention with nothing withheld fails (CS-02).
- **The bank has contour.** Read the hooks as a column: no run of 3+ same-length lines; short, medium, and long all present.
- **Survives read-aloud.** Read the bank cold, out loud — any stumble or backtrack is a rewrite signal, not a performance error.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Statement dressed as a hook.** A true, interesting line that opens no question. Recovery: run Step 5 on it — if you can't name the live question it forces, it already resolved; add the gap or cut it.
- **Literal-name "recognition."** Pasting `[first name]` or a segment label and calling it calling-them-by-name. Recovery: throw out the token, write the exact thought they had this morning — *Just Do It* uses zero names and every resolution-maker feels addressed.
- **Loud but false surprise.** A shock bolted on to stop the scroll that the body can't back up. Recovery: keep only pattern-breaks that are *true* on beat two; a lie that collapses costs you the whole piece.
- **Reporting emotion instead of causing it.** "This story made me emotional" describes a feeling that happened elsewhere. Recovery: use the comment-section test — if you can't already hear *"this is so true / I completely disagree / this made me cry,"* rebuild until you can.
