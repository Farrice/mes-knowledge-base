# The Character Canvas (Ben Watkins)
When Watkins is deciding whether to hand someone a crucial role, he doesn't lead with the résumé — he asks a question built to find what makes them tick: *"who's your nemesis? And I asked that question because if you have a nemesis, that's a chip on your shoulder. People sometimes are motivated by that."* He's seen the answer come back as a kid from the eighth grade, a rival at a high-school reunion — *"they're just like, I dream of them reading that deadline announcement when I got my first show."* That's not small talk. That's a screenwriter finding a character's engine. Watkins knows the engine because he ran on his own: he maxed two credit cards to fly to New York for a general meeting with no role attached — a foreclosed house behind him — betting on the delusional-naive move before there was any evidence it would pay. And it's the same move he runs on a customer: *"getting to know the consumer the way that you get to know a character, you are going to increase your odds of success there."* This workflow builds that character — the founder for a personal brand, or the customer you're selling to — the way a writer builds one before a single scene is shot: pages of backstory, a want and a deeper need, a nemesis, the flaw that makes them human, and the exact moment of their story they're living when they find you.

## Input Required
- **Who you're profiling**: the *founder* (for personal-brand content — profile the founder as the protagonist) OR the *customer* (for selling — profile the buyer as the hero of their own story). Pick one; a canvas serves one person.
- Everything you already know about them — raw. Interviews, DMs, reviews, sales-call notes, your own memory. Fragments are fine.
- The one moment / decision you eventually need to speak to (the buy, the opt-in, the reply, the belief-shift). We're building toward the line that lands *there*.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Write the backstory pages
A screenwriter knows the character's childhood, their parents, the thing that broke them, long before that ever shows up on screen — most of it never does. Do the same here. Write the upbringing, the formative relationships, the wound, the pattern they've been running since. Watkins puts it plainly: *"becoming a master story is almost like becoming a master in people."* You're not writing a demographic; you're writing a person with a history. Draft freely, then cut — most of this stays off the page and works underneath (subtraction: `genius.md § The Master's Secret Is Subtraction`).

| Backstory field | What shaped them (write it as narrative, not bullets) |
|---|---|
| Where they come from (upbringing, class, the early no) | |
| Formative relationships (who's in every turning point of their story) | |
| The wound / the pattern it set | |
| What they've been proving ever since | |

### Step 2: Name the WANT vs. the deeper NEED
The **want** is what they'd say out loud — the surface goal, the thing they think they're buying. The **need** is the thing underneath they can't name yet, the change they actually require. A great story gives them the want and quietly serves the need. Get both, because the want gets their attention and the need is where the emotion lives — and *"people buy based on emotion, not logic."*

| | Statement | How you'd know it's true (the evidence in their words/behavior) |
|---|---|---|
| **WANT** (stated, surface) | | |
| **NEED** (unstated, deeper) | | |

If the want and need are identical, dig again — you've only got the surface.

### Step 3: Name the NEMESIS — the chip on the shoulder
Ask Watkins's question of the person you're profiling: **who's their nemesis?** Not a competitor brand — the *person or force* they're quietly out to prove wrong. *"I want this person to hear that I just sold a show because they told me I'd never get there. That's a nemesis."* It's unfinished business, and it runs on the subconscious: *"it's actually operating on the subconscious and it's something you should think about because it's unfinished business."* The nemesis is the highest-voltage thing on this canvas — it's the emotion you can speak to that they haven't admitted they carry.

- **The nemesis (person / force / old story they're out to disprove):** ______
- **The scene they fantasize about (the vindication moment):** ______ *(e.g. the reunion, the deadline announcement, the "now they see what I've become")*
- **The line only someone who knows this nemesis could write to them:** ______

### Step 4: Flaws and fears — the internal conflict + the external obstacle
Perfect characters are unrelatable and unbelievable. The flaw and the fear are what make the audience recognize themselves. Separate the two layers a story always runs on: the **internal conflict** (the flaw, the self-doubt, the pattern from Step 1) and the **external obstacle** (the real thing in the way — money, time, gatekeepers, the market). Watkins lived both — *"I'll never make enough to pull us out of this hole"* was the internal, the foreclosure was the external. Name them separately; they need different lines.

| Layer | The specific flaw / fear / obstacle | The interior thought, in their own voice |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal conflict** (flaw, fear, self-doubt) | | |
| **External obstacle** (the real thing in the way) | | |

The interior thought is the recognition asset — mirror it precisely enough and they feel *"like you're talking to them"* without their name ever being said (that's "call them by their name," `genius.md § Pillar 1`).

### Step 5: The CHANGE arc — who they are by the end
A character is defined by how they're different at the end. Write the arc: who they are when they find you → the turn → who they've become. This is the transformation your work promises. Keep it a real change, not a feature acquired — from *"I can't rely on anybody"* to someone who let the right people carry them; from invisible to seen. State it as a before → after they'd feel, not a benefit list.

- **BEFORE (who they are when they find you):** ______
- **THE TURN (what changes them):** ______
- **AFTER (who they've become):** ______

### Step 6: Locate them in their own story — which beat, right now
The last move is the sharpest: *where are they in their story when they find you?* Buying their first domain, wide-eyed at the start — or, as the show's applied breakdown puts it, *"in their dark of the soul right now, who just needs a video to remind them that it's not over"*? Same person, different beat, completely different line. Pin the single moment they're living *at the point of contact* — because that, not the demographic, tells you exactly what to say. (Mapping the arc to a formal beat sheet is Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat* overlay — see the business-translation-map; the *"where are they in their story"* instinct is Watkins's own.)

- **The beat they're on right now:** ______ *(opening image / catalyst / all-is-lost / dark night / etc.)*
- **What they can and can't hear at this beat:** ______
- **The one thing to say to a person standing exactly here:** ______

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Short-form social (Reels/LinkedIn/X)** | Speak to *one beat*, not the whole arc. Pick the single moment (Step 6) and write the interior thought (Step 4) as the hook — the customer at "all-is-lost" hears a different first line than the customer at "opening image." One post = one person at one moment. Don't average the audience; the averaged line lands on no one. |
| **Long-form (Substack/YouTube/essay)** | You have room to run the full arc (Step 5). Open on the BEFORE (the flaw/fear they'll recognize), move through the turn, land on the AFTER they want — the reader should feel their own change, having never been told "this is your arc." The nemesis from Step 3 is the emotional engine under the whole piece. |
| **Live pitch / sales / VSL** | You're pitching *their* transformation, so the customer is the hero and you're the bridge, never the lead. Drop the want early (their attention), serve the need at the emotional turn, and speak the nemesis-vindication scene out loud — that's the beat they call their boss or their wife about. Know their beat before you walk in so you can flip a cold room (`/bw-pitch-room`). |
| **Brand / campaign** | The canvas becomes the audience bible: every asset speaks to one customer at one location on the arc, never to "everyone." Recurring content addresses the recurring character. Map want/need/nemesis once, then produce different assets for different beats — first-timer vs. dark-night — instead of one flat message for all of them. |

**Worked example** (Watkins himself as the founder-character — fill yours the same way, then withhold most of it):
```
CHARACTER CANVAS: Ben Watkins (☑ Founder)
Moment I'm building toward: the New York general meeting — betting everything on a room with no role in it

BACKSTORY PAGES (mostly withheld):
  • Origin / early no: grew up poor — "the way I grew up so poor I never wanted to be in that situation"
  • Formative relationships: his wife, the one who kept the family afloat and said "You give up. I give up."
  • Wound & pattern: the house went into foreclosure; he blamed other people, poured sauce on feeling sorry for himself
  • Been proving ever since: that betting on yourself first is the only bet — your first investor has to be you

WANT (stated):   sell the script, land the show, get paid
NEED (unstated): stop waiting to be picked — become the showrunner of his own story

NEMESIS: the person who told him he'd never get there
  • Vindication scene: "I want this person to hear that I just sold a show because they told me I'd never get there."
  • Line only someone who knows this nemesis could write: you already know whose face you're picturing

FLAW / FEAR:
  • Internal conflict: the give-up spiral → interior thought: "I'll never make enough to pull us out of this hole."
  • External obstacle: two maxed credit cards, a foreclosed house, a meeting with no role attached

CHANGE ARC:
  BEFORE → a good writer waiting to be chosen, blaming the chips for how they fell
  TURN   → "You give up. I give up." — the hustle bone kicks back in
  AFTER  → the showrunner of his own damn story; the pen was always his

BEAT AT POINT OF CONTACT: dark of the soul — the day before the turn, sorry for himself, about to quit
  • Can hear / can't hear: can't hear tactics yet; can only hear "it's not over"
  • The one line for a person standing here: a reminder that the next scene is still his to write
```

## Quality Gate
> 🛡️ **Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (row: *Recognition (Call By Name)* and *Purpose / North Star*). Flag and fix before delivering.
- **It's a person, not a demographic.** If the canvas would fit ten thousand people interchangeably, you profiled a segment, not a character. Watkins studies a customer *"the way that you get to know a character"* — go back and give them a specific wound and a specific nemesis.
- **Want ≠ Need.** The stated want and the deeper need are genuinely different. If they're the same sentence, you stopped at the surface; dig Step 2 again.
- **The nemesis has a face and a scene.** "Self-doubt" is not a nemesis. A person or old story they're out to prove wrong, with a vindication moment they can picture — that's the chip on the shoulder. Vague nemesis = redo Step 3.
- **The interior thought is in their voice, precise enough to be recognition.** They should feel the line was written *about them specifically*, no name used. Generic pain ("they're stressed and busy") fails — mirror the exact thought.
- **They're pinned to ONE beat, not the whole journey.** If the canvas doesn't say what moment of their arc they're in at point of contact, you can't write the line that only lands there. Locate them (Step 6).
- **Machinery invisible downstream.** Nothing produced *from* this canvas ever labels "here's their want, here's their nemesis." The canvas is the private backstory; the output just *feels* like it was written for one person.
- **Flow Gate** (references/flow-and-cadence.md § 4, applies to any prose the output carries): contour varies, sentences hand off (known-new), loops engineered not asserted, survives read-aloud — one fail = revise.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Profiling a segment and calling it a character.** Age, income, job title, "busy professional" — that's a demographic; it gives you a customer, not a line. Recovery: run Steps 1–3 on a single real person you can name (a past buyer, one DM), then generalize *outward* from the specific — never start from the average. Stack `content-audience-profile` for the demographic layer, then re-characterize.
- **Skipping the nemesis because it feels harsh.** It's the highest-voltage field on the canvas and the easiest to leave blank. Recovery: ask Watkins's question literally — *"who's your nemesis?"* — and follow it to the vindication scene; the emotion they haven't admitted is exactly the one your work can speak to. For the deeper identity-resistance version, stack `mcraney-deep-canvass`.
- **Locking the want and forgetting the need.** Writing to the surface goal (the want) alone produces a feature-list message that never closes, because *"logic is not going to make this decision."* Recovery: name the unstated need in Step 2 and route the emotional turn through it, giving them the want at the surface.
- **One canvas, all beats at once.** Trying to speak to the first-timer and the dark-night customer in the same piece, so it lands on neither. Recovery: pin one beat (Step 6), write for that person, and produce separate assets for the other beats — for the underlying story spine of who this character is, stack `stanton-spine`.
