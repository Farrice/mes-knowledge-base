# The Pitch Room (Ben Watkins)
Watkins walks into every pitch assuming the room is against him: *"I go in thinking you're either in a bad mood or you're running out of time or you're bored or you've heard five pitches. I'm the sixth. You had a heavy lunch."* Most people fear that room. He wants it — *"if I flip the script, I just became the best part of your day."* His whole method collapses selling and storytelling into one act, because *"People buy based on emotion, not logic,"* and his rule for whether the pitch worked is not whether they said yes: *"the first thing they should do leaving that room is call their boss or call their wife or their husband or grab a co-worker and be talking about the pitch they just heard."* This workflow builds that pitch.

## Input Required
- **The idea/offer** you're pitching — one line of what it actually is
- **Who is in the room** — names, roles, who holds the yes, and anything you know about them personally (school, hometown, past projects, mutual connections)
- **The stakes for them** — what greenlighting this costs them / wins them internally
- **Your genuine proof-of-belief** — the thing you've done that shows how much *you* believe (the flew-to-New-York-for-a-general moment)
- **Time you have** — 5 min elevator, 20 min room, 45 min deck-and-discussion

## Workflow

### Step 1: Lock the North Star and name the purpose to infect
Before a single line of the pitch, answer the four messaging questions and compress them to one catchable *why* — *"I need to do it in a way where you become emotionally attached to me as the storyteller and to what I like to say infect people with the purpose of the project."* The purpose is not the feature ("an AI scheduling app") — it's the thing they can catch like a virus and carry out of the room ("nobody should lose a Saturday to a calendar"). Write it as one sentence they could repeat to their boss verbatim. That sentence *is* the pitch's spine; everything else serves it.

```
PURPOSE TO INFECT (one repeatable sentence): __________
THE ONE EMOTION they must feel: __________
```

### Step 2: Read the room — assume the worst state, design the flip
Pre-load the hostile room and build the pitch to *transform* it, because the transformation is itself the memorable event. For each person, assume a bad state and design the specific move that flips it.

| Assumed state (assume ALL of these) | The flip move (what YOU do to reverse it) |
|---|---|
| Bad mood / had a fight before this | Open on something that makes *them* feel, fast — be the cause of the first emotion in the room |
| Running out of time | Front-load the surprise; structure so the first 90 seconds already earns the meeting |
| Bored / sixth pitch of the day | Break their pattern — they expect a deck and a market-size slide; give them a person and a stake |
| Heavy lunch / low energy | Lead with proof-of-belief (Step 4), not exposition — energy is contagious, exposition is sedative |

*"A lot of people, oh no, oh no, she's in a bad mood... But if I flip the script, I just became the best part of your day."* Name the actual flip you'll run per person — vague "I'll bring energy" fails.

### Step 3: Mine one genuine overlap — plant it, don't open on it
Research each decision-maker for a real point of connection — same school, same hometown, a shared collaborator, a project they made that shaped you. You are looking for **one** genuine overlap to drop at the *right moment* to trigger nostalgia and collapse the distance — never as the opening handshake (that reads as flattery and burns the asset). Hold it for a natural seam: after the first buy-in, or when the conversation turns to *why you*. If there's no genuine overlap, do not manufacture one — a fake connection is worse than none.

```
THE OVERLAP: __________
THE DROP MOMENT (the exact seam, not the handshake): __________
```

### Step 4: Open on surprise or proof-of-belief
The first beat has to break their pattern (Pillar 1) or demonstrate belief so intense the room reorganizes around it. Two live options — pick the one that fits your material:
- **Surprise / contrarian truth** — say the opposite of what they assume ("You think this market wants more features. It wants fewer.")
- **Proof-of-belief** — the evidence that you've already bet everything on this, the way Watkins *flew 3,000 miles in winter for a "general meeting" 95% of which lead to nothing* — and the casting director was so floored by the commitment she invented a scene on the spot. The most persuasive fact in the room is often *how much you believe*. Show the bet, don't describe it.

Do NOT open on a logic slide (market size, price, margins). Facts sit in the room; they never open it.

### Step 5: Structure modular sections — tag each TRUNCATABLE with its buy-in signal
Build the body as **modular sections you can cut mid-flight**, each carrying the specific buy-in signal that triggers its cut. *"I'm going to truncate that section. If you decide you want to come back and ask more about it, then we're having a conversation. Now I'm winning even more."* The trigger is emotional buy-in, not a stopwatch: *"As soon as you know you have somebody emotionally, that's when you move on in the pitch... We're not going to overexplain."* Over-explaining past the sale is the amateur's tell — the moment they lean forward, nod, finish your sentence, or ask a question, you've won that point; stop selling it.

| Section | The point it lands | Buy-in signal (what tells you to cut) | Truncate-to (the next section) |
|---|---|---|---|
| The general-meeting bet | I fly 3,000 miles in winter for a meeting with no role in it | She stops — "you flew all the way to New York for a *general*?" | The result the bet bought |
| The result the bet bought | That commitment made her invent a scene on the spot; I left with an agent | They lean in / ask "then what happened?" | The ask (what I'm here to build now) |

Fill this table for your pitch — the row above is Watkins's own general-meeting story worked as an example. Each section is a leave-them-wanting unit: land the feeling, sense the buy-in, cut before you talk past it. Cuts remove details, never the connective tissue between surviving sentences — re-read aloud after each cut pass.

### Step 6: Prep riffs to manufacture ownership
Decide in advance how you'll handle ideas the room offers — because that's your ownership machine. *"When they offer an idea, you riff on that. You don't even have to incorporate that idea. Just riffing on that makes them feel like they're part of the process... Now, they're going to fight tooth and nail to make sure that thing works."* Pre-write two or three riff-launch lines ("Ooh — and if we took that further...") so that when an exec floats anything, you extend it out loud, honor it, and let them feel co-authorship. Riff, don't adopt (dilution) or dismiss (alienation). The felt ownership converts an evaluator into an ally who defends your project internally when you're not in the room.

### Step 7: Close on a question
End on a live **question**, not a summary and not a hard ask — a question they have to close in their own head, that hands them the pen. *"I need to establish a question between myself and the audience that they have to get that answered,"* and everything before it must promise the answer is worth it. The close is engineered so that the *first thing they do leaving the room is call someone* — because you left a live loop open and a purpose they can't stop carrying. Write the exact closing question and confirm the pitch has promised its payoff.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| Short-form social (Reels/LinkedIn/X) | The "room" is the scroll and the assumed-worst state is *thumb already moving*. Compress the whole arc to hook + one truncatable section + closing question. The overlap becomes "call them by their name" — mirror the viewer's exact interior so they feel the post was made for them. No riff step (no live room) — but as an applied translation of the riff/IKEA-effect move, the comment section becomes where ownership gets manufactured: open a question that begs a reply so viewers co-author in the replies. |
| Long-form (Substack / YouTube / essay) | Modular sections become chapters, each with an internal buy-in beat that *promises the next*. Read-the-room becomes reader-state: assume distracted, half-committed, one tab away. The overlap is a mid-piece moment of recognition dropped after you've earned attention, never the intro. Close each section on a question so they scroll to the answer. |
| Live pitch / sales / VSL | The native format — run all seven steps intact. For VSL, "read the room" is pre-modeled from the avatar's objection state; truncate-on-buy-in becomes tight blocks that answer-and-advance before the viewer's finger drifts. Stack `luke-iha-vsl-leads` for the lead structure and `copy-engine` for the converting sentences *inside* Watkins's emotional arc. |
| Brand / campaign | The "room" is the market and the "call their boss" KPI becomes *they tell a friend unprompted*. The purpose-to-infect is the brand's catchable why (a world people want to belong to, not a product). Overlap scales to shared identity/values; riffing scales to co-creation (UGC, features, community ideas the audience sees themselves in). Every asset is a truncatable section that lands a feeling and opens a loop to the next. |

```
PITCH: __________          ROOM: [names / roles / who holds the yes]
TIME: ______

PURPOSE TO INFECT (one repeatable sentence): __________
THE ONE EMOTION: __________

THE PITCH SCRIPT
  OPEN (surprise or proof-of-belief): __________
  SECTION 1 → [buy-in signal] → truncate-to: __________
  SECTION 2 → [buy-in signal] → truncate-to: __________
  SECTION 3 → [buy-in signal] → truncate-to: __________
  CLOSING QUESTION (hands them the pen): __________

ROOM-READING PLAYBOOK
  Assumed state → flip move:
    • [state] → [flip]
    • [state] → [flip]
  Overlap → drop moment:
    • [genuine overlap] → [the seam, not the handshake]
  Section → buy-in signal → truncate-to:
    • [section] → [signal] → [next]
  Riff-launch lines (prepped): "__________" / "__________"

KPI: they leave and immediately call their boss/spouse/coworker about this. [modeled: what do they say?]
```

**Worked micro-example** (Watkins's general-meeting bet, filled — model your three lines on this, don't copy it):
```
PURPOSE TO INFECT:  The people who win aren't the most qualified — they're the ones who show up
                    before they're allowed to.
OPEN (proof-of-belief):  I maxed out two credit cards to fly 3,000 miles, in winter, with no coat,
                    for a meeting that has no role in it. She asked why I'd come that far for a
                    "general." I said, "Because general means you're generally interested."
CLOSING QUESTION:   So the question isn't whether the idea is good — it's who in this room is willing
                    to show up before it's safe to. Are we that room?
```

## Quality Gate
> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric. Flag and fix before delivering.
- **Purpose, not features.** The pitch is built around one catchable *why* they can repeat, not a feature/logic list. If the open is a market-size or price slide, it fails — facts sit in the room, emotion opens it.
- **The room was read.** Every person has an assumed-worst state AND a specific named flip move. "I'll bring energy" is not a flip. The transformation of their state is designed, not hoped.
- **Truncation is built in.** Every body section is tagged truncatable with its exact buy-in signal. If any section has no cut-point, it will talk past the sale — add one.
- **The overlap is genuine and planted, not opened on.** No manufactured connection; no flattery handshake. If there's no real overlap, there's none — don't fake it.
- **It closes on a question, not a summary.** The last beat hands them the pen and opens a loop, and everything before it promised the payoff. Predict what they say when they call someone — if you can't, the loop isn't live.
- **The machinery is invisible.** Nowhere in the delivered script does it say "here's the surprise hook" or "buy-in signal." The room only feels pulled. Labels showing = rewrite.
- **The script survives read-aloud.** A pitch is spoken prose — read it out loud, cold, at room pace; every stumble, stall, or backtrack is a rewrite signal (`flow-and-cadence.md § The Flow Gate`). Full oral craft: `/bw-spoken-story`.
- **Every sentence hands off.** Openings pick up what the room already holds; no subject swap without a bridge; every pronoun binds instantly.
- **Compression kept causality.** Truncation passes preserved the but/so/which-is-why chain — cut adjectives and credentials, never connectors.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Opening on the logic slide.** Leading with market size, price, or margins because they feel "professional." Recovery: move every fact to *after* the emotional buy-in; open on surprise or proof-of-belief (Step 4). Facts confirm a decision emotion already made.
- **Talking past the sale.** Finishing a section after they've already leaned in, because you rehearsed the whole thing. Recovery: re-run Step 5 — tag the buy-in signal for each section and drill cutting on it. The question they ask back is a bigger win than your monologue.
- **Manufacturing a fake overlap or dropping it at the handshake.** Forcing "oh we both love X" as an icebreaker. Recovery: if the overlap isn't genuine, cut it entirely; if it is, hold it for the seam in Step 3 where nostalgia does real work.
- **Adopting or dismissing the room's ideas instead of riffing.** Either bolting on their bad idea (dilution) or shutting it down (alienation). Recovery: use the prepped riff-launch lines from Step 6 — extend the idea out loud for the ownership, then quietly build what you were building. Co-authorship is the asset, not the idea. Deep proposal or council work: stack `draft-proposal` and `convene`; sharpen the cut with `/bw-subtraction`.
