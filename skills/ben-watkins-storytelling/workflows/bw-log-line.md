# The Log Line (Ben Watkins)
Early on, a New York casting director told Watkins to come in for a general meeting — no role, no audition, just a face-to-face. He and his wife took two credit cards that were almost maxed, combined them, and bought the ticket. That's the pressure the log line is built for: one shot, one line, and you'd better know the single thing that makes you worth the bet. Watkins says the master's secret isn't painting the picture more — *"it's the ability to know what to leave out,"* and his reason for cutting is blunt: *"the only way to get money is to tell the right story."* The log line is the tool that forces that subtraction into a single sentence you can say in an elevator. The craft here is Blake Snyder's — the log line and the *Save the Cat* beat sheet are his, applied to business in `references/business-translation-map.md` — but the discipline underneath it is pure Watkins: if you can't say it in one clean line, you don't know the one thing you want them to remember. In a film that sentence is *who's the main character, what do they want, what's standing in their way.* In business, one word changes and the whole thing inverts: the main character isn't you. It's your customer, and *you're* the thing that gets them there.

## Input Required
- The brand / offer / pitch you're compressing, raw — one paragraph of what it is and does
- Who the customer actually is (best guess is fine — we'll cast them as the hero)
- The problem they're living in when they find you, and what they want on the other side
- What *you* concretely do that bridges the two (the mechanism, not the mission statement)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Cast the customer as the main character
The business inversion is the load-bearing move, and most people get it exactly backwards — they make the brand the hero of its own log line. Snyder's film log line asks *who's the main character, what do they want, what's standing in their way;* the show's host does the business flip out loud: *"the main character is obviously your customer. It's about them, what they want, what they're up against, and you are the thing that helps them get there."* So fill the three slots for **the customer**, never for you. This is where Watkins's rule to know the audience like a character pays off — you're writing their want and their nemesis, not your feature list (`/bw-audience-as-character` owns that build).

| Slot | Ask | Fill for the CUSTOMER |
|---|---|---|
| **Hero** | Who's the one person? (one human, one moment in their arc) | |
| **Want** | What do they want on the other side? | |
| **Obstacle** | What's standing in their way right now? (the pain) | |
| **You = the bridge** | What do you do that gets them across? (never the hero) | |

If "you" landed in the Hero slot, delete it and start over. The customer is the protagonist; you're the guide. Watkins's own version of this: *"I don't want you to just see my resume. I want you to see me"* — the log line makes them see *themselves*, then you as the way through.

### Step 2: Draft the one-sentence log line — lead with the problem
Now compress all four slots into one clean sentence, and **open on the obstacle, not the want.** The host's rule for what makes a log line land: *"what the best log lines land almost always comes down to the problem, the stakes, because the pain point is the fastest way to spark that emotion"* — and Watkins's engine says the same thing from the other side: *"People buy based on emotion, not logic."* The pain is the fastest wire to the feeling. Lead with it.

Shape (don't fill blindly — bend it to sound spoken):
> *"For [the hero] who [is stuck in the problem/pain], [your offer] is the [thing] that gets them to [the want] — without [the obstacle they feared]."*

Write it once, plain and a little rough. Polish is the tell. If it needs an *"and"* to finish, you're carrying two log lines — cut to the one that matters.

### Step 3: Produce 3–5 variations
One draft is never the log line; it's the first ugly candidate. Generate **3–5 variations**, each pulling a different lever so you can hear which one *lands* out loud:

| # | Lever it leads with | Variation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The pain / stakes (the default — fastest to emotion) | |
| 2 | The impossible-before / after-state | |
| 3 | The enemy or the false path they're escaping | |
| 4 | The recognition — their exact interior thought ("call by name") | |
| 5 | The blunt promise, no adornment | |

Say each one aloud. The winner is the one you'd actually say to a stranger without wincing — not the one that reads best on the page.

### Step 4: The subtraction cut — "if you had 30 seconds, what would you leave in?"
This is the Watkins move that separates a good line from a master's (`/bw-subtraction` runs the full leave-in/withhold pass; here we apply it just to the log line and its two or three supporting beats). Take the winning log line and the story around it and ask the one question: **if you had 30 seconds, what would you leave in?** Everything sorts into two buckets — *"there's details you need to keep the story moving forward. And then there are details you withhold because you want people to lean in and ask, make them ask."* Keep the momentum, seed-and-withhold the rest, cut the dead weight. Produce the **30-second leave-in cut**: the log line said out loud, plus only the two or three beats that survive the 30-second knife. And the discipline that governs it — *"We're not going to overexplain."*

| Beat in your pitch | Keep / withhold / cut | Why (momentum, seed, or dead weight) |
|---|---|---|
| | | |
| | | |
| | | |

Twain's line is the standard: *"I'm sorry this letter is so long. I didn't have time to make it shorter."* The 30-second cut is you taking the time.

### Step 5: The retell test
The final gate, as the show's host frames it: *"could someone else tell your story after hearing it just once?"* Say your 30-second cut to one real person, then ask them to repeat it back. If they garble it, mangle the want, or forget the obstacle — the log line isn't finished, and the reason, as the host puts it, is this: *"If we can't get our story down to one clean sentence, it's usually a sign that we don't know the one thing that we want people to remember."* Rewrite the line until the retell comes back clean. The retell is the deliverable's proof, not a nicety.

## Content Type Adaptations
| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| **Short-form social (Reels / LinkedIn / X)** | The log line *is* the hook or the first line — lead on the obstacle so the pain sparks the emotion inside the first beat. The 30-second cut is the whole script; there's no room for a second log line. If a viewer couldn't DM your offer to a friend in one sentence, the retell test failed. |
| **Long-form (Substack / YouTube / essay)** | The log line is the spine you never state aloud — it governs which sections live and which get cut, the way `/bw-north-star`'s promise does. The reader should be able to tell a friend what the piece was about in one sentence; if they'd need a paragraph, the log line under it is muddy. |
| **Live pitch / sales / VSL** | The log line is the sentence they repeat in the car — Watkins's real KPI is behavioral: *"the first thing they should do leaving that room is call their boss or call their wife or their husband or grab a co-worker and be talking about the pitch they just heard."* The 30-second cut is your truncate-on-buy-in floor: the version you drop to the instant they've bought, so you never talk past the sale. Retell test = literally ask a colleague to pitch it back. |
| **Brand / campaign** | The log line is the one sentence the whole brand keeps across every asset — customer as hero, brand as the bridge, never the star of its own story. Every campaign asset is audited against it; the "clever" asset that makes the *brand* the hero is exactly the off-log-line piece to cut. This is the line partners like `donald-miller-storybrand` and `one-liner` frame the brand message around. |

  If garbled: rewrote until clean. Final: __________

— WORKED EXAMPLE (indie food brand that just landed in Costco) —

  • Hero: the shopper who roots for the little guy over another faceless conglomerate
  • Want: to keep finding scrappy family brands on the shelf, not just the giants
  • Obstacle / pain: indie brands get pulled from Costco fast if the first weeks don't move

## Quality Gate
> 🛡️ **Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` + § Expert-Specific Quality Rubric (esp. *Subtraction Discipline*, *Recognition*, *Emotional Ignition*). Flag and fix before delivering.
- **The customer is the hero and you are the bridge** — if the brand is the protagonist of its own log line, it fails the customer-as-hero inversion; rewrite so it's about *them*, what they want, what they're up against.
- **The line leads on the problem/pain** — the stakes are the fastest wire to emotion; a log line that opens on the want or the feature is starting cold.
- **It's genuinely one sentence with no "and"** — if it needs two clauses stitched, you have two log lines and don't yet know the one thing to remember. Cut to one.
- **The 30-second cut actually cut something** — at least one beat withheld or killed. *"We're not going to overexplain."* If nothing got left out, you didn't subtract, you summarized.
- **The retell test passed with a real person** — someone repeated it back clean after hearing it once. A log line that can't be retold isn't finished, no matter how good it reads.
- **The machinery stays invisible** — the output never labels "here's the hero, here's the obstacle." The log line just sounds like a sentence a person would say.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Making the brand the hero.** The log line brags about the offer instead of dramatizing the customer's want. Recovery: apply the inversion literally — *"you are the thing that helps them get there,"* not the one the story is about. Rewrite the Hero slot as the customer and demote yourself to the bridge.
- **Leading with the want, not the pain.** The line opens on the shiny after-state and lands flat. Recovery: front-load the obstacle — *"the pain point is the fastest way to spark that emotion."* Stakes first, want second.
- **Smuggling two log lines in one sentence.** An *"and"* joins two promises and the retell test always garbles. Recovery: force the subtraction — pick the one thing you want remembered and cut the other into a separate line or a later beat.
- **Skipping the retell test because the line "reads great."** Reading well on the page is not the bar; being repeatable out loud is. Recovery: say it to one real human and make them pitch it back — *"could someone else tell your story after hearing it just once?"* If not, it isn't done.
