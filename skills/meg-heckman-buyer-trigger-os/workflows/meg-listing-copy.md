# Listing Copy

A design survives the audit. It passes the 50ms gate. It scores a LEAD. Then it lands on a product page that opens with "100% ring-spun cotton, preshrunk for lasting comfort," and the buyer who was already leaning in scrolls past. The copy undid the trigger. The product page is the last gate — won or lost in the first line. Not by describing the item. By reflecting the person standing in front of it.

## Pre-Flight

Read these files before executing:

1. `skills/meg-heckman-buyer-trigger-os/genius.md` (Trigger 6: Emotion-First Reason, Design Mechanics — Mirror vs. Poster, Social Currency Moment)
2. `skills/meg-heckman-buyer-trigger-os/SKILL.md` (workflow routing, vocabulary contract)

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: Answer the Decision Framework from `genius.md` — all seven questions — before writing a single line. If you cannot name the specific behavioral moment (question 1) or the future social moment (question 5), stop. Copy written for "the buyer" produces poster copy. Copy written for "the dancer who stops overthinking and sends the count" produces a mirror. Do not proceed until the person is named as a behavior, not a category.

## Input Required

- The product and design concept (description or reference to the audit scorecard)
- The specific buyer: behavioral moment (from `/meg-trigger-audit` or your own pre-flight)
- The familiar/twist pair for the concept (enables bridge copy when the twist is niche-opaque)
- The platform: own-store PDP / Etsy / Amazon / Bonfire / drop announcement email / Instagram caption
- Anything known about who gifts this, who tags a friend, or who sends it with "this is you" energy

## Workflow

### Step 1: Run the Decision Framework (Mirror Check)

Before copy, answer all seven questions from `genius.md § Decision Framework`. The key three for this workflow: WHO is the one specific person (behavioral moment, not demographic)? What is the familiar/twist pair (this powers the bridge)? What is the future social moment (who laughs, who gifts, who texts "this is you")?

If any answer is "not sure," run `/meg-trigger-audit` first. Copy cannot manufacture a trigger the design doesn't already carry. A poster will produce poster copy no matter how well you write the lead.

### Step 2: Write the Recognition Lead (The Mirror Line)

The first sentence names the behavioral moment. Not the product. Not what it looks like. The person.

Template: "For the [behavioral descriptor] who [specific action or situation]."

Her proven example from the Josh pass: "For the dancer who stops overthinking and sends the count."

The lead is the mirror. Its only job is to make one specific person feel seen fast enough to stop scrolling. Recognition and appreciation are different reactions — appreciation is "that's nice," recognition is "that's me." Read the lead cold. If the reaction is "that's a nice line," rewrite it.

### Step 3: Write the Identity Statement

One to two lines. What owning this says, in first-person adjacent language. Not a tagline. Not brand voice. The thing the buyer would say if someone asked why they bought it, translated one degree toward dignity.

From the Josh pass: "This sits in the tiny overlap between the social floor and the terminal window: timing, trust, and the nerve to commit when the phrase turns over." Dancer with software overlap. Someone who lives in both worlds and didn't think a piece of clothing could hold both until this one did.

### Step 4: Write Bridge Copy (When the Twist Is Niche-Opaque)

If the familiar/twist pair requires context the buyer might not self-supply — write the bridge. The bridge carries the concept across the gap without explaining the joke. It names the world where the joke already makes sense.

The "No Drops In Prod" move: "Social floor is production. Test the chaos in rehearsal." Two sentences. The page creates the room the design opened the door to. Without it, a buyer one step outside the niche sees cleverness without recognition — and cleverness without recognition scrolls past.

**Bridge copy rule**: two sentences maximum. If it takes three sentences to bridge the concept, the concept has a specificity problem. Return to the design.

### Step 5: Write the Future Social Moment Line

The buyer does not only see themselves wearing this. They see the moment after it arrives. This is the "Social Currency Moment" trigger — "Beginners design for product pages, operators design for the moment after the shirt arrives at the customer's door."

Her framing: "my sister would absolutely love this... perfect for that girls trip" — those are the buyer's self-supplied reasons. The copy opens space for them; it does not write them wholesale.

"The person who gets this as a gift will already know the joke" lands differently than "Great gift for your dancing friends!" — the first trusts the buyer. The second sounds like nobody wrote it for anyone.

### Step 6: Write Logic Last (The Specs as Permission)

Fabric, fit, sizing, shipping. These are not the pitch — they are friction removal. The buyer already decided emotionally. The specs confirm they are allowed to buy.

Her framing: "I've been meaning to buy another hoodie anyway" — the buyer writes their own logic. The specs give them a clean sentence to write.

One line on material/weight. One on sizing. One on shipping/production time. No adjectives that double as copy ("luxuriously soft" is copy wearing spec clothing). The section should read like a friend confirming the details, not a brand making promises.

### Step 7: The "That's Nice" Test

Read the full draft cold. If the first reaction is aesthetic appreciation — "that's well written," "that's a nice description" — rewrite from the lead. "Nice doesn't open the wallet."

The draft passes when: the lead produces a specific person who would feel seen (not a segment who might find it appealing); the identity statement reads true, not aspirational; the bridge lands in the world, not in an explanation; the social moment pictures a real exchange, not a stock image; the specs feel like confirmation.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Platform | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Own-store PDP** | Full five-section sequence as written — lead → identity → bridge → social moment → specs. No character limits; the page has room for the emotion to build. |
| **Etsy listing** | Keywords live in back-end fields, tags, and titles — the visible copy stays a mirror. "Swing dance shirt for lindy hop dancers who love coding" is a poster. "For the dancer who stops overthinking and sends the count" is a mirror that also carries the organic language. Compress the bridge if needed; do not cut it — the bridge is where Etsy listings collapse into product descriptions. |
| **Amazon listing** | Back-end search terms handle SEO. Visible copy: lead → identity → social moment → specs. Feature bullets below the fold (Amazon expects them), but open each bullet with the behavioral moment before the spec: "For dancers who double as engineers — [spec]" over "[spec] — great for dance and tech fans." |
| **Bonfire campaign page** | More storytelling room than a PDP. Lead + identity carry through. Add a second social moment paragraph for the community/cause layer — what buying this says you belong to. Gift framing is especially active here; Bonfire buyers are often buying for a cause and a specific person simultaneously. |
| **Drop announcement email** | Subject line = recognition lead compressed. Body: identity → social moment → specs in one paragraph. Open with the feeling, not the product name. "The dancer who stops overthinking and sends the count — this is for them." Then the design. Then the window. Then the link. |
| **Instagram caption** | First line = recognition lead (visible before "more"). Second line = identity or bridge. Social moment = last line before CTA. No specs in caption — Stories or a pinned comment. Pure mirror and social moment. |

## Output Format

```
MEG LISTING COPY — [concept] — [platform] — [date]
TARGET: [behavioral moment — the mirror person]
SEQUENCE: [emotion] → [identity] → [social moment]

LEAD: [recognition lead — behavioral moment, never the product first]
IDENTITY: [1-2 sentences — what owning this says, first-person adjacent]
BRIDGE: [2 sentences max — only when twist is niche-opaque]
SOCIAL MOMENT: [1 sentence — the after-purchase moment]
SPECS: [material · sizing · shipping — one line each]

THAT'S NICE TEST: [PASS / FAIL + section to rewrite]
FACTUAL GROUNDING: [UNCONFIRMED on any revenue figures | N/A if none cited]
```

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review against `genius.md § Anti-Patterns` before delivering. Emotion-first is a sequencing law, not a creative preference. Violating it produces a product description that happens to have a warm first line.

- Lead names a behavioral moment, not a product ("For the [person who does X]" — not "Introducing the [product]" or "If you love [category]").
- No spec-led openings anywhere in the lead, identity, bridge, or social moment sections.
- Bridge is present when the twist is niche-opaque; absent when the concept is self-explanatory. Bridge explaining a self-evident concept kills recognition speed.
- Future social moment is specific — "Makes a great gift" is poster-level.
- Logic is last. Specs remove friction; they do not pitch.
- Her revenue/sales figures, if cited, carry UNCONFIRMED labels — self-reported, not independently verified.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Feature-first relapse.** "Premium heavyweight cotton tee featuring original artwork — for the dancer who..." The spec crept into the lead before the person did. Recovery: cut everything before "For the." The lead is the person, always.

- **Over-explaining the joke.** The bridge became a paragraph. Recognition speed is dead. Recovery: the bridge is a reframe, not a definition. "Social floor is production. Test the chaos in rehearsal." The reader either lands in the world or doesn't. Trust the ones who land.

- **Writing to everyone.** "For dancers, coders, and anyone who appreciates the overlap between movement and technology." That is a poster. Recovery: name the one person. The involuntary "send this to Sarah" reflex requires Sarah — not "anyone who might appreciate it."

- **Keyword-stuffing the visible lead.** "Swing dance shirt — lindy hop — social dance for developers — coding dancer tee." The algorithm can index this; the buyer cannot feel seen by it. Recovery: keywords live in back-end fields and tags. A well-written mirror carries the long-tail language naturally without announcing it.

- **Mistaking warmth for recognition.** "Aw, that's sweet" fails the same test as "that's nice." Warmth is appreciation. Recognition is "that's me." Recovery: the lead describes a behavioral moment that is the buyer's actual life, not a category they belong to.
