# The 50ms Gate

"Design A has 10 things fighting for your eye. Your brain doesn't know where to look. In 50 milliseconds, that's chaos." The woodblock with the mountains, the eagle, the van, the waterfall — the one that wins every design competition — sold zero in four months. A hiker and bold text sold 3,714 (UNCONFIRMED). This gate exists because the buyer's brain has already decided before you finish your sentence. The question is not whether the design is good. The question is whether anything survives a stranger's cold scroll. This workflow is fast by design. If running it takes more than five minutes, you're already over-thinking — which is the failure mode it exists to prevent.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing:
1. `skills/meg-heckman-buyer-trigger-os/genius.md` (Design Mechanics: 50ms Gate, Conceptual Interrupt)
2. `skills/meg-heckman-buyer-trigger-os/references/genius-patterns.md` (Patterns 8–9)

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: Run this gate COLD — before you've explained the design to yourself, before you've read the creative brief, before you've fallen in love with the craft. If you've been staring at it for 20 minutes, hand the gate to someone who hasn't. A warm eye is the one variable that breaks this test.

## Input Required

- The design: file, mockup, screenshot, or detailed text description
- The intended format: apparel, ad creative, thumbnail, landing hero, pitch slide, logo lockup
- Nothing else is required — the gate does not ask about the brief, the story, or the intent

## Workflow

### Step 1: Focal-Point Count

Count every element competing for the eye. Not elements you notice on close inspection — elements that pull attention in the first pass.

Apply her standard directly: one or two focal elements pass. Three or more is chaos.

> "Design A has 10 things fighting for your eye. Your brain doesn't know where to look. In 50 milliseconds, that's chaos."

She cites: "designs with one to two main elements account for 56% of all sales" — **UNCONFIRMED**. Label it as such if you repeat it. Deploy the principle regardless.

| Element count | Result |
|---|---|
| 1–2 focal points | Proceed to Step 2 |
| 3+ focal points | FAIL — stop here, issue directive |

If the design fails Step 1, skip to Step 5. The remaining tests are irrelevant when the buyer's brain never gets past the chaos.

### Step 2: The 5-Word Test

Describe the design in five words or fewer. Not the concept — the design as a stranger sees it at scroll speed.

If you cannot produce it cleanly, the buyer cannot either. "One thing loudly rather than three things quietly."

Reach for it instantly. If you pause to find the words, the buyer has already scrolled.

### Step 3: Conceptual-Interrupt Check

The goal is a second look without confusion. Those are two separate requirements and both must be met.

**The interrupt has to live in the IDEA.** A sloth in hiking boots is a conceptual surprise — two things the brain has never paired, both completely belonging to the niche's visual world. The brain can't auto-filter what it hasn't categorized before. That's the orienting response. It's involuntary.

> "The interrupt has to lead somewhere... It's the door. It's not the room."

The anti-case is the AI with 27 fingers. That catches attention. It also produces "what is wrong with this?" — attention WITH confusion, which buys nothing. Visual weirdness is not a conceptual interrupt. It is just chaos with better composition.

Check both:
1. Does the surprise live in the IDEA (a pairing, a contradiction, a twist on something familiar) — or is it just visual noise?
2. Does the execution still belong to the niche's visual world? A stranger scrolling this niche's feed would recognize the category, even if they've never seen this specific pairing.

Both must pass. Visual weirdness without conceptual grounding: FAIL.

### Step 4: Squint and Shrink Test

Shrink the design to feed-thumbnail size. Then squint.

One question: does a single message survive?

Not the full design — the single message. Apparel: does the identity statement hold at the size someone sees it on a person across a coffee shop? Ad creative: at scroll speed on mobile, what lands? YouTube thumbnail: does the design communicate before the title loads?

This is the physical version of the 5-word test. If the message blurs or fragments, the design was not built for where it will actually live.

### Step 5: Verdict + The One Directive

**PASS**: all four steps clear. The design survives cold-scroll speed, produces a five-word description, carries a conceptual interrupt that leads somewhere, and holds its message at thumbnail scale.

**FAIL**: any single step fails. One failure is enough. Identify which step failed and issue ONE directive below.

The directive is always removal-led. Never addition. Adding a new element to "fix" a 50ms problem compounds it. Something gets removed or merged — not supplemented.

Format the verdict as:

```
VERDICT: [PASS / FAIL]
Failed step: [Step 1 / 2 / 3 / 4]

Directive: Remove [specific element or elements]. The one thing that must survive is [X].
```

The directive names the specific removal. Not "simplify the design." Not "reduce visual noise." Specifically: which element comes out, and what the remaining anchor is.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Apparel design | Focal-point count = elements competing for eye on the garment; squint test = visibility at across-the-room distance, worn in public |
| Ad creative | 5-word test = the single hook that loads before the viewer decides to stop scrolling; interrupt check critical — visual chaos in ad creative = immediate scroll-past |
| YouTube thumbnail | Thumbnail must communicate WITHOUT the title; squint test = what lands at 120×90px; one message only |
| Landing hero (above the fold) | 50ms gate = the single claim the visitor reads before any scroll; one message, no competing anchors above the fold; this is the gate's strictest application |
| Pitch slide | Focal-point count applies to the single slide under review; conceptual interrupt = does the key idea produce an involuntary "wait, what?" that the rest of the slide answers |
| Logo lockup | Squint test at business-card size; 5-word test on the NAME + mark together as a single identity statement |

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check** before delivering:
- Verdict rendered faster than it took to over-think — this gate is fast by design; deliberation is a red flag
- Directive is removal-led — addition never appears as a fix
- No aesthetic judgment leaked in — "pretty," "well-executed," and "clean design" are not criteria here; the only criterion is survival at 50ms
- Her 56% stat labeled UNCONFIRMED if cited
- The conceptual-interrupt check distinguishes idea-based surprise from visual chaos explicitly — both types of "attention" are not the same thing

---

## Common Pitfalls

- **Adding elements to "fix" clarity.** A design failing the 5-word test needs subtraction, not a subtitle. Recovery: identify the one thing that must survive, and remove everything else.

- **Respecting the craft so much the chaos survives.** "As a piece of art, design A is the better design" (UNCONFIRMED — her live assessment). It sold zero. Craft quality is not a criterion here. Recovery: set the brief down before Step 1. Judge the artifact cold.

- **Letting the interrupt be visual weirdness instead of conceptual surprise.** An AI with 27 fingers catches attention and produces "what is wrong with this?" — not a purchase. Recovery: Step 3 distinguishes these explicitly. Both halves must pass.

- **Running the gate after falling in love.** The most common failure. A warm eye stops seeing the design cold — it sees the story, the effort, the ambition. The buyer sees none of that. Recovery: run the gate first, before brief review.
