# Sub-Identity Map

Every niche that looks crowded is full of brands talking to the same person. The summit-chaser. The serious cyclist. The competitive griller. That person is served. What the niche never notices is the other person — the one who does the thing at half the intensity and is twice as proud of it, because nobody ever acknowledged them before. That gap is not a problem. It is the whole business. This workflow maps who the niche is ignoring, builds the behavioral evidence that they exist, and hands you a dossier specific enough that you can picture one person standing in your store — because if you can't picture them, you can't mirror them, and a product that mirrors nobody is decoration.

## Input Required
- The niche or category under consideration (one sentence: "POD hiking apparel," "streetwear for EDM festival-goers," "LinkedIn content coaching for B2B consultants")
- Any existing brand assets, store name, or positioning if already decided — or "cold start" if building from zero
- Where the product lives: POD marketplace (Etsy/Amazon/Redbubble), own Shopify store, or hybrid
- A rough revenue target (optional but sharpens the 0.01% TAM framing in Step 1)

## Workflow

### Step 1: Saturation Reframe — Convert Competition into Proof

Before mapping the niche, kill the objection that will resurface at every decision point. "When somebody tells you that a niche is saturated, what they're actually telling you, without realizing it, is that the niche has proven demand." The amount of competition is not a warning. It is the evidence.

Nobody tells a restaurateur not to open a pizza place because pizza is saturated. The question is never "is there too much pizza?" — it is "what kind of pizza does this neighborhood not have yet?"

Run the demand audit:

| Signal | What to find | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| Search volume anchor | Monthly searches for the niche's primary keyword | Google Keyword Planner, Etsy search suggest, Amazon auto-fill |
| Competitor count | # of active stores selling to this niche | Etsy search, Merch Informer, manual Shopify scan |
| Revenue indicators | BSR, bestseller badges, review velocity on top listings | Jungle Scout, Etsy listing reviews, Amazon BSR |
| TAM estimate | Published market-size figures | Industry reports, Statista, IBISWorld |

**0.01% Framing**: Once you have a TAM figure, translate the user's revenue target against it.

> Example: Hiking niche, $28B market UNCONFIRMED (cited by Heckman). A $2.8M/year revenue target = 0.01% of that market. Label ALL market-size statistics UNCONFIRMED until sourced. The math still holds — the point is that you need almost nobody in a large-enough niche.

> **TAM Note**: Do not invent market-size figures. If no sourced figure is available, state: "TAM not sourced — substitute your own research before client presentation." Proceed on demand evidence (search volume, review velocity) alone.

**Checkpoint**: Can you name at least two demand signals that confirm real buying behavior exists here? If yes, proceed. If no, the niche is either too new or too narrow — name that constraint before continuing.

---

### Step 2: Map the Dominant Identity

The niche is already serving someone. Name them exactly, because that identity is the background you're contrasting against. Every ad in this space has the same implied buyer. Every bestseller is a mirror for the same person. The more precisely you can name who the niche already serves, the sharper the gap becomes.

| Dominant-identity dimension | What to capture |
|---|---|
| The behavior that defines them | What do they do that the niche celebrates? ("summits peaks," "logs PRs," "wears technical gear on casual trails") |
| What every brand says to them | The copy language across the top 10 competitors (pull actual taglines and product titles) |
| How they signal status | What makes this person the "serious" version in the niche's eyes? |
| What they're NOT | What casual or adjacent version of the activity does the niche dismiss or ignore? |

Write the dominant identity as a behavioral moment, not a demographic: not "serious hikers, 28–45, suburban" but "the person who leaves for the trail at 4:00 AM, posts the summit photo, and already has the next three peaks mapped." One sentence. First-person optional: *"I train for this. This is who I am."*

This is the identity the niche has already captured. Any brand you build that goes after the same person is bringing a weaker version of the same thing to a fight it can't win.

---

### Step 3: Generate 3–5 Ignored Sub-Identity Candidates

The winning move is not to find a different niche. It is to find a different person inside the same proven niche. That person is there. The niche's demand signals include them, but no brand is talking directly to them.

Generate candidates as **behavioral moments only** — never demographics, never job titles, never age ranges. The unit of a sub-identity is a recognizable moment of lived behavior: what the person does, how they feel about it, what they quietly call themselves.

Write each candidate using this structure:

**Candidate [#] — [working name]**
- Behavioral moment: [What they actually do in one or two sentences — the scene, not the label]
- What they're proud of that nobody validates: [The specific win the niche treats as "not real" or ignores entirely]
- Their inside language: [The words they use among themselves that the dominant identity would never say]
- Where they congregate: [Subreddits, Facebook groups, hashtags, Discord servers, comment threads]

> Heckman's calibration anchor: "The casual hiker. The person who goes for a 30-minute stroll on a mountain and calls it a hike, and they're genuinely proud of that." That is the candidate format — specific behavior, specific pride, zero demographic targeting.

Candidate quality check: if you could swap the behavioral moment between two candidates without it mattering, the candidates are not distinct enough. Each one should feel like a different scene, not a different filter on the same person.

---

### Step 4: Billboard Test + Identity-vs-Decoration Binary

Each candidate gets two gates before selection. Do not skip either.

**Gate A — The Billboard Test**

"A graphic tee is not a decoration, it is a billboard." For each candidate, write the billboard: the one-line identity statement a product would make on their behalf. This is what the wearer says about themselves the moment someone reads the shirt, the mug, the post, the bio.

| Candidate | Billboard statement (first person, one sentence) | Mirror or Poster? |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate 1 | "I ___ and I'm not apologizing for it." | |
| Candidate 2 | | |
| Candidate 3 | | |

A poster names a category ("I love hiking"). A mirror reflects a specific behavior the buyer recognizes as privately theirs ("I 'hiked' for 30 minutes and I'm already calling it a win"). Posters do not proceed. If a candidate only produces a poster billboard, either sharpen it or drop it.

**Gate B — Belonging Evidence**

An ignored identity without a belonging community is not a market. It is a hypothesis. The community must pre-exist — you are serving it, not creating it. For each mirror candidate, name:

- One specific place they gather (link or platform + community name if possible)
- One phrase or inside joke only they would use
- One content creator, account, or piece of media they already follow that partially reflects them

If you cannot find belonging evidence for a candidate, do not build for them. The absence of a community is not an opportunity — it is a signal that the identity does not have the critical mass to sustain a brand.

---

### Step 5: Select + Write the Full Sub-Identity Dossier

Select one candidate. The right candidate has:

- Mirror-grade billboard (behavioral, first-person, specific)
- At least one named belonging community with forward-impulse energy — not just people who exist, but people who are actively celebrating this identity among themselves
- A quiet pride that the dominant niche ignores or gently dismisses (this is the wound the brand heals)
- Language you can steal directly into product titles, copy, and brand voice

Write the full dossier. This becomes the brief for every downstream workflow.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|--------|-----------|
| POD niche (Etsy / Amazon / Redbubble) | Full workflow as-is; belonging community evidence doubles as keyword-mining source — their language is your search terms |
| Streetwear / independent apparel brand | Belonging community becomes brand community; Step 5 dossier feeds drop strategy, not just product titles; inside jokes become collection names |
| Client category entry (service business) | "Dominant identity" = the client the market currently serves; sub-identity = the underserved client segment who exists but feels unseen; dossier feeds positioning and offer naming |
| Personal brand / LinkedIn positioning | Sub-identity gap = LinkedIn positioning wedge: find the professional the niche of thought leaders ignores; the billboard statement becomes the "about" hook; belonging community = where your actual readers are, before you have any |
| SaaS / newsletter category | Dominant identity = the power user every tool and newsletter targets; sub-identity = the less "serious" user who needs the thing but doesn't want the identity overhead; inside language becomes headline copy |

## Output Format

See the dossier block in Step 5. Deliver exactly that format — nothing else. The dossier is the product; surrounding commentary is waste.

---

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Sub-Identity Integrity Check**: Review each criterion before delivering the dossier.
- The chosen sub-identity is written entirely in behavioral moments — no age, no income bracket, no job title used as targeting criteria anywhere in the dossier.
- Saturation is treated as demand proof in Step 1 — never as a warning or disqualifier. If it was used as a warning, revise.
- The chosen identity has at least one named belonging community — a specific platform/group/hashtag — not "probably exists somewhere."
- TAM and market-size statistics are labeled UNCONFIRMED unless a primary source is cited in the dossier.
- The dossier ends with a person you can picture in a specific scene — not a segment you can describe on a slide.
- The familiar seed in Step 5 gives `/meg-concept-sprint` something to work with immediately (not "TBD").
- The marketplace-vs-platform note is present and specific to the user's actual setup.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Picking the underserved-but-nonexistent identity.** The most dangerous candidate is one you find compelling but nobody is actively celebrating. An identity without a belonging community is a hypothesis, not a market. Recovery: return to Step 4 Gate B — if you cannot find one specific place they gather, the identity fails the gate.

- **Narrowing to absurdity.** There is a version of specificity that produces an identity so narrow it cannot sustain a brand — "people who hike only on Tuesdays in rain" is not a sub-identity, it is a filter. Recovery: check that the behavioral moment has enough members to pass the 0.01% framing — even 10,000 people spending $50/year is a real business.

- **Choosing the identity the operator likes vs. the one with forward-impulse energy.** Operator taste is not a belonging signal. If the chosen identity feels right to you but shows no evidence of people actively celebrating it among themselves, you have picked for yourself, not the market. Recovery: find the candidate where the belonging community is loudest, not the one that feels most interesting.

- **Demographic relapse.** The dossier passes intake containing "women 25–40 who hike casually." That is a demographic, not an identity. Demographics describe who is in a room; behavioral moments describe why they'd put on the shirt. Recovery: strip all demographic language and rewrite every candidate description as a scene — what they do, how they feel about it, what they call it privately.

- **Skipping the billboard test on candidates who seem "obviously" good.** The billboard test is not a formality — it is the moment you find out whether the identity produces a mirror or a poster. Skipping it lets weak candidates through to design work. Recovery: run the test cold, as a stranger reading the shirt for the first time with zero context.
