## The core claim: build the reference set, then shoot it

A Ward pre-production package is not a mood board. It is a set of **reusable identity assets** —
character sheets, location plates, a voice bed, a style anchor — each built once at high quality
and then referenced into every generation. The shot list is the spine that says which asset goes
where. Generation is the last step and the cheapest to redo.

This is why his failures are recoverable and most people's aren't: when a shot comes back wrong,
he changes the reference assignment, not the adjectives.

## The 26 patterns

### A. Story-first, before any tool opens

**1. The ladder, and where it stops for you.** Idea → logline → treatment → screenplay → visual
inspiration → characters → shot list → (optional) storyboard → plates → motion → edit → finish.
*"A film always starts with an idea."* [FILM26] 00:40. The logline is *"a simple sentence that
explains what your core idea is all about. It will give information about who the characters are
and what the conflict is."* [FILM26] 01:01.

**2. The treatment is a coordination artifact, not a creative one.** *"A treatment is a single
document that lists out your characters, the conflict, and the main story beats… If you're working
on a film by yourself, you don't have to put together a treatment. It's entirely up to you, but it
can be helpful if you want to involve more people in the process."* [FILM26] 01:22–01:47.
**Mechanism:** the treatment's function is alignment. Solo, it's overhead. The moment a second
person (or a client, or a sub-agent) touches the project, it becomes mandatory. Decide by headcount,
not by ritual.

**3. AI as creative assistant, never as author.** *"If you rely on AI alone, your script is frankly
going to be pretty boring and terrible. But if you use AI to assist you with the process of putting
together a script, that's where the magic happens."* [FILM26] 01:57–02:08. *"When you use AI more as
a creative assistant rather than somebody who is replacing you in the storytelling process, it's
where the magic happens."* [FILM26] 01:12. And the sourcing practice: *"The best stories come from
your own personal experience and observations with the world, and so I do recommend keeping a
journal or a notepad."* [ANIME26] 03:16–03:24. His own worked example is exactly that — *"fusing
the idea of an anime project with the city of Positano that I was able to visit whenever Shelby and
I were digital nomads a few years ago… an intense high action scene… taking place in Positano,
which is a very idyllic and slow-paced environment."* [ANIME26] 01:36–01:55. **Idea = a personal
observation crossed with a tonal opposite.**

**4. Plan the tension curve, not the shot count.** Reverse-engineering his own scene: *"there was a
level of tension that it started out with, and then we alleviated it, and then we built up the
tension as the scene progressed."* [ANIME26] 03:02–03:08. The scene is designed as a felt shape
first; shots are what deliver the shape.

### B. The shot list floor and the sketch board

**5. The shot list is the non-negotiable floor; the storyboard is optional.** *"You need to at the
very least have a shot list, if not a storyboard, for your film projects because it just helps with
communication and keeping everything organized."* [FILM26] 08:13. And keep it cheap: *"you can keep
this list inside a simple spreadsheet. You don't have to get super complicated with it."*
[FILM26] 08:34.

**6. The sketch board's real job is protecting your own decision-space.** The crown-jewel pattern,
and the one nobody else in the house states:

> *"A storyboard is a rough visual approximation of what you want the composition to look like…
> I really find that with simple sketches, it allows you to have more creative flexibility, and your
> brain doesn't get so locked in on some of the supporting details that will be found whenever you
> create photorealistic imagery."* — [FILM26] 08:41–09:12

**Mechanism:** a photoreal frame is an *answer*; a sketch is a *question*. Generate photoreal too
early and you start art-directing accidents — the lamp, the extra, the wall texture the model chose —
instead of deciding the shot. Roughness is a feature: it keeps the composition decision separable
from the render decision. This survives every tool generation because it's a fact about *your*
cognition, not the model's.

**7. The doctrine moved, honestly, between April and June 2026 — and only halfway.** By [ANIME26]
he says: *"in the past… you would then have to go in and create individual storyboards of each of
the shots… you actually don't need to do that anymore. AI tools are so intelligent now that you can
describe what you want to see."* [ANIME26] 09:36–09:57. But the reason he gives is specific, not
general: *"I didn't have to upload a storyboard to be specific about what I was looking for because
really it was the idea of the shot and not the individual composition being exactly right that
would help me to tell this specific story."* [ANIME26] 15:43–15:53.

**The decision rule that falls out of it — the composition question.** Board the shot when the
*composition* is load-bearing (a reveal, a geography-establishing move, an eyeline that has to
match, a frame the story depends on). Skip the board and write the shot in prose when only the
*idea* of the shot matters. The shot list never becomes optional; the drawing does.

### C. Reference-asset architecture

**8. The character sheet is the identity lock, and it gets a different budget.** Four panels —
*"one of the panels front-facing, one to the side, one behind, and then a close-up of his face"*
[FILM26] 06:15–06:26; the [ANIME26] version adds expressions: *"Create a dynamic character sheet of
the man in image number two, in the same painting style as image number one. Show me the man from
the front, back, and side, and have him in different expressions. Include one close-up of his
face."* [ANIME26] 06:00–06:17. And the budget rule: *"because this is going to be an asset that we
use again and again as a character reference, we want to have maximum resolution."* [FILM26]
06:35–06:42. **Reference assets get maximum quality because the cost amortises across the whole
film; shot plates get only what the next step consumes.**

**9. One identity per sheet — a contaminated sheet poisons every downstream shot.** A generation
that put two characters on one sheet: *"we don't want the other character to be in there cuz that
would be very confusing for the AI video model."* [ANIME26] 07:16–07:19. A character sheet is a
*definition*, and definitions can't be ambiguous.

**10. Locations are reference assets too — and a location is a set of angles, not an image.**
*"Environmental consistency is a real challenge when you're working with an AI tool, and so having
imagery that can define what the actual environment and scene is like is just really helpful for
continuity."* [ANIME26] 07:51–08:02. His own bank for one scene: restaurant, narrow alley, hotel
interior, **and a second angle of the same alley** [ANIME26] 08:35–08:47. Building the second angle
before you need it is what makes the reverse shot possible later.

**11. Style is anchored to a picked image, not to adjectives.** He goes to a browsable image library
for style *"and still to this day I think stylistically Midjourney is still the best tool on the
market"* [ANIME26] 03:52–03:56 — and picks with a reason, not a vibe: *"I liked how it was kind of
this anime manga style, but then it almost had elements of oil painting in it, which I thought was a
pretty cool fusion of both classical Italian style and then also the anime aesthetic."* [ANIME26]
04:24–04:36. The style anchor image then rides into every subsequent generation as a reference.
Standing practice: *"just every now and then just to go through and download images that you find to
be inspiring for future projects."* [ANIME26] 04:03–04:08 — a personal style bank, built ahead of
need.

**12. IP contamination enters at the moodboard, not at the render.** *"You can see a lot of Vikings
from the TV show Vikings. So that is problematic if you want to monetize your film in the future.
So just be sure that any images and videos that you create do not have any third-party IP."*
[FILM26] 03:02–03:15. Same eye on a lighting-preset library: *"they are definitely not shying away
from just like the blatant IP rip-off in the lighting."* [CINE26] 08:28–08:33. **The clearance
decision belongs in inspiration-gathering, where it is free, not in delivery, where it is fatal.**

### D. Board → plate conversion

**13. Decompose the shot into orthogonal channels and pin each to its own reference.** The actual
move: upload the storyboard frame, the character sheet and the style, then instruct
*"use the composition from image number one, which is our storyboard, and keep the character as at
character number one."* [FILM26] 11:40–11:50. **Composition · identity · style are three separate
channels.** Naming which reference owns which channel is the whole technique — and it is what makes
a wrong result diagnosable (which channel drifted?) instead of just wrong.

**14. Volume beats prompt-craft at the discovery stage — and mis-diagnosing this is the classic
trap.**

> *"If you're only getting one output at a time, you may think that the problem is your prompt
> whenever the actual problem is just you didn't generate enough images."* — [FILM26] 07:44–07:52

Hence his rejection of one-shot chat interfaces for creative work [FILM26] 07:32–07:41, and the
batch habit: raise generation count to the maximum, then *"hit that generate button a few times"*
[FILM26] 04:29. **Never conclude anything about a prompt from a single output.**

**15. Iteration is normalised in advance so a bad take doesn't kill the plan.** *"You're going to
get really weird results, and that's all a part of the process."* [FILM26] 07:20–07:24.
*"In the same way that it's completely normal to be on a film set and do multiple takes of a scene
to get exactly what you're looking for."* [ANIME26] 12:32–12:37. His worked count: seven generations
to get one usable 15-second opening [ANIME26] 14:49–14:53.

**16. The salvage rule — judge at the shot level, never the generation level.** *"Just because an
entire generation fails does not mean there's not something you could salvage from there… if you do
like certain aspects of a scene that was generated, go in and cut it out and use it as a select."*
[ANIME26] 13:03–13:17. And the editorial version: a generation with a hallucinated tower in the
background is still a keeper because *"all we really need of that shot is just about a half of a
second."* [FILM26] 15:44–15:47. **A failed generation is a bin of selects until an editor has looked
at it.**

### E. Coverage and chaining

**17. Prompted coverage — brief the model like a second unit, not a still camera.** *"You could
break down shot by shot exactly what you want to see, or you can have the AI system give you coverage
that will help you to tell the overall story that you're looking to tell."* [ANIME26] 11:01–11:12.
The prompt that does it reads as a scene brief with an emotional target attached:
*"Play out the following scene in this hotel lobby. Have the man in the character sheet sitting in a
chair, and have multiple cuts as we see guests enjoying the hotel, but keep the same painted anime
style represented in both of the uploaded images"* — closing with *"he's at peace. So we want the
entire vibe to be very very tranquil."* [ANIME26] 11:14–11:40. **The unit of generation moved from
shot to sequence; the unit of *direction* moved from composition to intent.**

**18. Rolling-reference chaining — the edit becomes the continuity carrier.** Cut a ~15-second
sequence from what you have, then feed that cut back in as the reference for the next generation:
*"Continue the scene using the uploaded reference footage, and keep the character of the woman as
the woman and the man as the man."* [ANIME26] 15:28–15:34. And the context-packing trick:
*"it doesn't have to be a direct scene. If you want to add even more contextual data, you can break
up those clips into 1 second chunks in that 15 second video clip to give the AI system as much
context as possible."* [ANIME26] 17:16–17:27. **Your reference window is a budget — you can spend it
on one continuous beat or on fifteen one-second context cards, and that is a directorial choice.**

**19. Start with the easiest shot, because it becomes the reference for the hard ones.** *"I think I
want to start with what I believe to be the easiest part of the entire scene."* [ANIME26] 09:21–09:27
— a man reading a magazine, at peace, before the chase and the fight. **Cheap, controllable beat
first; the resulting clip then anchors identity, style and world for every expensive beat after it.**

### F. Voice, performance and the human seam

**20. The voice ladder — four rungs, each with a named failure, with a verdict.** [VOICE26], whole
video. Rung 1, prompt-directed native audio: works only for broad characters — *"This tends to work
better if your characters are much more over the top. Whenever they are more basic as characters, it
can be very noticeable that the voice will change from scene to scene."* 01:54–02:07. Rung 2, the
video tool's own voice clone: rejected — *"you can hear that metallic AI timbre that ultimately is
not what you would want inside a professional project… simply not up to par if you are working on a
professional project."* 07:00–07:14. Rung 3, speech-to-speech replacement: consistent but strips the
world — *"the voice is now consistent, but the big problem is the sound effects and kind of room tone
are not being introduced into the scene."* 08:38–08:46 — fixed by isolating the background stem,
re-layering, and *"adjust EQ and add in reverb if they're inside of a room just to help it match and
feel like it lives together in the scene."* 10:07–10:17. Rung 4, current best: condition the
generation itself on a reference-audio asset — pass ~15 seconds of the target voice and instruct
*"use the audio from at video number one."* 12:26–12:30. Verdict, honestly bounded: *"it's not
identical to my voice, but it's pretty much as close as you could hope to get utilizing AI tools
before you literally have to go through the ADR process."* 13:33–13:43.

**21. Cast the voice once, as an asset — and cast it with range, not just timbre.** *"All you need
is about 15 seconds of your character's performance. Now, it is better if you have different
stylistic clips to inform the tonality of your character when they're performing different types of
audio. So, for example, 15 seconds of them being kind of sad or 15 seconds of them being excited."*
[VOICE26] 13:50–14:12. **A voice reference is a performance-range sample, not an identity sample.**
Same architectural idea as the character sheet's multiple angles — coverage, applied to sound.

**22. ADR inverted: let the AI set the timing, then have the human match it.** The load-bearing
hybrid rule, and it is a *sequencing* rule:

> *"You might think that it's now time to create our voices. But when you're working in an AI
> pipeline, it's actually easier to apply the voices in post-production using ADR. Basically, you're
> going to take the voice that AI gives you, and then have a live-action voice actor speak over it.
> And they're then going to apply the voice once the timing has been worked out and animated."*
> — [ANIME26] 08:49–09:13

Traditional ADR locks picture to a human performance. Here picture *generates* the performance
timing and the human matches it — *"work with your voice actors to do ADR in post to match their lips
exactly to what the character's performance looks like."* [ANIME26] 18:43–18:49. The enabling craft
step is source separation: isolate vocals from the generated clip, keep the background bed
(*"not only did it remove the voice, it also left the sound effects and the music bed intact"*
[ANIME26] 18:33–18:36), and hand editor + actor a clean stem pair.

**23. Direct performance in performance language, not model language.** The prompt shape he uses is a
director's note: camera behaviour + the line in quotation marks + emotional state + a camera
constraint — *"a handheld camera shot of a man with trepidation saying, 'So, I've been thinking a lot
lately and I don't know if we should drink coffee anymore.' And he's having a serious conversation,
really reinforcing what we want to see from the character, and then the camera stays on the man. We
don't want the camera to be moving around the scene."* [VOICE26] 03:00–03:17. His house ordering:
*"we always like using the CCR method here at Curious Refuge. That's camera, character, and then
rig."* [CINE26] 13:12–13:16. And his critiques stay in the same register: *"the director on set needs
to tell her, 'Hey, you got to look at me. You're breaking the fourth wall a little too much. You're
making the audience uncomfortable.'"* [CINE26] 13:41–13:47. **Say it the way you'd say it to a
performer; the fix lives in the direction.**

### G. Judgment, economics and where it's heading

**24. Models are DPs with temperaments — audition them per shot.** *"There's not a best AI video tool
on the market. There are some tools that are better than others, but for some specific shots, you may
have to hop between different AI video models."* [FILM26] 13:21–13:31. His method is a parallel
bake-off: same image, same prompt, four models at once [VOICE26] 02:21–02:39, judged on craft, not
spec — timbre, timing, whether the audio *"rises to the surface"*, whether the performance holds
across two shots.

**25. Resolution follows reuse and style tolerance, not vanity — and the film is budgeted in
generations.** Tiering: maximum resolution for reference assets (reused forever), mid for plates that
feed a video model, low for stylised motion that will be up-ressed — *"when you're working on a 2D or
anime style aesthetic, you actually can run with 720 because it's much cheaper to take that 720
version and then up res it in post as opposed to generating it natively in 4K."* [ANIME26] 11:50–12:05.
And the quote arithmetic he does out loud: *"it took seven generations, which each generation was $3
each. And so it was ultimately about $21 to get to where we're wanting to go, which is more or less
80 bucks per minute. So to put together a 2-minute long short film… conservatively, it would be about
150 to 160 dollars."* [ANIME26] 14:51–15:12. **Iteration count × unit cost × runtime = the number you
can quote before you start.** He never pretends it's free: *"this is not a free creative medium."*
[ANIME26] 12:14.

**26. The node-graph refusal, and the canvas that replaced it.** Offered a node-based canvas, he
declines: *"I actually do not prefer to utilize that workflow whenever I'm working on a film project
because it can get messy and slow very quickly."* [FILM26] 10:08–10:14. What he uses instead is
spatial and glanceable — storyboard row on top, finished plates directly beneath: *"organize the
final shots below your storyboard. So it's all just in one place."* [FILM26] 13:13–13:17.
**Pre-production wants a wall, not a dependency graph.**

**27. Where it's going — write pre-production in terms that survive the 3D transition.** *"The future
of AI filmmaking is very much going to be a 3D informed process."* [CINE26] 07:45–07:50. In a
world-model tool he does not prompt a camera, he *places* one — position, focal length, aspect —
then projects a character into it [CINE26] 03:32–04:32. **Blocking, focal length, eyeline and camera
position transfer to that lane; prompt adjectives do not.** Write shot lists in the transferable
vocabulary now.

**28. A preset that is secretly a prompt will drift beyond its stated parameter.** Testing a
one-click relight: *"it changed her pose. Like it's only supposed to change the lighting, but it
actually changed the character entirely… a lot of these lighting presets are actually prompt presets
that sometimes can go awry."* [CINE26] 11:22–11:38. His better move is to *show* the light rather
than name it — upload a reference photograph carrying the grade you want and ask for its lighting and
colour to be brought across [CINE26] 09:39–10:08 — while expecting to finish by hand: *"you'll
probably have to go in by hand… to really finesse it."* 10:17–10:23. His hedged prediction, stated as
a hedge: *"I have this nagging theory that the future of lighting design on a film set is primarily
going to happen in post-production as opposed to it happening natively on location."* [CINE26]
10:35–10:43.

## Signature moves

1. **The four-panel dynamic character sheet** — front · side · back · face close-up, plus
   expressions, one identity only, max resolution, generated in the locked style.
2. **The channel-assignment prompt** — "use the composition from image 1, keep the character as
   character 1" — composition, identity and style each pinned to a named reference.
3. **The location angle bank** — every location gets at least two angles built before shooting, so
   reverses exist.
4. **CCR ordering** — Camera, Character, Rig — the house sentence order for a motion prompt.
5. **The 15-second voice bed** — a clip of the target voice (ideally one per emotional register)
   carried as a castable asset and referenced into every generation.
6. **The rolling reference cut** — edit ~15 seconds of approved footage and feed it forward as the
   reference for the next generation; pack it with one-second context chunks when you need breadth
   instead of continuity.
7. **The parallel bake-off** — one image, one prompt, four models at once, judged on performance
   and consistency.
8. **Board above, plates below** — a flat canvas laid out so the intended frame and the delivered
   frame are always adjacent to the eye.
9. **Easiest shot first** — seed the reference chain with the most controllable beat in the scene.
10. **Salvage pass** — before rejecting a generation, cut it for selects; half a second counts.

## Voice profile

Warm, plainspoken, unhurried American instructor. Sentence connectives are *"So,"* *"Now,"*
*"Okay, so"*, and *"for our example"* opens nearly every demonstration. He narrates his own
uncertainty rather than hiding it — *"I don't know why it's covering up his face"*, *"I'm not
entirely sure what's going on there"*, *"I have this nagging theory"* — and grades his own results
honestly and unglamorously: *"not too bad"*, *"it's not perfect"*, *"pretty darn close"*, *"okay,
that's actually pretty good."* Jokes are dry and self-puncturing (*"Salvador Dalí would be very
happy there"*; *"Truly nobody, but he has a mustache and a YouTube channel"*). He volunteers
non-sponsorship unprompted — *"I'm not sponsored by Freepik, nor have I ever been. I just really
like their tool"* [FILM26] 10:45; *"we're not paid by any tools to say anything"* [CINE26] 01:46.
Failure is always framed as procedure, never as defeat: *"it's entirely a part of the process"*,
*"it's a back-and-forth conversation with the AI tool."* He gives away the method and sells the
depth: every video ends by pointing at a course for the part he didn't cover.

**Register for this skill's outputs:** instructional, second-person, sequential, honest about cost
and about what does not work yet. Never hype. Never "revolutionary." When a step is optional, say
who it's optional for.
