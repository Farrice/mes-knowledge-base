# Source Quotes — Sean's Voice Fingerprints

Direct quotations from "How The Gurus (Almost) Killed Copywriting" (Sean Macintyre × Matthew Volkwyn, 2026). Use these for voice calibration when writing in Sean's mode, for citation in workflows that reference his specific framings, and for the "Source Quote Bank" callouts in workflows. Use sparingly — the quotes are seasoning, not substance.

## The Mechanism Doctrine

> "You don't come up with a mechanism. You go and do research on the product to find the unique selling points. You do interviews with the customers. You ask the CEO what their vision is for the brand and why they did what they did. You find that unique thing within what's already happening."

> "If you come up with it out of thin air, the second people start digging, they don't find any substance and then they're like — their ad skepticism. But if the mechanism or the unique selling point is actually in reality, the more they dig, the more they discover that it is the thing."

> "This is the perfect way to identify a copy guru — if you do digging on their mechanism, do you find substance? Or does it just end up being this fluff or this hot air?"

## The Awareness-Armor Frame

> "[Hormozi's] books are great if you are selling something that people already want. They are not great if you're reaching somebody who doesn't realize they have a problem, or maybe who is just on the cusp of realizing they have a problem, or maybe who realizes they have a problem but have been advertised so much about how to solve that problem that they've become inured to advertising and to enticements."

> "Most people walk through life with an armor of apathy, ignoring the advertisements around them."

> "There's nothing in [Hormozi's] books that really helps you reach an audience that has an armor around them — either the armor of defense or the armor of apathy."

## The Three-Vector Idea

> "When an idea hits you like that, you feel it. It's emotional. It's intellectually compelling. It's personally persuasive. But that only gets you through the first five pages of a 70-page promo."

> "[Michael Ford's idea] — somebody who was retiring when this crash happens would never live to see another bull market. Oh, chills."

## The Litotes / Classical Rhetoric Move

> "The best line of copy I saw recently in BizOp was — when I say best line, I don't mean effective line. I just mean the most entertaining line was: 'You know, it's really great. I literally just turn it on in the morning. It pays me like $3,000, $5,000 or whatever. And when I'm finished, I turn it off. Not bad. Not bad for zero hours of work.' That is from an old Agora promo."

> "That's psychologically doing something very interesting there because you are of course framing something very big but you're doing something — I think the Greek the rhetorical term for this is litotes. That's rhetorical understatement. We've been doing this for thousands of years, man. So, it's not it's not some innovation from a copy coach guru."

> "Most of the best bullets in that promo, they were all the headlines of previously winning promos."

## Adherence / Discipline-Motivation-Habit

> "Imagine there's like a y-axis and sort of like halfway up the y-axis is a line where they actually do shit to advance towards a goal. The only way you're going to get somebody from zero awareness of that thing to remotely close to doing something about the thing is to inspire them at first. But the thing with inspiration is — it's a very fleeting emotion. Inspiration does not last."

> "Then there's the dip below adherence — that adherence line where you actually do stuff. And you need to use discipline to bring yourself back up above the adherence line to where you're doing it again."

> "Once you have the habit, then you don't really need motivation to start working on your copy. You don't really need discipline to get you in the chair to write copy. You just kind of wake up and do it. It's just a thing that you turn on. It's as simple as like pissing, having sex, or eating."

> "It's only for some people that habit eventually transmogriies into passion. Very few people are passionate about writing ads for dick pills. But the people who are passionate about it [become great]."

## Hedonic Treadmill / Death of Desire

> "Every time you inspire yourself about a particular topic, diminishing returns. The human brain is pretty good at keeping you at baseline. And over time, the hedonic treadmill kicks in and your ability to feel inspired about something, especially work, it diminishes."

> "Humans are desiring machines. We only eat because we yearn to eat. We only drink because we yearn to drink. The moment that you stop desiring something is the moment that you die. The death of desire is death."

> "It doesn't matter if it's a Tangelo orange or a Rolex watch — you will always be able to take advantage of the fact and exploit the fact that human brains will never be truly happy with what they have."

## Local-Maxima Career Frame

> "You at a local maxima cannot know if you've reached a global maxima. The only way to do is to explore. And if you are at a local maxima, all that you can do is exploit it until you realize that's all you're going to get from this particular plateau of your life. Unless you leave the plateau and go looking for some larger global maxima — which by the way you may never find, because you may have found the global maxima of your life. Which is a scary thought."

> "Most entrepreneurs and most copywriters — we get into the profession because we wanted something more."

> "The goalposts will always move no matter how far you keep improving."

## Cross-Pollination / Anti-Mastermind

> "I'm at the point in my career where I'll spend $5,000 on a mastermind and you know what my goal is going to these — I really hope to fuck that I learn one thing that I can apply from this. So I'm willing to spend 20, $30,000 on something if I learn one thing because of the diminishing returns."

> "Hear me out here. What if I just don't spend that and go read a goddamn book? Just go to the library, man. Pick a book at random and see — go into it with the frame of like 'I have this copywriting project in mind. What can this teach me about that at the level of the word, the level of the sentence choice, the level of structure, the level of the way the ideas are presented or even the ideas themselves?'"

> "David Deutsch right now is not paying for masterminds at this point in his career. He's not paying for one-on-one coaching as far as I know. David is looking to music and seeing, okay, what can I learn from this field that I can apply to my copy to make it better?"

> "If you adopt that perspective and rather than look for people with these insights and start looking instead for cross-pollination — one, you won't have to spend 10% of your income, and two, you will have an infinite source of novelty in your life, an infinite source of fulfillment, because learning is one of the very few sustainable pleasures in life."

## Mark Ford / Never Retire

> "Mark Ford. He still writes every day. He still reads every day. At 75, he still works 10 to 12 hours per day. At 75, he never plans to retire again. Because the three times that he's tried to retire, the moment he does — the moment he stops the work — he feels his mind start to dull. He feels disconnected from the things that gave him sustainable pleasure. He feels alienated from the insights, the learning, the growth that motivated him. Again, the death of the desire is the death of the self."
