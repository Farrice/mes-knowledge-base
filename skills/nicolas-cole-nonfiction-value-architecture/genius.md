# Nicolas Cole Nonfiction Value Architecture - Genius Context

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

The ten value modes, spotlight discipline, and specificity ladder are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Internalize them, then produce originally. If the output visibly stamps "Value Mode 1, Value Mode 2..." or narrates "per the Ten Value Modes framework," it has failed. The test: would Nicolas Cole recognize this as a writer who actually decided what they were giving the reader before drafting — or would he recognize this as someone reciting his "10 magical ways to expand anything" vocabulary without doing the underlying list-level work?

Specifically:
- Do NOT enumerate which value mode, spotlight decision, or specificity level was applied unless asked. Do the decision; never announce it.
- Do NOT label sections "here's the value spotlight" or "here's the specificity ladder." Cole's own teaching voice is casual and interactive — a livestream teaching format with "drop in the chat" prompts to his audience, not a framework recital — and the finished piece should read the same way: plain, direct, un-outlined.
- His calibration line for weak writing is not a vibe check, it is a mechanical test: can the reader "imagine it," "feel it," "measure it"? A claim that survives without a literal moment or a countable unit has not cleared his bar, no matter how polished the sentence is.
- Polish-is-the-tell warning: a piece that sounds finished but still traffics in bundled words (love, push, accountability) has failed the only test Cole actually applies. Fluent bundled prose is worse than rough specific prose, because fluency disguises the missing decision.

## Core Operating Question

Before drafting, ask: **what am I giving the reader?**

Not "what am I writing about?" Not "how do I make this sound good?" The value decision comes first.

## The Ten Nonfiction Value Modes

| Mode | Reader Receives | Use When |
|---|---|---|
| Tips | Practical advice | Reader needs what to do |
| Stats | Data or quantified proof | Reader needs why the claim matters |
| Steps | Sequence | Reader needs order of operations |
| Lessons | Future-pulled insight | Reader should learn before paying the cost |
| Benefits | Motivating upside | Reader needs desire or buy-in |
| Reasons | Logic for action | Reader needs conviction |
| Mistakes | What to avoid | Reader needs risk prevention |
| Examples | Pattern recognition | Reader needs to see the thing in the wild |
| Questions | Self-diagnostic prompts | Reader needs to think better |
| Story | Confidence/proof | Reader needs "this is possible for me" |

## Genius Patterns

### 1. Value Before Prose
Cole places the value decision before the draft. Sentence structure, adjective choice, tone, and style all come second to the basic question: what is the reader getting? In his own words: "That is the core decision before you get into the writing, before you start debating sentence structure, before you start picking different adjectives, before you decide whether the writing is good or bad." He puts a number on how much of the outcome this decision controls: "80% of the value and 80% of the decision before you even start writing is here."

### 2. Spotlight Discipline
A piece can use multiple value modes, but one mode must be in the spotlight. If the title promises tips, the subheads need useful tips. If it promises mistakes, the subheads need specific mistakes. Cole states this as a hard rule, not a preference: "one of them has to be in the spotlight."

### 3. List-Level Architecture
The outline is the first proof of value. If the primary value is tips, list the tips before drafting. If the primary value is reasons, list the reasons. The writer does not need a long draft to find the value. Cole names this as the second decision after spotlight: "Then you go decision number two. what's my list of that thing?" — and he is explicit that skipping straight to prose is wasted motion: "I do not need to go spend 4 hours writing 3,000 words in order to decide what am I giving the reader."

### 4. Specificity as Proficiency
Writing skill is not a mystery gap between bad and genius. It is the writer's ability to speak with increasing specificity, objectivity, actionability, and instruction. Cole grounds this claim in scale, not theory: "I have mentored and trained north of probably 15,000 writers at this point. I will tell you that 99% of the time when I look at and evaluate someone else's writing, this is what I see" — meaning the same two failures, repeated at volume.

### 5. Bundled Term Detection
Weak nonfiction hides inside bundled words: love, push, discipline, mindset, accountability, consistency, trust, authenticity, authority. A bundled word names a category but does not tell the reader what to do. Cole's own worked example of a level-one failure: "the first tip is you got to love your kids. And the second tip is you got to push them. And the third tip is you got to hold them accountable." His diagnosis, verbatim: "these are bundled terms."

### 6. Moment Translation
The fastest upgrade is to turn a broad claim into a literal moment. "Love your kids" becomes "no phones at the dinner table; give them full attention."

### 7. Measurable Helpfulness
Specific writing lets the reader measure it. If the reader can count nights without phones at the dinner table, the advice became more objective. Cole's own version of the test: "How many nights a week did you allow phones at the dinner table? That is measurable."

### 8. Skim-First Delivery
Online readers skim first. They check title and subheads to decide whether the writer delivers the promised value. Subheads are value receipts. Cole puts a time box on the decision — "this happens within 4 seconds" — and names exactly what the subheads are doing in that window: "The subheads tell me how good the writer is at delivering on the thing that they're promising."

### 9. Same Idea, Higher Resolution
Two pieces can say the same thing. The useful one says it at a higher resolution: more concrete, more objective, more actionable, and more instructional. Cole's own framing: "They might be saying the same things but they're saying them in different levels of specificity."

### 10. Secondary Value Stacking
Once the primary value is clear, other modes can deepen it. A tip can be supported by a stat, made easier with steps, clarified with an example, challenged with a question, and made credible with a story. Cole walks through this live: "Could I also include steps if I wanted to?... But one of them has to be in the spotlight."

## Hidden Knowledge

1. The list is the first product; prose is packaging.
2. A vague outline cannot become a valuable draft through polish alone.
3. The body does not rescue weak subheads for skim-first readers.
4. Most nonfiction is not useless because it is false. It is useless because it is too bundled.
5. A "good idea" is not yet reader value. Reader value requires a deliverable.
6. The writer's first job is not expression. It is exchange.

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### High Agency Kids
Weak: "Love your kids. Push them. Hold them accountable."

Stronger: "No phones at the dinner table. When your child fails, do not give them an excuse to quit; require one more attempt. When your child breaks a promise, create a consequence tied to the action."

Why it works: the same high-level idea becomes visible, measurable, and executable.

### The Four-Second Reader
A reader sees "Tips to raise high agency kids," clicks, and skims subheads. If the subheads are generic, they decide the writer has not given much. If the subheads are specific, they keep reading.

### The Value Spotlight
Before writing one newsletter, choose the mode in the spotlight. Tips, mistakes, reasons, examples, or questions each creates a different piece. The choice prevents meandering opinion — Cole's own description of what happens without it: "the first decision is which one is in the spotlight?"

## Signature Moves

- Name the promised value mode.
- List the exact deliverables before drafting.
- Circle every bundled term.
- Ask "what literal moment proves this?"
- Add a measurable or observable standard.
- Rewrite subheads so they deliver the title promise.
- Add secondary modes only to support the spotlight.
- Run the skim test before sentence polish.

## Quality Gate

A Cole-grade nonfiction piece passes these checks:

1. The title makes a clear value promise.
2. One value mode is clearly in the spotlight.
3. The outline lists the exact deliverables.
4. Every subhead proves the title promise.
5. Bundled terms have been unbundled into moments or actions.
6. Advice is specific enough to imagine.
7. At least one key point is measurable or observable.
8. Examples, questions, stats, or stories support the primary value instead of adding clutter.
9. A skim reader can see value in four seconds.
10. Sentence craft happens after value architecture, not before.

## Anti-Patterns (Sourced from the 2026-05-05 Transcript)

A piece fails this list the same way Cole's level-one students fail: would Nicolas Cole recognize this as his own value-decision process, or would he recognize this as a writer wearing "ten value modes" vocabulary without doing the list-level work first?

- **Bundled abstractions posing as tips.** Cole's own worked example of the failure: "the first tip is you got to love your kids. And the second tip is you got to push them. And the third tip is you got to hold them accountable." — his level-one beginner example, not a paraphrase (Nicolas Cole, "The Framework I Use To Make Any Nonfiction Piece More Valuable," YouTube, 2026-05-05 transcript).
- **Telling the reader something they already assume.** Cole names this as the first of the two most common writing failures he sees across "15,000 writers" he has mentored: "I didn't tell you anything you didn't already know" (2026-05-05 transcript).
- **Drafting before deciding the value mode.** Cole rejects writing a full draft to discover what a piece is about: "I do not need to go spend 4 hours writing 3,000 words in order to decide what am I giving the reader" (2026-05-05 transcript).
- **Meandering, opinion-only pieces with no value spotlight.** Cole's diagnosis of why so much nonfiction fails: it reads like "meandering, you know, long opinions with no like where are we going" because the writer skipped the spotlight decision (2026-05-05 transcript).
- **Generic subheads that don't deliver the title's promise.** Skim-first readers judge fast: "The subheads tell me how good the writer is at delivering on the thing that they're promising" — and a generic subhead fails inside the window Cole names directly: "this happens within 4 seconds" (2026-05-05 transcript).
- **Stacking multiple value modes with none in the spotlight.** Cole allows secondary modes but is explicit that "one of them has to be in the spotlight" — a piece with five value modes and no primary is not stacked, it is unfocused (2026-05-05 transcript).
