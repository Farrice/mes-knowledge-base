# Chris Do — Genius Context

The tacit operating system behind Chris Do's teaching. He is not a "tips" educator; he is a *reframer* — every stuck situation (price resistance, faceless competition, sales dread, "I'm not creative") gets its frame moved until the same facts mean something different. He teaches by Socratic interrogation of live audiences, prices to filter rather than to bill, and treats self-acceptance as the prerequisite infrastructure of any brand. Load this before producing any expert-domain output so the work *thinks* like him rather than merely quoting frameworks.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Reframing as the Master Move (move the frame, change the meaning)
Everything Chris teaches reduces to one operation: change the frame around unchanged facts. Paint spilled on canvas is garbage; the same canvas in a gallery behind velvet rope is art. Listerine's "strong medicinal burn" becomes Scope's "medicine breath." His own $5,000/hr price isn't a fee — it's a frame that makes his $10K course look cheap. He never fights the fact; he relocates it.
- **Execute:** When a client is stuck, list the "facts" they believe are fixed (price, size, inexperience, competitor dominance) and reframe each one before proposing any tactic. Ask: "In what frame is this exact fact an advantage?"
- **Success Metric:** The deliverable changes the *meaning* of at least one thing the client thought was immovable — without changing the thing itself.

### Pattern: Pick a Fight You Can Win (anti-positioning against the giant)
Positioning without a named enemy is decoration. Chris's sequence: identify who the buyer is *actually* considering (not aspirational competitors), name the top 3 traits that competitor is known for, then be the deliberate anti. The dirty-but-legal move is guerrilla reframing — restate the giant's strength so it reads as a weakness (Scope vs. Listerine, 7UP "The Uncola," Mac vs. PC). You force the buyer to make a choice, and a forced choice is a position.
- **Execute:** Never write positioning in a vacuum. Require the real competitor set first, run the strength→weakness inversion on the dominant player, and end with an explicit either/or the customer must choose between ("big faceless firm, or someone who worries about your payroll?").
- **Success Metric:** The positioning statement names (or unmistakably implies) the competitor and converts their #1 strength into a liability.

### Pattern: Price as Positioning Instrument (the ladder, not the invoice)
Chris raised his hourly rate $250→$500→$750→$1,500→$5,000 and each raise had a *strategic* function, not a billing one: stop the overload, filter for believers, and reposition everything below it on the value ladder. High price changes buyer behavior — people who pay a lot *act* on advice ("say jump the fence, they jump"). The calibration test is emotional, not financial: charge enough that the Friday-6pm phone call makes you happy, enough that scope requests stop mattering.
- **Execute:** Set price top-down from the position you want to occupy, not bottom-up from costs or hours. Check every price against (1) who it filters in/out and (2) what it makes the rest of the product ladder look like. If the client isn't "worth it" yet, keep the number and close the gap with skill/service.
- **Success Metric:** The price has a stated positioning function (filter/anchor/reposition) and passes the Friday-6pm test; no time sheets, outcomes only.

### Pattern: Sales as Change Management (diagnose the gap, never pitch)
Chris's entire sales system is one diagram: current state → obstacle → desired future state. The seller's job is to map that gap with questions, because "whoever asks more questions is in control of the relationship." The SALES acronym (Serve, Ask, Listen, Empathize, Summarize) replaces pitching entirely — you end by *summarizing* their situation so accurately that hiring you feels like their own conclusion. Three pressure-releases make it kind: the inbound prospect is already qualified (stop proving yourself), they always have three options (you / someone else / nothing), and your goal is align-or-decline, including "I don't think you need to do anything."
- **Execute:** Enter every sales conversation with zero pitch and a question architecture: what do they want, what's in the way, what has it cost them, what happens if nothing changes, what would solving it be worth *to them*? Close by summarizing the gap in their words, then let them choose among the three options honestly.
- **Success Metric:** The prospect talks more than you; the conversation ends with a stated gap, a proportionate budget anchor, and an honest align-or-decline verdict — no urgency theater.

### Pattern: Hunt Bigger Problems, Not Better Pitches
"How much they spend is proportionate to the size of the problem — relative to the buyer." Chris's ceiling at $200K+ projects broke not by selling harder but by realizing the competition and the buyer had changed (Keir McLaren: "you're using a minor league strategy in the Major League") — which doubled revenue in a year and took close rates from ~20% to ~75%. Small problems have small budgets no matter how good the craft is.
- **Execute:** Before improving any offer or pitch, re-target: whose version of this problem is a $3M problem instead of a $3K one? Reprice and re-position for that buyer. When a client outgrows a level, audit which "minor league" habits (proving, over-explaining, feature-selling) must be retired.
- **Success Metric:** The offer is aimed at a named buyer for whom the fee is obviously small relative to the problem ("$10K/month ain't nothing, please take care of my business").

### Pattern: Know → Accept → Express (the personal-brand stack runs on self-acceptance)
Chris refuses to treat personal branding as a content problem. The sequence is: know who you are, accept/love that person (he explicitly prescribes therapy — "the paint is dry" otherwise), then express it publicly. Skipping to expression produces the locked-jaw camera energy that tells viewers "I'm not comfortable, please don't watch." Self-labels are load-bearing: "your words shape your world," so "I'm not creative / not funny / not interesting" is self-sabotage dressed as humility, and asking "what's the exact next step?" is usually a stalling tactic.
- **Execute:** Diagnose which layer is actually blocking (knowing, accepting, or expressing) before prescribing content tactics. Ban violent self-language in the brief; replace step-obsession with "do the first step, see how it feels." Progress over perfection.
- **Success Metric:** The person ships imperfect authentic content on a cadence, and the plan addresses the acceptance layer explicitly (not just posting mechanics).

### Pattern: Lean Into the Weakness (asymmetric brand warfare)
The thing you hide is usually your best material. Johnny Cupcakes' book title — "Short stories from a short guy about short-sleeve T-shirts" — only works *because* he's short; "short stories from a 6'2" guy" is nothing. Chris generalizes it from Sun Tzu / guerrilla warfare: make your weakness your strength and your enemy's strength their weakness, because you cannot beat the bigger player by doing what they do with fewer resources. Old = seasoned. Small = personal. Weird = memorable.
- **Execute:** Inventory what the client is embarrassed by or hiding (age, size, accent, lack of credentials, niche obsession). Pick the one their biggest competitor *cannot* copy and build the hook, bio line, or campaign on it.
- **Success Metric:** The brand asset produced would NOT work if the "weakness" were removed — that's the proof it's a moat and not a gimmick.

### Pattern: Creativity Is Remix, One Tweak Deep
Chris dissolves "I'm not creative" with evidence: creativity is taking what's known and making one small tweak. Apple's copy is familiar phrases with one word swapped ("No pain. All gain."); Danger Mouse = White Album + Black Album; Waterworld got greenlit as "Road Warrior on water." Ken Robinson's frame: we don't grow into creativity, we grow *out* of it — so the job is remembering, not learning. And originality-worship is usually "a lack of history": don't reinvent the wheel, reinvent how it's used.
- **Execute:** When generating names, hooks, copy, or campaign ideas, start from a familiar reference (phrase, format, genre) and change exactly one element. For "non-creatives," assign remix exercises before blank-page exercises. Study Apple product pages as the masterclass in tweaked-familiar copy.
- **Success Metric:** Every "creative" output can name its source material and its single tweak; the blank-page excuse never blocks shipping.

### Pattern: The Marketing Curriculum (teach your way to the sale)
Chris operationalizes Godin's permission marketing as an educator: design content like a curriculum — broad, exciting, general material at the top; deeper, more detailed, higher-value material as the relationship deepens — so the selling happens invisibly throughout the journey. The three-question gate for every piece: is it anticipated, personal, relevant? And the journey doesn't end at conversion: Awareness → Interest → Desire → Conversion → **Delight**, because it's hard to get a client and easy to keep one, and Delight is what makes them tell their business friends.
- **Execute:** Map content/offers to curriculum depth levels before writing anything. Front-load generosity (help them get what *they* want), sequence value so each level earns permission for the next, and design an explicit post-sale Delight moment worth talking about.
- **Success Metric:** Content passes anticipated/personal/relevant; the funnel has a designed Delight stage that generates referrals without asking.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: Confidence is downstream of permission, and "they" doesn't exist
When people say they lack confidence, Chris hears something more specific: they're waiting for someone to give them permission. His own pricing breakthrough was literally a friend saying "if you charge less than $500/hr I won't respect you" — "nothing real or tangible about it, just some fool says go ahead." And the industry authority people cite ("they say you can't do that") can never be named: "Who's the they? You see that person every day in the mirror."
- **Deploy:** When a client stalls on pricing, niching, or publishing, don't argue the economics — identify whose permission they're waiting for, then have them name "the they." Grant permission explicitly and immediately ("decide and you will be"); it works because that's all that was missing.

### Insight: Scarcity and friction are premium signals, not sales obstacles
Everyone in professional services believes they win by being easy to work with. Chris inverts it: you command a premium by making it *harder* — scarcity, waitlists, high prices — because friction signals "I'm worth it, I'll wait in line." Ease signals commodity.
- **Deploy:** When positioning a premium offer, audit for excessive ease (instant availability, negotiable everything, unlimited access) and deliberately add legitimate friction: limited slots, application steps, a price that makes people pause. Never manufacture fake scarcity — reduce actual availability by raising the value of the yes.

### Insight: The buyer's belief is bought with the price, not despite it
The counterintuitive mechanic inside the pricing ladder: expensive clients are *easier* — "when you charge so much money you attract a different kind of buyer, a buyer who believes in you," and because the conversation cost so much, they execute the advice instead of debating it. Cheap clients relitigate everything; premium clients jump the fence when you say jump.
- **Deploy:** When a client complains about difficult, micromanaging customers, check price first — difficulty is often a symptom of underpricing (non-believers filtered in). The prescription is a raise, not a boundaries workshop.

### Insight: Future-first, data-second (conviction is the currency)
From the narrative work with Michael Margolis that Chris co-signs: data is a story of the past; disruption is a story about the future. Most organizations sequence it backwards — leading with research to *acquire* conviction, which is outsourcing belief. The functioning order is: stake in the ground (thesis, conviction) → then research to test and legitimize it. Research without existing conviction "rots on the vine" no matter how good it is; and those who tell the stories rule the world, but almost nobody believes they have permission to tell the bigger story.
- **Deploy:** For any strategy, rebrand, or innovation deliverable, write the future-state thesis FIRST as a conviction statement, then assemble data as legitimizing evidence — never open a deck with the research. Index to the most senior conviction-holder in the system; if no one with power holds the belief, fix that before commissioning research.

### Insight: The story attached is the value (the Zippo ladder)
Same hunk of metal: a 2022 Zippo is $15, a WWII Zippo is $200, General Marshall's D-Day Zippo with the photograph is priceless. Nothing about the object changed — the story attached to it changed, and the buyer's *own* story about what owning it means. Providence (story of place/origin) is why champagne, Kobe beef, and New York pizza command premiums. This is the mechanism under all premium pricing: you can't out-craft commodification, but "the only thing nobody can compete against is your story."
- **Deploy:** When a client's offer is being commoditized, don't add features — add attached story: origin, providence, who it was made by/for, what owning it says about the buyer. Price the story-laden version against the naked version deliberately far apart.

### Insight: Strategy must survive street language (the sandbox test)
From the Mark Pollard conversations Chris hosts and teaches from: corporate strategy dies of vocabulary — "ridiculous language nobody would use on the streets." If the strategy can't trigger an emotional reaction inside the team, it won't trigger one in public. The proof of a real strategy is compression: an organizing idea sayable in three words (two topics colliding — "Knicks = anger therapy") that feels small from outside and enormous once you're working inside it. Divergent idea-generation is the cheap part; convergence — killing options into one informed opinion about how to win — is what's actually scarce and paid for.
- **Deploy:** After drafting any strategy, run the compression pass: state it in ≤3 plain words, check it produces a felt reaction, and verify tactics/design decisions flow from it without further explanation. If it needs the deck to make sense, it isn't a strategy yet.

### Insight: Story transcends the algorithm
Chris on why he ignores platform mechanics: "I just write a story, and I don't really care what the algorithm is doing... story transcends all, and you can layer the hack of the day on top." The foundation of every post is a compelling story; formats, hooks, and platform tricks are amplifiers of a story that already works, not substitutes for one.
- **Deploy:** In any content engagement, sequence story-quality before format-selection. Reject briefs that start with "what's working on the algorithm right now"; start with "what's the story only you can tell," then choose the format that carries it.
