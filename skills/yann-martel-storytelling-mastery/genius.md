# Yann Martel Genius Context

> "It's not about praise. Art is a gift." — Yann Martel, on releasing finished work to readers (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript)

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb them, then build originally. If the output announces "here I withdraw detail for co-creation" or stamps "Envelope 1, Envelope 2, Envelope 3" in visible order, you have failed. The test: would Yann Martel recognize this as his own withdrawal-and-mystery method, or would it read as someone using storytelling vocabulary without doing the actual subtraction? If it's the second, rebuild.

Specifically:
- Do NOT narrate the machinery. Martel never says "I am now creating allegorical distance" — he just picks the animal and lets it carry the weight. Execute the move; never announce it.
- Do NOT over-describe to prove effort. Martel's own rule: "If you emphasize anything about someone's nose, you suddenly imagine this enormous nose... It's a caricature." Polish that reads as exhaustive specificity is the tell that the reader's imagination has been shut out, not invited in.
- Withdrawal is the craft, not decoration. His texture is analog and physical (research cut into paper fragments, sorted into Manila envelopes) before it is ever digital or clever. Keep the delivery grounded in gathered material, never invented from a blank page.
- Endings, not openings, are where he spends his care. "The words that have been coming to mind for me are satisfying and mysterious." An ending that answers everything or an ending that answers nothing are both failures — the target is closure with one glowing question left alive.
- Exclamation points, adjective-stacking, and clever formatting are self-indulgence tells. He calls the exclamation mark "highly manipulative" and reports his own editor calling a formatting experiment self-indulgent and "just annoying" — and he cut it. If the model reaches for a typographic flourish to prove craft, that is the moment to stop and ask what the reader is being denied room to do.

## Core Philosophy
Martel's craft treats story as a human exchange, not a delivery mechanism. The writer prepares deeply, arranges material carefully, and then withdraws enough control for the reader to co-create. The best story gives closure without full possession: the reader feels guided, then released with a living question. In his own words: "The more you withdraw, the more the reader, the viewer has to come halfway."

## Master Method

### 1. Gather Before Drafting
Collect research, words, facts, images, scene flashes, structural ideas, and ending signals. Do not rank too early. The raw mass matters because unexpected details later become doors. For `Life of Pi`, roughly 400 pages of research notes accumulated across about two and a half years before a single scene was drafted — down to single collected words, like the Indian-English word "bamboozle," kept because it might one day belong to a character's voice.

### 2. Turn Research Into Envelopes
Break the mass into fragments. Sort each fragment by story function:
- scene
- fact
- language
- symbol
- character pressure
- question
- ending

The envelope is a thinking tool. It prevents blank-page panic and forces material to take shape. Martel's own system was literal: purely factual fragments (his example: "Turtles of the Pacific") went into their own labeled Manila envelope, separate from the chronological envelopes that became chapter backbones.

### 3. Know the Destination
Martel does not worship beginnings. He cares more about where the reader lands. Before drafting, name the final feeling, the final unresolved question, and the final proof that the journey meant something. His own description of what he is chasing at the close: "The words that have been coming to mind for me are satisfying and mysterious."

### 4. Draft With Local Freedom
The plan is a departure point. During drafting, fragments can move, shrink, grow, or disappear. The writer is prepared enough to improvise — but improvisation still answers to craft handrails, like his sentence-length rule: "don't have sentences that are the same length one after the other. You don't want to have 17, 10 word sentences in a row. That gets boring."

### 5. Make the Reader Work
Books beat passive media by giving the reader space. Withhold enough description, explanation, and emotional labeling that the reader must supply inner images and meaning. Martel's own line on the trade: "what you don't want to do is write it out too much that it leaves nothing to the imagination of the reader."

### 6. Use Allegorical Distance
Animals, masks, footnotes, altered forms, and strange containers let the writer approach heavy subjects without dullness or sermonizing. Martel is explicit that the choice is functional, not decorative: "I just chosen for what they might symbolize" — the animal or mask has to earn its meaning, the same way animals work in classic "vehicles for human thought."

### 7. Score the Prose
Punctuation is not grammar housekeeping. It is rhythm, breath, emphasis, acceleration, and pause. Martel treats the exclamation mark as the most dangerous mark in the kit: "you got to be really sparing with the exclamation mark because it's highly manipulative."

### 8. End With Resolved Mystery
The ending must complete the reader's core expectation but not answer the work into death. A good ending satisfies and still glows. Martel's own cautionary case, on a crime plot left dangling in an adaptation: "How can you not address the question of who killed the little boy?" — proof that unresolved is only "mystery" when the story has earned it; otherwise it reads as a hole.

## Signature Moves

### Sort the Hodgepodge
Before writing, turn research into discrete fragments and assign each to a job — Martel's own `Life of Pi` research ran to roughly 400 pages before any of it became a scene.

### Protect the Reader's Imagination
Remove surplus description and emotional instruction. Keep vivid cues; delete controlling explanations. "What you don't want to do is write it out too much that it leaves nothing to the imagination of the reader."

### Let Animals Carry Human Weight
Use animals when direct human representation would trigger prejudice, fatigue, or excessive heaviness. Martel's rule for the choice itself: pick the animal for "what they might symbolize," in the tradition of animals as "vehicles for human thought" — never for surface cuteness.

### Keep the Character Open
When the subject is the pressure, make the character ordinary enough for reader entry. Martel is blunt about the cost of over-specifying: "That's why I never describe my characters... If you emphasize anything about someone's nose, you suddenly imagine this enormous nose... It's a caricature."

### Elevate a Humble Form
Footnotes, envelopes, chapter counts, reports, and other practical forms can become story architecture. Martel's own discovery moment, on turning footnotes into a narrative device in `Son of Nobody`: "Hey, that's a great idea. I could elevate the humble footnote."

### Break One Rule at a Time
Formal experiment works when the reader still has handrails — and sometimes even the author's favorite experiment fails that test. In `Life of Pi`, Martel wanted a lifeboat dialogue scene (Pi and a blind Frenchman) to float around the page instead of running flush left; his editor killed it with a blunt verdict: "it's just you masturbating on the page thinking you're really clever. It's just annoying." He kept the dialogue flush left after that.

### Release the Gift
After full effort, the work belongs partly to readers. Their interpretation is not a bug. "It's not about praise. Art is a gift... you don't give a gift wanting a gift in return."

## Anti-Patterns
- **Over-Supplied Story**: Describes, explains, and resolves until the reader has no role — Martel, transcript: "what you don't want to do is write it out too much that it leaves nothing to the imagination of the reader" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).
- **Clever Formatting Without Need**: Experimental layout that advertises author cleverness instead of serving story — Martel's own editor, transcript: "it's just you masturbating on the page thinking you're really clever. It's just annoying" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript, on the cut `Life of Pi` lifeboat margin-dialogue experiment).
- **Fact Hoarding**: Research displayed because it was collected, not because it earns story value — Martel, transcript: "you have the facts of the story, you have the research, you have the scenes, but then as you write the book, it kind of elevates into a story" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).
- **Symbol as Costume**: Animal or allegorical figure chosen decoratively rather than functionally — Martel, transcript: "I just chosen for what they might symbolize" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).
- **Character Caricature**: Physical description so specific it becomes cartoonish — Martel, transcript: "If you emphasize anything about someone's nose, you suddenly imagine this enormous nose... It's a caricature" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).
- **Mystery as Fog**: Withholding clarity where the story actually needs coherence — Martel, transcript, on an unresolved subplot in an adaptation: "How can you not address the question of who killed the little boy?" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).
- **Genre Betrayal**: Violating the reader's core contract without giving a better contract — Martel, transcript: "in genre fiction, you want a confirmation of your expectations" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).
- **AI Sincerity Fraud**: Delegating intimate human feeling to a machine while pretending it came from the heart — Martel, transcript, on learning a personal message was AI-written: "I would have so much rather you written something that's half as sincere as that, filled and littered with typos, than to have AI write that. It felt like a violation and a betrayal" (extractions/yann-martel/transcript.txt, interview transcript).

## Recognition Test
Before shipping, ask: would Yann Martel recognize this as his own — a piece that was gathered, sorted, withdrawn from, and released — or does it read as someone using storytelling vocabulary (envelopes, allegory, co-creation) as decoration without doing the underlying subtraction? Two tells give away the second case: over-description that removes the reader's imaginative work, and formatting or symbolism chosen to look clever rather than to earn its function. If either tell is present, the output would not survive Martel's own editor's verdict — "it's just annoying" — and needs a withdrawal pass before it ships.

## Quality Gate
Before shipping any Martel-style output, answer:
1. What fragments did we gather before drafting?
2. What is the destination?
3. What does the reader get to imagine?
4. What has been withheld for good reason?
5. Does the chosen form carry meaning?
6. Does punctuation control tempo?
7. Which reader expectation is fulfilled?
8. Which mystery remains alive?
9. Does the output feel authored by a person?
