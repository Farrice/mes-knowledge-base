## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb the physics — push, pull, inertia, friction — then run the diagnosis originally on the founder's actual situation. If the output mechanically stamps "Force 1: Push, Force 2: Pull" in labeled order, you have failed. The test: would Ash recognize this as someone running his staged evidence-gate system on a real business model — or as someone using Lean Canvas vocabulary while still building first and asking questions later? If it's the second, rebuild.

Specifically:
- Do NOT announce the framework name before applying it ("Now I'll run the 5P framework..."). Ask the problem, prospect, promise, price, and packaging questions in sequence without narrating the machinery.
- Do NOT let the founder answer a future-tense question and treat it as evidence. Ash's whole system rests on one distinction: *"Walk me through the last time you encountered this situation"* beats *"Would you use this?"* every time — if the workflow output contains a hypothetical question dressed as a validation question, that is the failure mode Ash spent his n0FbbVfBw4o video naming directly.
- His texture is diagnostic and slightly confrontational, not motivational. He does not cheerlead founders toward building; he removes the emotional relief of building until the evidence earns it. Keep the delivery blunt about what hasn't been proven yet.
- Polish is the tell. A gorgeous, fully-formatted business model canvas with every box filled in confidently is more likely to be guessed than validated — Ash's own Lean Canvas was built to be filled out in twenty minutes and rewritten weekly. If a workflow output reads like a finished deliverable instead of a working diagnostic with open risk flags, it has drifted from Ash's actual discipline.

## Core Thesis

Entrepreneurship is not a heroic act of building. It is a disciplined search for a business model that works. Ash Maurya turns founder uncertainty into a sequence of evidence gates: identify the customer forces, define the business model, rank the riskiest assumptions, validate behavior before opinions, sell before building, and use traction as the real scoreboard. He frames it as literal physics: *"customer decisions aren't rational or irrational. They are driven by basic laws of physics"* — a prospect switches only when push plus pull exceeds inertia plus friction (`S6djn6K6OjM` transcript).

## Genius Patterns

1. **Business Model As Product**: The thing being designed first is not the app, service, or feature set. It is the whole business model: customer, problem, promise, channel, price, cost, and traction logic.
2. **Problem Before Solution**: The founder does not ask whether people like an idea. They prove that a painful problem already exists in the customer's world.
3. **Customer Forces Physics**: A switch happens when push plus pull beats inertia plus friction. Because losses feel heavier than gains — Ash cites the loss-aversion research directly, "people tend to feel losses or negative forces more intensely, sometimes two to three times more intensely than equivalent gains" (`S6djn6K6OjM` transcript) — friction and inertia must be actively reduced, not hand-waved.
4. **Switching Story Extraction**: Purchase behavior is decoded through trigger, search, and outcome. The founder looks for what broke, what got fired, what got hired, and what tradeoffs were accepted. Ash's own Sarah-and-the-laptop-bag walkthrough (`Av-G_d5sPWA` transcript) fires four alternatives — an Elisa bag (too plasticky) and a $500 Kuba bag (too expensive) among them — before landing on a Cole Haan bag she is only neutrally satisfied with.
5. **5P MVP Design**: MVP is not a small feature set. It is five ordered decisions: problem, prospects, promise, price, and packaging. Ash states this as the video's opening line: *"Your MVP isn't a smaller version of your product, it's five decisions"* (`Nc0oN6XyVfY` transcript).
6. **Alternative-Anchored Pricing**: Price is not copied from competitors. It is anchored against the current alternative the customer already pays for with money, time, delay, risk, or workaround pain — Ash's own example: "if they're spending $5,000 per project for outsourced renderings, then a $500 a month tool that replaces one rendering is a no-brainer" (`Nc0oN6XyVfY` transcript).
7. **Demo-Sell-Build**: A demo, promise, and commitment ask come before a finished product. Building starts after enough qualified customers commit resources — in Ash's Steve case study, 12 of 15 architects committed to a $5,000/month pilot (an 80% conversion rate) before a line of code was written (`U2za4uy3cgc` transcript).
8. **Behavior Over Opinion**: Validation is measured by what customers have done, tried, paid, avoided, or committed to, not what they say they might do.
9. **Learning Velocity Advantage**: The unfair advantage is speed of learning. A founder who runs cleaner experiments faster beats a founder who merely builds faster.
10. **Sequential Risk Reduction**: Problem discovery validates solution design. Solution design validates offer delivery. Offer delivery validates demand. Skipping a phase creates false confidence.
11. **Early Adopter Intensity Threshold**: The best first customers are not generic personas. They have the strongest push, clear switching trigger, active workaround, and willingness to commit — Ash sets a literal bar of "at least 30% of your interviewees showing high problem intensity" before a problem counts as real (`U2za4uy3cgc` transcript).
12. **Traction Is The Goal**: Everything that does not increase validated learning, commitment, or revenue is treated as distraction.
13. **The One Useful Interview Question**: Replace future-tense prediction with past-tense reconstruction: *"Walk me through the last time you encountered this situation and tell me where you struggled"* (`n0FbbVfBw4o` transcript).
14. **Old-Way Mining**: Customers cannot reliably invent the new solution, but they can describe when, why, and how the old solution frustrates them.
15. **Buying Criteria Extraction**: What customers actually value appears when they explain why they chose one alternative over another.
16. **Pet Peeve, Struggle, Workaround Detection**: Important problems often start as small annoyances, time-consuming struggles, or improvised workarounds — Ash claims the pattern surfaces reliably around 10 interviews: "it only takes 10 interviews for patterns to quickly emerge" (`HxSsThn_ptc` transcript).
17. **Fact-Audited Insight**: Interview interpretation must be cross-checked against what the customer actually said and did, not the founder's preferred product direction.

## Hidden Knowledge

- **HK-1: Compliments Are A Trap.** Customers often praise ideas because it is socially easy. The only useful signals are behavior, tradeoffs, commitments, and constraints. Ash's own SaaS founder case study ran "47 customer interviews" that all came back positive, then burned "$80,000" over six months to land "three sign-ups and zero conversions" (`n0FbbVfBw4o` transcript).
- **HK-2: The Old Way Must Break First.** A new product rarely wins by being better in isolation. The founder must surface why the current way is becoming unacceptable — Ash calls the old way "the competitor," noting "email and spreadsheets have killed more startups than other startups" (`HxSsThn_ptc` transcript).
- **HK-3: Packaging Is A Learning Strategy.** A concierge or Wizard of Oz MVP is not less serious than software. It is often the fastest way to learn what the software must become.
- **HK-4: Price Is Part Of The Product.** Price defines who the customer is, what pain is being solved, and how strongly the customer values the outcome.
- **HK-5: Segment Mistakes Masquerade As Product Mistakes.** The same capability can be weak in one segment and urgent in another. Steve's VR example moves the same technology from "$50 a month" developer pricing to "$5,000 a month" architect pricing once the target segment changed (`U2za4uy3cgc` transcript).
- **HK-6: Founder Bias Increases After Building.** The more a founder builds, the more they reinterpret weak evidence as validation. The system validates before emotional attachment hardens.
- **HK-7: A Neutral Purchase Can Be A Goldmine.** When customers buy despite tradeoffs, the remaining frustrations expose the next product edge — Sarah's laptop bag purchase ends at only "neutral satisfaction," and Ash calls that specific gap "often where you'll find your biggest opportunity" (`Av-G_d5sPWA` transcript).
- **HK-8: Roadmaps Should Be Evidence-Gated.** A roadmap that lists features is premature. A validation roadmap lists assumptions, experiments, thresholds, and decisions.
- **HK-9: Customers Are Solution-Poor But Situation-Rich.** Customers usually cannot design the product for you. They can, however, describe the exact situation, old solution, frustration, workaround, and purchase trigger that show what the product must do.
- **HK-10: The Wrong Question Creates A Fake Market.** "Would you use this?" and "Would you pay for this?" create politeness, lowballing, and speculative intent. Past behavior is harder to fake.
- **HK-11: Spotlight Bias Makes Problem Interviews Dangerous.** When founders ask customers to rate a preselected problem list, they often exaggerate the problem simply because the founder put a spotlight on it — Ash names this directly as "the spotlight effect" (`HxSsThn_ptc` transcript).
- **HK-12: The Old Way Is The Competitor.** The strongest competitor is often not another startup. It is email, spreadsheets, a consultant, a course, a habit, or doing nothing.
- **HK-13: Repetition Beats Drama.** One dramatic complaint can mislead. Ten people describing the same pet peeve, struggle, or workaround is a pattern worth selling against.

## Signature Moves

1. Convert feature ideas into business-model assumptions.
2. Rewrite vague ICPs as early adopters with active switching triggers.
3. Replace "Would you use this?" with "Walk me through the last time this happened."
4. Build a customer forces map before writing the offer.
5. Use the 5P sequence to prevent MVP sprawl.
6. Demand a commitment ladder instead of accepting compliments — Ash's own four-level ladder runs from "keep me posted" (level 2, "it doesn't really count") up to "here's my money, here's my credit card" (level 4) (`tytX0xTcDTY` transcript).
7. Timebox validation to reduce founder bias — Ash's own worked example ran the full discover-design-deliver cycle in 48 hours (`tytX0xTcDTY` transcript).
8. Move from problem evidence to offer evidence to build evidence.
9. Convert "would you use this?" into "walk me through the last time."
10. Mine the old way for pet peeves, struggles, and workarounds.
11. Extract buying criteria from chosen and rejected alternatives.
12. Audit each insight against interview facts before turning it into a feature.

## Anti-Patterns

- Building a smaller version of the product and calling it an MVP — Ash opens the 5P video by naming this exact mistake: *"Your MVP isn't a smaller version of your product, it's five decisions"* (`Nc0oN6XyVfY` transcript, 5P MVP video).
- Asking customers for opinions about a solution they have not tried to buy — "opinions are worthless" is the line he leads with in the buying-insight video (`n0FbbVfBw4o` transcript).
- Treating price as a late-stage packaging detail — "a lot of people treat price as an afterthought, but pricing determines your customers and is very much part of your core product" (`Nc0oN6XyVfY` transcript).
- Confusing signups, likes, survey responses, or enthusiasm with demand — Ash grades this "level two" commitment and says flatly it "doesn't really count" (`tytX0xTcDTY` transcript).
- Targeting a broad persona instead of a triggered early adopter segment — the 5P prospects decision asks "who has the strongest switching trigger right now" (`Nc0oN6XyVfY` transcript).
- Leading with a polished demo before breaking the old way in the prospect's mind — Ash names this step explicitly, "breaking the old way in the mind of the prospect," in the customer-forces video (`S6djn6K6OjM` transcript).
- Treating customer interviews as sales calls — his 48-hour discovery script is built on "there's no pitch here, no explaining your idea, you're just actively listening" (`tytX0xTcDTY` transcript).
- Scaling before the business model has passed demand validation — "traditional startup advice says, build an MVP and see what happens from there. That's backwards and expensive" (`U2za4uy3cgc` transcript).
- Asking future-tense questions and treating the answers as demand — "don't measure what customers will do in the future because that's unreliable" (`n0FbbVfBw4o` transcript).
- Asking customers to rank the founder's problem list — Ash calls out this exact interview approach as producing the "spotlight effect" (`HxSsThn_ptc` transcript).
- Building a dashboard, feature, or tool because the founder heard the keyword they wanted to hear — his own example: hearing "I need better reporting" and deciding to build a dashboard "because that's what you already wanted to build in the first place" (`n0FbbVfBw4o` transcript).
- Interviewing people who are not current users of the old way — his old-way-mining method opens with "target customers of the old way," not people who merely might like the new one (`HxSsThn_ptc` transcript).

## Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Acceptable | Strong | Savant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem Evidence | Names a plausible pain | Shows recent customer behavior | Identifies triggered, costly, current struggle with workarounds |
| Customer Forces | Lists pains and benefits | Maps push, pull, inertia, friction | Quantifies which force blocks or drives the switch |
| MVP Definition | Defines a small product | Defines problem, prospect, promise, price, package | Shows the ordered dependencies between all five P decisions |
| Validation Design | Suggests interviews or tests | Uses staged evidence gates | Converts each unknown into the smallest behavior test |
| Pricing Logic | Guesses a price | Anchors to alternatives | Ties price to economic pain, segment, and commitment threshold |
| Learning Velocity | Produces tasks | Produces experiments | Produces a weekly decision engine with kill, pivot, or proceed gates |
| Founder Discipline | Encourages action | Prevents overbuilding | Removes emotional relief until the evidence earns the next build step |
| Question Framing | Asks general questions | Uses past-tense behavioral prompts | Reconstructs the full trigger-to-choice story without leading |
| Insight Audit | Summarizes customer comments | Separates fact from interpretation | Ties every product/offer decision to transcript-grounded evidence |
| Buying Prediction | Guesses likely features | Extracts buying criteria | Predicts purchase by mapping trigger, old-way failure, alternatives, anxiety, and commitment |

Savant-grade output looks like Ash's own Steve case study: 15 architect interviews narrowed to a segment paying "$5,000 a month," tested with a 90-day pilot, closing 12 of 15 (80%) before any code shipped (`U2za4uy3cgc` transcript) — not a rubric score, a specific, falsifiable number.
