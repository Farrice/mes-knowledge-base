# Jonah Berger — Genius Context

Source: Berger's long-form interview on word-of-mouth science, Contagious, and The Catalyst (Valuetainment, "How Marketers Create Billion Dollar Trends"). Patterns below are his stated methodology, in his logic, stripped of interviewer noise.

## Genius Patterns

### Pattern: Virality Is a Recipe, Not Lightning
People call viral hits "lightning in a bottle." Berger's entire career refutes that: analyzing thousands of articles, tens of thousands of brands, and millions of purchases shows the same six factors recurring. If you understand why one thing hit, you can engineer the next one — like a cake recipe: know what to add so it works, don't just point at hits and flops.
**Execute**: Before creating anything, run it against STEPPS as a checklist. Score each of the six drivers; strengthen the two weakest before launch instead of shipping and praying.
**Success Metric**: You can name, in advance, which specific drivers a piece is engineered to fire — and after launch, sharing correlates with the drivers you built in.

### Pattern: Social Currency — Make the SHARER Look Good
We share "I met a celebrity / I got promoted," never "I got fired." Everyone obsesses over how *they* look; almost no one asks how the *customer* looks when they talk about you. The unlock is inverting the question: how does mentioning us make our customer more impressive, insider, or in-the-know?
**Execute**: For every product/idea, write the sentence a customer says at dinner that makes THEM look good — not the sentence that flatters you. If sharing you confers no status, redesign what's shareable (insider access, remarkable fact, visible achievement).
**Success Metric**: Real customers repeat a status-conferring line about you unprompted; word of mouth references the customer's discovery, not your ad.

### Pattern: Triggers — Top of Mind, Tip of Tongue
Peanut butter reminds us of jelly. Most talk isn't motivated — it's cued by the environment. Products linked to frequent environmental cues get talked about continuously; clever-but-uncued campaigns die in a week.
**Execute**: Pick a frequent, stable cue in your audience's daily environment (a time, place, object, routine) and weld your message to it repeatedly until the cue itself does the advertising.
**Success Metric**: Mentions recur steadily over weeks (trigger-driven), not in one spike (novelty-driven).

### Pattern: High-Arousal Emotion Drives Sharing — Both Directions
When we care, we share — but only activating emotions. Awe, excitement, hope, anger, anxiety fire people up to act; sadness and contentment sedate them. Obama ran on hope and inspiration; Trump ran on anger and anxiety — opposite valence, same mechanism: high arousal. Berger's newspaper text-analysis work showed adding emotion makes content meaningfully more likely to hit most-emailed lists.
**Execute**: Audit your draft: which specific emotion does it evoke, and is it activating? If the honest answer is "mild interest," rework the framing until it produces awe, anger, anxiety, or inspiration — not just information.
**Success Metric**: A reader can name the feeling in one word, and it's a high-arousal one.

### Pattern: The Kernel Comes Before the Story
Everyone wants to jump to the punch line. Berger forces clients backwards: "Imagine you could script one conversation between two customers. What's the ONE thing they say about you?" That kernel — the differentiator, the moral — is what the story exists to carry. The Blendtec video shows one thing only: the blender is powerful. Not price, not availability. One kernel, demonstrated unforgettably. A story that entertains but teaches the wrong moral (or no moral) is the ad everyone remembers while forgetting the brand.
**Execute**: Write the kernel as one sentence before drafting anything. After drafting, ask a cold reader "what did you learn?" If their answer isn't the kernel, the story failed — no matter how funny it is.
**Success Metric**: Retelling test: when someone repeats the story secondhand, the kernel survives the retelling.

### Pattern: Surface Stories, Don't Invent Them — Hub and Spoke
You don't have to be creative; you have to be strategic. Five salespeople and 100 customers already hold 20–40 stories. Some are already working. Capture them, pick the ones that carry your kernel, then act as the hub of a hub-and-spoke: push the proven stories out to the rest of the network.
**Execute**: Interview existing customers and salespeople: "What do you tell people about us?" Rank the collected stories by kernel-fidelity and repeatability; amplify the top 2–3 everywhere instead of writing fiction from scratch.
**Success Metric**: Your flagship story is one a real customer told first — and it spreads without you present.

### Pattern: Catalysts Ask "Why Haven't They Changed?" — Not "How Do I Push?"
To move a chair, you push it. Push a person and they push back — counter-arguing every reason you're wrong. Berger interviewed hostage negotiators, substance-abuse counselors, salespeople, and parenting experts and found the same inversion everywhere: find the parking brake. A car on an incline doesn't need more gas; it needs the brake released. Five recurring brakes spell REDUCE: Reactance, Endowment, Distance, Uncertainty, Corroborating evidence.
**Execute**: When a change stalls, ban the question "what else can I say?" Diagnose instead: which of the five barriers is engaged? Mitigate that one specifically. Doctor rule: diagnose before you put a cast on the leg.
**Success Metric**: You can name the specific barrier before proposing the next move — and the move targets the barrier, not the pitch.

### Pattern: Reactance Disarms — Menus and Attitude-Action Gaps
People carry anti-persuasion radar; feeling sold makes defenses go up. Two of Berger's disarms: (1) Provide a menu — offer options A/B/C so the listener's mental job flips from "why is this wrong?" to "which do I prefer?" (works on clients and three-year-olds: "broccoli or chicken first?"). (2) Highlight the gap — ask questions that expose the distance between their own stated attitude and their behavior ("Would you want others to wear a mask around your grandmother? ...So why aren't you?"). They persuade themselves.
**Execute**: Never present one recommendation. Present 2–3 curated options and let them choose; or lead with a question whose honest answer commits them to your conclusion.
**Success Metric**: The other party states the conclusion in their own words before you do.

### Pattern: Uncertainty Is Killed by Trial, Not Argument
New = unknown = frozen. The old thing's flaws are at least familiar. Freemium (Dropbox, Pandora), free shipping/returns (Zappos), free trials — these don't argue uncertainty away, they let people experience the answer cheaply. In disrupted times, forced change makes people newly open to challenger brands — uncertainty flips from enemy to opening.
**Execute**: When adoption stalls on "what if it doesn't work for us," stop selling and lower the cost of finding out: trial, pilot, sample, money-back guarantee.
**Success Metric**: Conversion pipeline shifts from convince→buy to try→keep.

### Pattern: Corroborating Evidence — Concentration Beats Credibility
One person says you have a tail, you laugh; five people say it, you turn around. One advocate — however credible — is dismissible as "that's just you" (the translation problem). Multiple similar sources, concentrated in time, are not.
**Execute**: For big asks, orchestrate several independent proof points from people similar to the target, landing close together — not one glowing testimonial repeated louder.
**Success Metric**: The target hears the same message from 3+ sources they identify with, within a short window.

## Hidden Knowledge

### Insight: 90% of Word of Mouth Is Offline
**Insight**: Only about 10% of word of mouth happens online. The rest is face-to-face — dinner tables, drinks with friends — where the listener is actually listening, unlike a feed where attention is split three ways. Marketers systematically over-invest in the visible 10%.
**Deploy**: Design campaigns with an explicit offline-conversation goal ("what will they say at dinner?") and measure downstream action (calls, emails, purchases), not impressions.

### Insight: Story Beats Product, Sales Team, and Culture
**Insight**: Asked to pick a single winner among great product / marketing / sales team / culture / storytelling, Berger rules out product ("there are lots of great products you've never heard of — who needs it?") and picks story: it's memorable, retold, and turns customers into the largest sales force you'll ever have — and a friend's recommendation isn't discounted as self-interested the way a salesperson's is.
**Deploy**: Budget storytelling as infrastructure, not decoration. Before scaling paid spend or headcount, verify there's a story customers physically retell.

### Insight: Start with Psychology, Not Platform
**Insight**: Would-be influencers ask "which platform, what cadence?" Berger's first five moves are all psychology: who are my existing customers, what do they like about me, what stories are they already telling, why do people find this attractive — build from there. Platforms rotate (MySpace→Facebook→whatever's next); the drivers of sharing haven't changed since cavemen bragged about the hunt.
**Deploy**: When someone asks for channel tactics, first make them answer the four audience-psychology questions. Channel strategy falls out of the answers.

### Insight: Fragmented Media Makes Sharing the Only Reliable Distribution
**Insight**: No single ad buy reaches everyone anymore; everyone is their own media channel with their own followers. That fragmentation makes engineered sharing MORE valuable than paid reach — it's cheaper and more persuasive — and it's the equalizer that lets small players beat big ad budgets.
**Deploy**: For under-resourced brands, treat shareability engineering (STEPPS) as the primary distribution strategy, paid media as amplification of what already spreads.

### Insight: Same Principles, Different Application per Context
**Insight**: The six drivers hold across B2B, politics, religion, parenting, education — but the application differs: what makes a 50-year-old executive look good is not what makes a 15-year-old look good. Principle-level thinking transfers; tactic-level copying fails.
**Deploy**: Never port a tactic between audiences. Re-derive it: same driver, fresh answer to "what does THIS audience gain socially by sharing this?"
