# LENS CARD — Nicolas Cole (sentence-level craft engineer: compression, terminal power, reader-demand mechanics)
> Compiled 2026-07-27 from genius.md (canonical). FIRING RULE: this card decides WHETHER this lens has something to say about a draft line; when a lens fires, READ the matching genius.md section before treating. Never treat from the card alone.

## Lenses
### Terminal Word Power Placement
Before finalizing any sentence, identify the most important word. Restructure so that word lands last. Small words (is, it, why, the) should never end sentences. The last word echoes in the reader's mind — weak endings drain power because the echo is empty. Audit move: read ONLY the last word of every sentence aloud; restructure any that end on articles, auxiliary verbs, or weak prepositions.
**Test:** Final words are nouns or power verbs, never articles or auxiliary verbs.

### Three-Pass Economic Compression
Pass 1: write the complete thought without editing. Pass 2: circle every word under 4 letters; remove as many as possible. Pass 3: reorder remaining words for grammatical flow. Writing is fundamentally a compression exercise — the first draft captures meaning; every subsequent draft compresses signal while eliminating noise. Treat editing as compression, not addition.
**Test:** 30-50% word reduction while maintaining full meaning.

### Contraction Introduction Protocol
First mention = full term + (ABBREVIATION). All subsequent mentions = abbreviation only. Never use undefined contractions.
**Test:** Zero undefined contractions; reader never encounters an unknown abbreviation.

### Audience Sizing Through Vocabulary
Before writing, define audience size goal. Contractions and jargon = smaller, insider audience. Plain language = broader reach. Contractions are not about saving space; they define who belongs in the conversation.
**Test:** Vocabulary matches intended audience sophistication level precisely; contraction level chosen consciously before writing.

### Redundancy Elimination Radar
After each sentence, ask: "Did I already say this?" After each paragraph: "Does this add NEW information?" Delete anything that restates without advancing. Repetition signals to readers that you've run out of things to say — even well-disguised repetition triggers subconscious disengagement. Watch for restated-transition tells like "as mentioned above" or "like I said."
**Test:** Every sentence introduces new information or perspective.

### Word Variation Substitution
Circle any word that appears twice in close proximity. Replace the second instance with a synonym, related term, or pronoun.
**Test:** No exact word repetition within 2-3 sentences (except intentional emphasis).

### Fourth-Grade Vocabulary Anchoring
Write at fourth-grade level. Add maximum ONE elevated word per sentence or paragraph. One friction point per sentence feels like intellectual reward; multiple friction points feel like punishment — "ascertain" and "utilize" in the same sentence punishes the reader twice in one breath.
**Test:** Flesch-Kincaid grade level 4-6 with strategic vocabulary elevations; never more than one per sentence/paragraph.

### The Two-Comma Rule
Count commas in each sentence. 0-2 = controlled. 3-6 = the "valley of death" — split or restructure. 7+ = intentional stylistic choice only. Comma count correlates with thought clarity: writers who can't control commas can't control their thinking. High commas? Ask "what am I actually trying to say?"
**Test:** Zero sentences in the 3-6 comma valley of death.

### Hemingway Adverb Elimination
Search for -ly words. For each, ask: "Is this saying something the verb doesn't already say?" If no, delete. Replace weak verb+adverb with a strong single verb. Adverbs are training wheels propping up weak verbs — when you find an adverb, the verb is the problem. "Walked slowly" becomes "crept," not "walked slowly (deleted)."
**Test:** Adverb count reduced by 80%+.

### Opening Sentence Power
The first sentence determines whether readers continue. No throat-clearing ("In this article..."). Lead with curiosity, controversy, or specificity.
**Test:** Reader must continue within the first 10 words.

### Information Density Optimization
For each paragraph, count sentences delivering NEW information vs. restating/padding. If a paragraph adds nothing, delete, merge, or rewrite it.
**Test:** Target ratio 0.8+ — 80% of sentences add new value; every paragraph advances reader understanding.

### Voice Preservation
Identify voice markers (unusual word choices, rhythm patterns, personality quirks) before optimizing. Protect these even when they violate "rules."
**Test:** A reader familiar with the author cannot tell the text was edited — except it's better.

### Sentence Demand Architecture (evolution layer)
Five demand mechanics deployed at sentence boundaries so readers can't stop at ANY of them: Incomplete Transfer, Expectation Violation, Specificity Spike, Reversal Bridge, Compression Cliffhanger (the most powerful — it leverages terminal word strength so the echo POINTS FORWARD into the next sentence). Plus Energy Crescendo Mapping (Build/Spike/Cascade) and the Flat Paragraph Kill Rule.
**Test:** Demand Test — can the reader stop at this sentence boundary? Any flat paragraph gets killed.

## Anti-patterns / calibration
Standing checklist (provenance note: these carry no verbatim Cole quote — UNCONFIRMED against primary source; treat as documented craft convention, not quotes):
1. Never end a sentence on a weak function word ("is," "it," "the," "of," "to").
2. Never let a sentence run 3-6 commas — split or restructure back to 0-2.
3. Never keep an adverb the verb already implies.
4. Never open with throat-clearing — the reader decides within the first 10 words.
5. Never stack more than one elevated word per sentence.
6. Never restate a point across two sentences without adding new information.
7. Never introduce an abbreviation cold.
8. Never sand down a voice marker in the name of "cleaner" prose — idiosyncratic phrasing survives editing even when it bends the other seven rules.

**Calibration — the craft must be invisible:** Do NOT enumerate patterns applied, do NOT label sentences by pattern name, do NOT explain the framework — embody it at the sentence level. The reader should never see the scaffolding, only feel the result: a sentence that lands, an ending that doesn't leak power, a paragraph with no fat left on it. Cole's texture is mechanical precision married to plain speech — never academic, never showing its editing marks. Polish is the tell-class warning: if every sentence ends on a "power word" with mathematical regularity, that regularity is itself what gives away the machine. Cole's real prose varies — most sentences end strong, not all, and the occasional short flat sentence is what makes the strong ones land. Sentence craft fails when the craft is visible — checklist execution surfaces the mechanism and kills the prose. The test: would Cole recognize this as his own writing — or clock it as someone reciting his rules back at him? If unsure, it's the second — rewrite.
