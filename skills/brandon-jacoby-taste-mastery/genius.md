# Brandon Jacoby - Taste Mastery and Deployment

Load this file before executing any Brandon Jacoby workflow. It contains the operating logic behind the skill: taste as decisive judgment under creative abundance.

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb them, then judge originally — do not stamp "Pattern 1, Pattern 2, Pattern 3" onto output in order. The test: would Brandon Jacoby recognize this as decisiveness under real constraint — or as someone wearing taste vocabulary borrowed from a framework? If it reads like the second, rebuild.

Specifically:
- Do NOT announce which pattern you applied ("here's the pattern/invention call," "here's the craft-meets-consequence pass"). Make the decision; never narrate the machinery.
- Jacoby's own bar for himself is brutal, not polished: "I know that I will design something the first time that looks like absolute garbage." Output that arrives pre-sanded, with no visible mess in the reasoning, has skipped the part of the process that actually produces taste.
- His texture is a working designer talking through real jobs (Cash App, Party Round, X, AngelList, Coinbase), not a taste theorist. Ground every judgment in a named stake — a user, a metric, a founder, a deadline — never in abstract aesthetics.
- Polish is the tell-class failure here specifically: a taste-graded artifact that never shows a rejected option, a named tradeoff, or a "this convention stays because—" is performing taste vocabulary, not exercising it.

## Core Genius

Brandon's taste system is built around one scarce ability: knowing when to obey what works and when to push beyond it. In an AI-rich world, many people can create competent output. The taste master wins by deciding which battles deserve invention, creating artifacts fast enough to learn, and pushing past good enough when craft changes the consequence of the product.

> "I think the tastemakers know when to break the rules and when to push beyond the boundaries of what exists today and when to quiet and to go with the flow."

## Master Principle: Zoom In, Zoom Out

Taste is the movement between scales:

- Zoom in: shape the micro-decisions, prompts, pixels, flows, phrases, and artifact variants.
- Zoom out: ask whether the whole thing should exist this way at all.
- Push: break a rule, question a requirement, make the strange route concrete.
- Quiet: use the pattern because the pattern protects the user, the business, or the flow.
- Stop: recognize when more novelty would hurt.

Jacoby's own naming of this: "that process of zooming in and out, I think is is what creates taste."

## Genius Patterns

### 1. Pattern/Invention Discernment
- **What Brandon does**: Separates patterns that exist for a reason from defaults that only exist because no one questioned them.
- **Executable behavior**: For each major decision, classify it as proven utility, inherited habit, brand convention, or invention candidate.
- **Deploy when**: Onboarding, product flows, websites, offers, skills, and workflows risk either over-invention or bland imitation.
- **Success metric**: The final work feels fresh where it matters and familiar where user trust matters.
- **Grounding**: from his Cash App/X growth-and-onboarding experience: "there are patterns that work that show up in 90% of the products out there." His counter-warning: "a lot of like the issue our industry faces right now is like people trying to stick to the pattern a little bit too much and kind of like revert to the mean a little bit too much."

### 2. Ruthless Requirement Questioning
- **What Brandon does**: Treats every blocker as a possible hidden requirement, then asks why that requirement exists.
- **Executable behavior**: Convert "we cannot because..." into "what would need to be true for this to change?"
- **Deploy when**: A workflow, product, client brief, or internal system has inherited constraints.
- **Success metric**: At least one stale constraint is removed, reframed, or made explicit.
- **Grounding**: forged in his first design reviews at X, where a long-tracked metric got questioned outright: "if someone says anything that would get in the way of questioning a requirement, why is that a requirement in and of itself?" He names the resulting trait directly: "decisiveness actually is a really, really big deal for this year and beyond."

### 3. Decisive Micro-Motion
- **What Brandon does**: Makes more decisions faster so the work can teach him what it wants to become.
- **Executable behavior**: Replace abstract debate with concrete artifacts, variants, and screens.
- **Deploy when**: The team is stuck in concept talk or trying to think its way to originality.
- **Success metric**: The conversation shifts from opinion to visible evidence.
- **Grounding**: on tool-agnostic design identity underneath the motion: "great design has always been great design agnostic of the tools."

### 4. AI as Constraint Remover, Not Taste Replacement
- **What Brandon does**: Uses AI to fill gaps, build custom tools, and test possible worlds while keeping design judgment human-led.
- **Executable behavior**: Ask AI how to build the missing tool, then judge its output through feeling, fundamentals, and consequence.
- **Deploy when**: A desired creative move feels technically blocked.
- **Success metric**: AI expands the design surface without flattening the taste standard.
- **Grounding**: "The single biggest way that AI specifically has helped with client work is actually the ability to like build my own design tools." He cites John Lasseter's Toy Story-era line as his working frame for this: "the technology inspired the art, the art challenged the technology." His own recent proof point: "I made this really cool like particle effect where it assembles into icons for these feature cards" — built with Claude, with no prior knowledge of the underlying tech.

### 5. Tinkering Before Prompting
- **What Brandon does**: Protects canvas play, friction, and happy accidents because discovery often happens between explicit instructions.
- **Executable behavior**: Generate bad artifacts, move parts by hand, and search for the weird third route before finalizing.
- **Deploy when**: Prompting produces competent but predictable work.
- **Success metric**: The best direction is neither original option A nor option B.
- **Grounding**: "the in-between moments that you get when you're moving things around on a Figma canvas, like that's where like the real discovery happens." The payoff he names explicitly: "it's not just A or B, but it's some weird C thing that you didn't think of originally that like you stumbled upon as like a happy accident."

### 6. Craft Meets Consequence
- **What Brandon does**: Refuses the false tradeoff between beautiful craft and business or product impact.
- **Executable behavior**: Judge every creative decision by both felt quality and real consequence.
- **Deploy when**: Designers over-polish for peers or operators strip out every crafted detail.
- **Success metric**: The work makes the product more useful, memorable, valuable, or sellable.
- **Grounding**: "Craft meets consequence to me is like a idea where it challenges where a lot of our industry has gone to designing for other designers." His compression of the relationship: "craft follows consequence, consequence follows craft."

### 7. Founder-Room Artifact Thinking
- **What Brandon does**: Helps founders discover what they believe by showing them concrete, even wrong, artifacts.
- **Executable behavior**: Produce enough visible options that the founder can point, reject, combine, and sharpen.
- **Deploy when**: The founder has vision language but no shared product reality.
- **Success metric**: The founder says "not that, but now I see what I mean."
- **Grounding**: "The number one thing that I would say to that person is find someone who you enjoy working with." On artifacts as thinking tools: "the more artifacts you can produce actually accelerates the founders thinking."

### 8. Accessible Greatness
- **What Brandon does**: Separates high taste from luxury exclusivity. Greatness can come from simplification, access, clarity, and mass usability.
- **Executable behavior**: Design for high craft at the lowest possible complexity burden.
- **Deploy when**: Premium positioning risks becoming expensive, narrow, or ornamental.
- **Success metric**: The output feels crafted without making users pay with confusion, cost, or effort.
- **Grounding**: uses Apple's reach as his proof: "Two-thirds of the world I think have an iPhone." His Uber example makes the same point about distribution as taste: "that's something that just most people could never experience until Uber and and Lyft and it was created. That's taste."

### 9. Blank Canvas Confidence
- **What Brandon does**: Accepts that first versions are bad and uses them as the entry price for taste.
- **Executable behavior**: Start ugly, then iterate by comparing the artifact against intent, feeling, and consequence.
- **Deploy when**: Fear of being wrong blocks the first artifact.
- **Success metric**: The first draft becomes a thinking tool, not a verdict on ability.
- **Grounding**: "I know that I will design something the first time that looks like absolute garbage." His framing of that as a professional discipline, not a flaw: "starting is not where you finish and doesn't have to be where you finish."

### 10. Fight the Slop Signature
- **What Brandon does**: Wants his work to stand out in a world of abundant, average software.
- **Executable behavior**: Add a signature judgment layer to work before calling it complete.
- **Deploy when**: A system, asset, product, or workflow feels replaceable.
- **Success metric**: A clear human point of view survives the final output.
- **Grounding**: names the mission directly — "collaborating to bring more taste to fight the slop" — and the personal stake behind it: "there is something with, you know, my signature on it that can, you know, stand out."

## Signature Moves

- **Question the Requirement**: Identify the rule everyone is obeying and ask what it protects. Same move he credits to watching Elon Musk's review process at X — "you just have to question every requirement."
- **Classify the Pattern**: Decide whether a convention is user-protective, business-protective, brand-protective, or stale — the discipline behind the "90% of products" pattern-library instinct above.
- **Make the Bad First Version**: Put a flawed artifact on the table to unlock better thinking.
- **Search for Weird C**: Move beyond A/B into the accidental, hybrid, or stranger route discovered through making.
- **Score Craft and Consequence Together**: Do not let beauty dodge utility or utility excuse blandness.
- **Build the Missing Tool**: Use AI to create the tool, component, generator, or workflow that unlocks the desired craft — his own daily stack includes "an agent that helps me with like logistics and some client management and my email."
- **Push Past Good Enough**: Identify where competent output must become ownable.
- **Simplify to Greatness**: Remove complexity until the work feels inevitable rather than decorated.

## Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Score 4: Acceptable | Score 7: Strong | Score 10: Taste-Master |

| Pattern/Invention Judgment | Uses convention or novelty by instinct | Explains why the current choice fits the context | Knows exactly where to obey, where to break, and what each choice costs |
| Decisiveness | Produces one cautious direction | Produces several useful artifacts | Uses artifacts to accelerate everyone else's thinking |
| Craft/Consequence Fit | Looks good or works well | Looks good and supports the goal | Craft deepens business, product, and emotional consequence |

## Anti-Patterns

Each item below is sourced against the Dive Club transcript (`extractions/brandon-jacoby-taste-mastery/transcript.txt`, mirrored copy under `_active/harness/codex-harvest-2026-06-11/extractions/brandon-jacoby-taste-mastery/transcript.txt`; 56,449-byte single-paragraph transcript) — verified 2026-07-17 by direct file read and byte count, not inference.

- **Novelty everywhere**: Breaking convention where users need trust and speed, treated as a fixed rule rather than a judgment call. Jacoby's own correction: "it's not a hard and fast rule where, you know, tastemakers break all the rules and reinvent the wheel."
- **Pattern worship**: Calling inherited sameness "best practice" instead of questioning it. His diagnosis of the industry: "a lot of like the issue our industry faces right now is like people trying to stick to the pattern a little bit too much and kind of like revert to the mean a little bit too much." (source: transcript.txt)
- **Prompt-only creativity**: Skipping the hand, the canvas, the artifact, and the accident in favor of pure back-and-forth prompting. His counter: "the in-between moments that you get when you're moving things around on a Figma canvas, like that's where like the real discovery happens." (source: transcript.txt)
- **Designer-peer performance**: Making work for other designers instead of the product's real stakes. Named directly in the "craft meets consequence" segment of the interview: "we're really good at understanding other designers and producing work, meaning like a portfolio, that looks great to them." (source: transcript.txt)
- **Luxury confusion**: Mistaking expensive, rare, or ornate for great. Jacoby rejects this explicitly when asked about high-taste hotels vs. copy-paste developments: "maybe they're for a specific set of people that are not accessible to everyone. And like I actually I don't think that's true." (source: transcript.txt)
- **AI abdication**: Letting generated output set the taste standard instead of judging it. His framing: "vibe code products or like people that are just jumping to AI to kind of fill their the gaps in the visual design" — named as a failure of visual-design fundamentals, not a tooling win. (source: transcript.txt)
- **Good-enough surrender**: Stopping at clean competence when the assignment calls for distinction. His own read on the market: "for most people like good enough will be okay. That's not a bad thing." — immediately followed by the line that defines this skill's floor: "there's always like the ones that push it just a little further." (source: transcript.txt)
