**1. Teaching-Is-Highest-Paying** — Teaching, not selling, is the highest-leverage skill, because the business model "teach and get paid" runs without a sales team.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar rejects the consensus "sales is the highest paying skill" and structures the whole business around teaching as the revenue act: *"sales isn't the highest paying skill... Teaching is the highest paying skill."* The proof he points to is the absence of a sales department — *"I've made millions of dollars in my business... without a sales team"* (UNCONFIRMED). He also fixes the owner's intent up front so the model fits the goal: *"I'm going to make a lot of money in my young years,"* not build-to-sell.

**Executable behavior:** Before optimizing any script, funnel, or close, ask the higher-leverage question: *can you teach the thing in a way that makes people feel like taking the next step?* If yes, route effort into the teach — every phase of revenue gets carried by teaching, so no separate sales motion is required. State the owner's intent (cash-now vs. build-to-sell) in one sentence, and let it pick the model.

**Deploy when:** A client is grinding on cold outreach, closing scripts, or hiring closers while sitting on real expertise people already ask them for help with.

**Success metric:** Revenue is produced through teaching assets (content, web class, delivery) with no dedicated sales team — selling is a byproduct of the teach, not a separate function.

**2. Four-Phase Teach-Sequence** — Every business is four phases (Lead Gen → Conversion → Fulfillment → Retention/Ascension), and teaching is the engine of all four, sequenced.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar's unlock is that no phase is a "sales phase" or a "delivery phase" — *"teaching could be used at every phase of the business."* Teach for lead gen (content is the magnet), teach them what the business can do for them so they buy, teach them whatever they bought (delivery IS the teach), then teach them into the next level. The machine self-feeds: *"the group that came to the teach jumps into the next teach, the next of that teach goes into the next level of teach."*

**Executable behavior:** Lay the four phases in a row and write the single teaching objective for each — Lead Gen: get in front / build trust; Conversion: shift the belief that makes the next step obvious; Fulfillment: teach the bought outcome; Retention: teach the next level. Each phase is a teach with a DIFFERENT objective, never a different function.

**Deploy when:** Mapping or auditing any expertise business (coach, consultant, service provider, expert, speaker) — especially one that treats marketing, sales, and delivery as separate departments.

**Success metric:** All four phases have a named teach-objective, and graduates of one teach visibly ascend into the next, producing predictable repeat enrollment without a re-acquisition spend.

**3. Feeling-First Objective** — The goal of teaching is to create a feeling that moves the person to the next step, not to transfer information.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar resets the objective at the root: *"The goal to teach is not to get people information. The goal of teaching is to create the feeling in a person to take the next step."* The law beneath it, from Eileen Wilder: *"People do things for one reason and one reason only. Because they feel like it."* So a stalled audience simply *doesn't feel like it.* This makes him a **servant communicator** — *"someone who's thinking about the person receiving the communication. It's not just what you say, it's how you say what you say."*

**Executable behavior:** Before drafting any teaching asset, write the TARGET FEELING, not the target information ("they should feel that this is doable for someone like them, and feel relief that there's a clear next move"). Then reverse-engineer the teach to produce that feeling. If you can't name the feeling, you're not ready to write.

**Deploy when:** Content, emails, or web classes get attention but no movement — people watch, nod, and don't act or buy.

**Success metric:** The asset is judged by the impulse it created ("I felt ready to start / book / buy"), not by how much was covered ("I learned a lot").

**4. Fire-Hose Audit** — The #1 mistake online teachers make is over-teaching; an overwhelmed person never takes action, so boil it to ONE thing.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar names over-teaching as the top failure: *"if someone was thirsty and they needed a drink of water, and you came with a fire hose, you didn't serve them. You hurt them."* The mechanism is hard: *"an overwhelmed person never takes action... will not buy from you and they will actually... be more confused."* He separates two things people conflate — over-teaching (drowning them) is NOT over-delivering (someone watches a free video "for the seventh time" and feels they got a fortune). And he diagnoses the root cause as insecurity: *"insecure people tend to over-teach because they are trying to prove... there's nothing to prove"* (Frank Kern: *"Prove to people you can help them by actually helping them."*).

**Executable behavior:** Run every asset through the fire-hose check before publishing — if it teaches more than ONE thing, front-loads how-to weeds, or leans on industry lingo, cut. Hold the discipline phrase: *"Boil it down to one. One thing at a time. It's one drink at a time."* Over-deliver on value and feeling; never on information count.

**Deploy when:** Reviewing any draft from a domain expert who is "too close to the solution" and tends to dump everything they know.

**Success metric:** The asset teaches exactly one thing, uses zero unexplained lingo, and leaves the receiver feeling capable and clear — not impressed-but-paralyzed.

**5. Possible-Easy-Trust Triad** — The real job is shifting a belief, accomplished by making the person feel it's possible, feel it's easy, and feel they can trust you — and "easy" pre-sells the paid relationship.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar defines the work: *"Your job is for someone to think about or believe about the thing differently than they did before they met you."* The lever is three felt beliefs — *"making people feel like it's possible... like it's easy... like they can trust you."* The hidden second-order effect closes the sale by itself: *"if they feel like it's easy because you said it... psychologically they're thinking to themself, it's going to be easy if I work with them."* (Case: Paige — new IG in January, ~300K followers, $300 product, ~3,000 buyers, ~$1.2M, no sales team — *"she's helped people make like creating content could be so easy."* UNCONFIRMED.)

**Executable behavior:** For any offer or asset, write the three belief-shifts explicitly: (a) what makes the outcome feel POSSIBLE for someone like them, (b) what makes the path feel EASY, (c) what makes YOU trustable (proof, calm, specificity). Install the "easy" belief in free content so the paid relationship is pre-sold before any pitch.

**Deploy when:** Building a conversion asset, a free lead magnet, or any content meant to move a cold audience toward a first purchase.

**Success metric:** A receiver can state, after the asset, that the outcome now feels possible AND easy AND that they'd trust this person to deliver it — a measurable shift in all three beliefs vs. before.

**6. Context-Value Law** — The context in which content is consumed determines its value, which dissolves all gatekeeping anxiety: give the WHAT generously in transient channels, reserve depth for committed contexts.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar's frame: *"The context in which the content is consumed determines the value of the content."* Identical information is worth nothing on a feed and a fortune in a paid room because attention differs — *"where your treasure is, there your heart is also. If you pay them, you'll pay attention more."* He gives the entire play away on YouTube precisely *because* it's transient: *"you're going to click away in a couple minutes."* Paid attention creates a *"post-transaction consumer"* who *"pays attention differently."* The proof: people flew in from Florida, Denver, Vancouver, drove from California and Utah to a live recording they KNEW would be posted free — *"because the context of the content determines the value of the content."*

**Executable behavior:** Stop asking "how much do I give away?" Ask "what's the context?" In free/transient channels, give the WHAT generously (it builds trust and proves obsession). In paid/committed containers, that's where depth lives — because the attention is structurally different. Set the disclosure depth from the channel, not from fear.

**Deploy when:** A creator or client hesitates to publish their best material for fear of "giving away the secret sauce."

**Success metric:** Free content is generous and gatekeeping-free, yet paid enrollment holds or rises — proof that the paid container sells the CONTEXT (committed attention), not withheld information.

**7. Title-How-Deliver-What** — Title content in "how to," then deliver WHAT / WHEN / WHY / WHERE (and their negations); reserve actual HOW for the right (paid, anyone-can-do-it) context.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar's rule from V1: *"Don't teach people how to do something tactfully. Teach when or when not... why or why not... where or where not... what or what not. But don't teach how"* — because *"when you start teaching how, you will start to lose people."* The packaging trick: you CAN title it "how to" — his "how to edit in Premiere Pro" video has 2M+ views (UNCONFIRMED) and never teaches editing; it shows what to do *before* editing (make a folder, open the app, save, create a sequence). People *"felt more empowered to edit"* — that's the win. V2 reconciles the apparent contradiction: *"when somebody pays you, they'll be more receptive of the how-to,"* delivered *"in the right context and in a way that anyone can do it."* (Hormozi's version: *"teach how-to then sell the implementation"* — disguise the how, still deliver the what.)

**Executable behavior:** Take any "how-to" topic and rewrite the BODY as WHAT/WHEN/WHY/WHERE + negations (what NOT to do, when NOT to). Keep the "how to" in the TITLE for reach. Park the step-by-step HOW for the paid container, where the receiver is receptive and it's framed so anyone can execute. Treat gatekeeping and context-calibration as different things — this is calibration, not withholding.

**Deploy when:** Writing free/transient content (YouTube, podcast, social) on a tactical skill, or auditing content that drifts into premature step-by-step from the creator's POV.

**Success metric:** The free asset leaves people empowered to START (and saying "I did X because of you"), with the granular HOW reserved for paid — reach is high, and confusion/drop-off from over-instruction is absent.

**8. Four-Soils Filter** — The same seed (information) lands on four kinds of soil; design for the receptive fraction, and gate your own student-quality (implementation) first.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar applies the parable of the four soils to both sides of the exchange. The seed lands on: the **path** (closed heart, snatched away), **rocky** (shallow heart, springs up and withers — *"I'm four minutes in and there's nothing... you missed it"*), **weedy** (distracted by *"the worries of life, the deceitfulness of wealth"* — *"a million's not enough, be a billionaire... focused on the wrong thing"*), and **good** soil (*"those who do with what they hear"*). As a teacher, he holds the healthy assumption that *"not everyone's going to get what I'm going to put down"* and concerns himself only with *"the receptive group."* As a student, he turns the lens inward: *"the reason why you suck at teaching is because you're a sucky student... if you suck at learning, you will suck at teaching."* Good soil = implementation — *"there is so much revelation in implementation."* (Principle holds without Scripture: receptivity, not the message, determines harvest; and you can't teach what you won't implement.)

**Executable behavior:** Stop trying to convert all four soils — design content for the receptive fraction and treat the unreceptive (closed, shallow, distracted) as expected, not as failure. Then self-audit before teaching anything: *am I good soil? Do I implement what I consume?* If you only consume, fix that first — only good-soil students become teachers worth paying.

**Deploy when:** A creator takes every dropoff, hostile comment, or non-buyer as a personal failure; or before deploying anyone as a teacher who is a chronic course-collector but non-implementer.

**Success metric:** Effort and emotion are spent on the receptive segment (not the unreceptive), and the teacher has a current implementation track record — they ship what they learn.

**9. Undeniable Long-Form** — Set the objective per platform before creating, and use long-form to become undeniable, since "you can't fake who you are for a long time."

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar fixes the objective by context: *"there's different objectives for Instagram and YouTube and having a webinar... a master class... a workshop... a boot camp."* His YouTube objective is long-form so *"you undeniably know who I am"* — *"you can't fake who you are for a long time."* His read on Trump: won by sitting in 2-3 hour long-form formats (Rogan, golf with Bryson DeChambeau, Dave Ramsey) — *"how are you going to be a politician for three hours on the golf course?"* He defines mastery (via Myron Golden) as *"the use of unconscious resources to execute a given task"*; if a task feels hard you have a *"grow gap"* you haven't closed yet (he learned cadence by watching himself back and slowing down). Then he lets the audience self-select: *"99.99% of you guys won't even click the link... and that's totally fine. But for the 0.1%, let's go."*

**Executable behavior:** Before creating, write the objective for THIS platform (Instagram ≠ YouTube ≠ webinar ≠ workshop). Use unedited long-form as the trust-and-identity engine — duration exposes who you actually are. Where a task feels hard, name the grow gap and close it by reviewing your own reps (cadence, clarity), not by adding gear. End with a clean self-selection step; build for the receptive fraction, not the 99.99%.

**Deploy when:** A creator uses one undifferentiated objective across all platforms, hides behind heavy editing, or chases reach instead of building undeniable identity.

**Success metric:** Each platform has a written objective; long-form content produces "I undeniably know who you are" trust and a self-selecting trickle into the next step — quality of fit over raw volume.

**10. Mindset-Wrapped Tactics** — It's always mindset; deliver the tactic INSIDE a belief-shift, because the package can never out-run who the person is becoming.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar's verdict: *"It's always mindset."* Broke people chase tactics; people who've made it say it was always mindset, because *"you've got scales over your eyes"* that experience removes. The real bottleneck: *"We don't have an information problem. We have an application problem"* — plus a **consolidation problem** (drowning in unconsolidated inputs). This defines **true influence**: *"someone thinks about the thing differently than they did after they met you... and then they act on it."* Two laws ride alongside: *"The way you sell is the way you buy"* (cheap buyers become cheap sellers), and **teaching is a revealer** — *"all you're doing is you're revealing and projecting who you are... focus on who you're becoming. We don't become what we don't believe... we live in the lies we take on."* (This is the bridge back to the Interior Game.)

**Executable behavior:** When a client demands tactics, give the tactic embedded in a belief-shift — package the how-to, lead with the mindset that makes the how-to land. Diagnose stalls as application/consolidation problems, not information gaps ("what is the ONE doable action this season?"). And remember the ceiling: who the teacher is becoming caps what they can teach, so develop the person alongside the asset.

**Deploy when:** A client begs for "just the tactics/hacks," or content teaches steps that don't transform because no belief moved.

**Success metric:** The receiver thinks about the thing differently AND acts on it (true influence), and tactical advice always arrives wrapped in the belief that makes it usable — not as standalone hacks.

**11. Only-You Moat** — Never run out of content, because the uncopyable moat is content only you can make — mined from your lived client work into named, drawn frameworks.

**What they do (often unconsciously):** Omar reframes the fear of running dry as a diagnosis: *"if you're breathing, you should never run out of content"* — and if you feel you might, *"congratulations, you've identified that you need to grow."* The fix is becoming a learner — *"everything is a reference point"* — and reading often (one Proverb a day, by date). *"If eight YouTube videos in you're like 'now what,' you maxed out"* — an unwanted ceiling. The moat: *"How do you make content no one can copy? You make content only you can make"* — your stories, failures, wins, clients. The engine is the **Client Rolodex**: *"list out every single client you had... what situation they were in, what they did, and then what happened... find the thread in it to see — you have a framework in there."* Plus Myron Golden's $110K gem (UNCONFIRMED): *"for every letter of the alphabet, create frameworks and formulas for your expertise"* (three A's, three B's, three C's). Then name it (alliteration) and draw it — *"our brains are always looking for a picture."*

**Executable behavior:** Run the Client-Rolodex Mining pass — open a doc (or AI), list every past client with situation → action → result, and find the recurring thread; that thread is a framework. Mine the alphabet (frameworks/formulas for each letter of your expertise). Convert lived experience into NAMED (alliterative, e.g. his "ALVS" — Audio, Lighting, Video, Shot) and DRAWN frameworks. When idea-supply feels low, treat it as a growth signal: read often, cut the passive-consumption inputs, restock reference points.

**Deploy when:** A creator fears running out of ideas, produces commoditized advice anyone could give, or needs a content moat AI and competitors can't replicate.

**Success metric:** The content pipeline runs on lived, uncopyable material (client stories, named/drawn frameworks), the creator never hits a "now what" ceiling, and competitors literally cannot recreate the assets.
