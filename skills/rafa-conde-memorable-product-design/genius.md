## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

These patterns are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb the feeling-first sequence, then build originally — if the output visibly walks through "Step 1: name the feeling, Step 2: pick the artifact," you have failed. The test: would Rafa recognize this as a decision he'd actually make shipping Hand Mirror or Retro's onboarding flow — or as someone reciting "surprise and delight" vocabulary because it sounds designer-y? Rafa names that exact failure mode himself: "I know like surprise and delight. It's a very overused term nowadays, but I'm just going to use it again cuz I don't know what else to call it" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 217-220) — the phrase survives only when it is attached to one specific, felt mechanism, never used as a label.

Specifically:
- Do NOT announce the emotional target as a header before the work ("Target feeling: delight"). Name it internally, then build so the feeling is *felt*, never labeled on the page.
- Do NOT default to a static screen, mockup, or screen recording when the deliverable exists to sell an idea. Rafa's own rule: "don't just design the feature and share mock-ups. Don't just make a prototype and do a screen recording of you using the feature. Make an ad, right? Sell this idea." (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 5-10). If the artifact can't be watched, felt, or retold, it isn't finished.
- Restraint is the craft, not a caveat appended at the end. A first pass with too much animation, too many features, or too much explanation is a rough draft, not a finished Rafa move — his own slideshow prototype started "way too much motion" with a Ken Burns effect on every photo before he cut it down (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 645-648). Cut before you ship, not after feedback forces it.
- Polish-is-the-tell: a glossy, on-brief, competent deliverable with no specific remembered detail is the exact failure this skill exists to prevent (see Anti-Exemplar below). One real number, one real sound, one real texture beats five adjectives.

## Executive Summary

Rafa's core genius is treating software as a remembered feeling, not a feature list. He designs for the moment a user says "I know exactly what this is, why it matters, and how it made me feel." His method starts with emotional clarity, then uses pacing, context, tiny surprises, restraint, video, and tactile details to make the idea land.

This skill produces finished briefs, audits, onboarding designs, demo scripts, feature ads, scope rules, launch plans, and agent workflow upgrades that make product ideas felt and remembered.

## Core Thesis

Do not merely design the feature. Design:

- The feeling the user should remember.
- The context that makes the idea obvious.
- The first moment that sets the stage.
- The tiny detail people retell.
- The artifact that sells the idea before the product explains itself.

Rafa states the whole job in one line, unprompted, before the interview even formally opens: "The goal is to communicate this idea as cleanly as you can to someone else" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 3-4). Everything below is that sentence broken into executable moves.

## Decision Framework

Before executing any workflow, answer:

1. What is the target feeling: delight, relief, confidence, play, pride, intimacy, cleverness, safety, momentum, or calm?
2. What should the user remember one day later?
3. Is the product currently being explained with mockups when it needs a story, ad, or demo?
4. Where does the first-run experience set expectations for everything else?
5. Which detail could become the product's signature?
6. What must stay out so the product remains legible and charming?
7. Which artifact will carry the feeling: app flow, video, landing page, release note, prototype, onboarding, or agent workflow?

Weigh question 4 against real cost, not hypothetical cost: when Retro shipped a required onboarding video with only a tap-and-hold-to-skip gesture, Rafa reports "I think we lost like 20% or 30% of signups for people, you know, who would see the video first" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 417-419). The fix was an easy skip, not cutting the video — decide what the feeling is worth before you ship the friction, and know the number that friction can cost.

## Signature Moves

- **Sell the Idea, Not the Screen**: Turn a feature into an ad, short video, or demo that gives context and emotion before showing mechanics.
- **Feeling Before Feature**: Name the emotional target before choosing UI, animation, copy, or scope.
- **First-Run Stage Setting**: Treat onboarding and first impressions as memory formation, not orientation.
- **One-Step-Ahead Magic**: Create moments where the product seems to anticipate the user without hiding control.
- **Tiny Signature Detail**: Add a small, ownable, emotionally specific detail that users notice and remember.
- **Scope as Charm Protection**: Reject additions that make the product less legible, even when they are useful.
- **Prototype in Context**: Show product ideas on-device, in motion, with pacing, narration, and a human face where useful.
- **Feeling Library**: Save remembered details from apps, games, films, launch videos, stores, and personal experiences as raw material.

The anchor move ("Sell the Idea, Not the Screen") is not a metaphor — it is Rafa's literal production advice: "Make an ad, right? Sell this idea." (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 10 and 1027-1029, said twice in the same conversation).

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Exemplar 1: First MacBook Setup as Memory Formation
- **Context**: Rafa remembers opening his first MacBook and seeing the first-run video with stars and words: "you open the laptop and you see that video with the stars and the words. It was like a whole experience" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 31-33).
- **What It Reveals**: The setup was not just configuration. It set the emotional frame for the whole machine.
- **Calibration Rule**: Onboarding should make the user feel what kind of world they have entered.

### Exemplar 2: Make an Ad for the Feature
- **Context**: Rafa says that when you have a feature idea, do not just share mockups or a screen recording. Make an ad. Sell the idea: "don't just design the feature and share mock-ups. Don't just make a prototype and do a screen recording of you using the feature. Make an ad, right? Sell this idea." (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 6-10).
- **What It Reveals**: Designers are always selling ideas. The artifact must carry context, stakes, and feeling.
- **Calibration Rule**: If a feature needs advocacy, the deliverable should be a story artifact, not only a UI artifact.

### Exemplar 3: Hand Mirror Scope and Charm
- **Context**: Hand Mirror began as a one-button menu-bar camera check, built to replace Rafa's own habit of opening Photo Booth via Raycast before video calls. It was so minimal that Apple rejected the first App Store submission: "it got rejected because it said it's too simple of an app. I need to add more stuff" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 1320-1322). Rafa shipped it as a website download instead, and it spread on its own: "the verge picked it up and wrote about it and then it blew up" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 1330-1331). Only after that did he add an about page, settings, and credits — the minimum Apple required, not a feature roadmap.
- **What It Reveals**: Simple products can earn affection and press attention through restraint and a few charm details, not endless feature expansion — restraint was so extreme here it initially failed a platform's own bar for "enough app."
- **Calibration Rule**: Add only what intensifies the core feeling or makes the product more itself; let outside pressure (a store policy, not the user) be the reason you add anything more.

### Anti-Exemplar: The Useful But Anonymous Feature
- **What Mediocre Looks Like**: A clean feature with competent UI, generic copy, a static mockup, and no felt reason to care.
- **Why It Fails**: It asks the audience to infer the feeling, context, and value on their own. It often reaches for the label instead of the mechanism — Rafa's own caution: "I know like surprise and delight. It's a very overused term nowadays, but I'm just going to use it again cuz I don't know what else to call it" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 217-220). Naming the vibe is not designing the vibe.

## Quality Standard

Use [references/quality-rubric.md](references/quality-rubric.md). The output passes only if it:

- Names the exact feeling and memory.
- Produces a finished artifact, not advice.
- Uses product context, pacing, and story.
- Adds charm without gimmickry.
- Protects usability and scope.
- Includes a signature detail or explains why none should be added.
- Makes the product easier to understand and easier to remember.

Rafa's own bar for "finished, not advice": "Ultimately, it's that make it entertaining. They're paying attention. Like they want to consume this. They want to see this. It's it's funny. It's cool. Make something that it's interesting, funny, and hopefully communicates the idea very well." (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 1016-1022). A deliverable that only explains, without being entertaining or felt, has not met the standard.

## Anti-Patterns (Sourced)

- **Selling a feature with a static mockup or screen recording instead of a persuasive artifact.** Rafa's own standard: "don't just design the feature and share mock-ups. Don't just make a prototype and do a screen recording of you using the feature. Make an ad, right? Sell this idea." (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 6-10). A mockup shows mechanics; it never argues for the feeling.
- **Forcing users through a full-length onboarding video with no easy exit.** At Retro, the shipped onboarding video only allowed a tap-and-hold-to-skip gesture; Rafa: "I think we lost like 20% or 30% of signups for people, you know, who would see the video first" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 417-419). Easing the skip recovered signups without cutting the video for people who stayed.
- **Obeying UX orthodoxy past its expiration date** — avoid scroll, add pagination, label every control. Rafa names this directly: "don't make users scroll or whatever. Don't like labels everywhere" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 533-535) — rules inherited from a less computer-literate era, still applied to users who now scroll and swipe without instruction.
- **Over-animating the first pass instead of subtracting to the felt rhythm.** Rafa's own slideshow prototype: "My first take was like way too much motion and I had like a little Ken Burn effect and all that stuff and I realized like it's too much" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, lines 645-648) — he cut per-photo transitions entirely and kept one slow zoom across the whole sequence.
- **A talking-head walkthrough with no pacing device.** Rafa on plain narration: "If it's just talking, it's more boring" (`extractions/rafa-conde/transcript.txt`, line 1002) — a soundtrack cue, a scene change, or a joke every beat or two is what keeps a demo watched instead of skipped.
