# Wang Big-Questions Frame — Name Them, Then Answer Them (Dan Wang)

Wang's hedge-fund clients in 2021 were not asking him to summarize the year. They were asking three specific things, urgently, with money on the line: *Is the tech crackdown the end of Chinese capitalism or a reset? How does zero-COVID actually resolve? What does "common prosperity" mean for the companies we hold?* Those three questions were the live tensions of that moment — and Wang's letter earned its existence because it did not merely *catalogue* them. It *answered* them, imperfectly, with a position you could disagree with. That is the whole device. Most analysis surveys the open questions and calls it rigor; Wang's instruction is the opposite: "let's not just try to outline what the important pertinent tractable questions are. Let's also try to have an answer." This is the writing he calls "draft 1.5 of history" — past the daily-news first draft, short of the historian's settled final one. This workflow runs *before* you write a word of the piece: it forces you to name the 2-3 questions your smartest reader is actually preoccupied with right now, and stake a real answer to each — so the piece has a reason to exist instead of being one more survey of things people already know are uncertain.

## Pre-Flight

Read before executing — load these `genius.md` sections (do not paraphrase from memory; the patterns are precise, and this workflow's whole value is the discipline of *answering*, not surveying):
- **Pattern 17 — Big-Questions-First Framing** — the core engine: name the 2-3 live tensions, then *commit to answering*, not outlining. "Draft 1.5 of history." The conversational-reality test (what *I* want from the world meets what *the world* wants from me).
- **Pattern 18 — Genre-Incentive Mapping** — what your format's gravity pulls toward (the airport book, tenure-padding, the daily take) and how the big-questions frame is the escape from it. The questions worth answering are usually the ones the genre's incentives push you to avoid.
- **Decision Framework Q1 (the big question) and Q7 (escaping the genre's pull)** — the two gates this workflow exists to pass before drafting.
- **AN-4 — The Single-Expert / Insight-Porn Formula** — the failure this guards against: a piece sourced from one voice running one repeatable take, or surveying open questions to *look* rigorous without ever staking a position you could be wrong about.

> **🔒 Pre-Flight Gate**: run the **Decision Framework** in `genius.md § Decision Framework` before executing. Specifically confirm **Q1** (you can name the 2-3 tensions actually animating *this* audience right now — not the evergreen ones, the *this-period* ones — and you are prepared to *answer*, not just survey) and **Q7** (you have named what your format usually rewards into mediocrity, and the big questions are chosen to escape that pull, not to feed it). If you cannot name a single live question your smartest reader is arguing about this period, **do not draft** — the piece has no reason to exist yet; go back to your notes (the year's accumulation) until the live tensions surface. A piece that answers no question nobody was asking is the definition of content no one waited for.

## Input Required

- **Field / subject** — the domain the piece lives in (China-tech, AI labs, a company's year, a market, a craft, a culture). Be specific: "the AI-coding-tools market in mid-2026," not "AI."
- **The audience, named precisely** — *who* is the smartest reader you're writing for, and what is on the line for them? Wang's were hedge-fund clients with capital deployed. Yours might be operators deciding where to bet, founders choosing a wedge, peers who'll judge whether you actually thought. The questions are *theirs*, not yours — name them concretely.
- **The period** — what window is this? ("This quarter," "since the model release," "the year just ending.") Big questions are time-stamped; the tensions of *now* are different from the evergreen ones.
- **Your raw material** — the year's notes, the reading, the travel, the conversations, the data (Pattern 5 / Pattern 20: the accumulation the deadline now forces into synthesis). What have you actually been collecting? The answers must be staked from *this*, not from training-memory consensus.
- **Format** — annual letter / Substack essay / strategy memo / LinkedIn post / newsletter (drives how many questions you can carry and how the genre's gravity will try to pull you off the staked answer).
- **Your distinctive lens** (the conversational-reality input) — what do *you* see in this field that others miss? Your outsider distance, your productive disappointment, the thing only you would say. This meets the audience's big question; both are required, or the piece is either joyless (only their question, none of you) or delusional (only your lens, no live question anyone's asking).

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Name the live tensions, not the evergreen ones (Pattern 17)

Before anything, list every question you *could* write about in this field. Then ruthlessly cut to the ones that are **live this period** — the tensions your smartest reader is *arguing about right now*, where reasonable, informed people genuinely disagree, and where the answer is not yet settled. This is the difference between a question and a topic.

Audit every candidate against the table. A real big question passes all four columns; a topic-in-disguise fails at least one.

| A real big question must be… | Why | Pass / Fail test |
|---|---|---|
| **Live this period** (not evergreen) | Wang's 2021 questions were specific to *that* year's crackdown, *that* year's COVID policy — not "is China rising?" | Would this question have read identically three years ago? If yes, it's a topic, not a live tension. |
| **Genuinely contested** | Insight lives where smart people disagree; consensus questions have no staked answer worth reading | Can you name a credible person who holds the *opposite* view? If no one disagrees, there's nothing to stake. |
| **The audience's, with stakes** | The hedge-fund client asks because capital is on the line; the question must matter to *them*, not just interest you | Whose decision changes based on the answer? Name the person and the decision. |
| **Tractable** (Wang: "tractable questions") | "What is consciousness?" is live and contested but you cannot stake an answer; pick questions you can actually move | Can you write a real, falsifiable position on it in a paragraph? If it's unanswerable, it's a seminar, not a letter. |

Cut to **2-3**. Not one (too thin — one question is a take, not a frame), not five (the genre's pull toward survey is winning; you're cataloguing again). Wang carried three in the 2021 letter for a reason: enough to map the moment's tension, few enough to *answer* each with real weight.

```
LIVE BIG QUESTIONS (2-3, time-stamped to this period):
  Q1: __________
  Q2: __________
  Q3 (optional): __________
For each: who is arguing the other side? __________   ·   whose decision turns on it? __________
```

### Step 2 — Apply the conversational-reality test (Pattern 17, the David Whyte input)

Each question must sit at the intersection of two things, or the piece fails in a predictable direction. Wang frames it through David Whyte's "conversational reality": **what I want from the world** (your distinctive lens — the thing only you would say) meeting **what the world wants from me** (the live question the audience is actually asking). Both, or the piece is broken.

| If you have… | …without… | The piece becomes | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The audience's question** | your distinctive lens | **Joyless** — a competent survey anyone could write; you've answered the question but added no *you*. The insight-porn risk (AN-4): one consensus take, well-formatted. | Add your outsider angle, your productive disappointment, the contrarian read only your vantage gives. |
| **Your distinctive lens** | a live audience question | **Delusional** — a beautiful answer to a question no one is asking; self-indulgent, no reason to exist. | Tether the lens to a question someone with stakes is actually arguing about. |
| **Both** | — | The reason the piece exists | Proceed. This is the conversational reality — your seeing meets their need. |

For each question from Step 1, write the one-line version of *what only you would say* about it. If you can't — if your answer is the consensus answer — you don't have a reason to write yet. Go back to your notes (Step 1's raw material) and find the friction (genius Pattern 4) between the official story and what you actually observed.

### Step 3 — Stake an answer to each (the move that separates this from a survey)

This is the step everyone skips and the step that *is* the workflow. For each question, write a **real, falsifiable answer** — a position someone could disagree with, that you'd defend, that commits you. Not "it depends," not "time will tell," not a balanced both-sides paragraph. Wang's whole instruction: "let's also try to have an answer."

The answer is "draft 1.5 of history" — and the table below is what that altitude means in practice. You are deliberately writing *between* two registers, escaping the gravity of both (this is Pattern 18 in miniature).

| Register | What it does | Why Wang rejects it as the *whole* job |
|---|---|---|
| **Draft 1** (daily news / the hot take) | reports what just happened, fast | too close to the event — no synthesis, no contingency, just reaction. The Substack/Twitter pull toward ever-shorter, ever-faster takes. |
| **Draft 1.5** (Wang's altitude) | **stakes a synthesized, contingent answer while the dust is still settling** | this is the target — committed enough to be wrong, considered enough to be earned |
| **Draft 2** (the historian's final word) | the settled retrospective, all uncertainty resolved | too late, too safe — and dishonest about how contingent the moment actually felt (genius Pattern 19: just-so smoothness). You don't have it yet, and pretending you do is hindsight bias. |

For each answer, force the contingency (Pattern 19): name what would have to be true for you to be *wrong*, and what was genuinely uncertain at the moment of writing. An answer that admits no way of being wrong isn't a staked position — it's a hedge wearing a position's clothes.

```
For each question:
  STAKED ANSWER (a position, not a survey): __________
  WHAT WOULD MAKE ME WRONG: __________
  WHAT'S GENUINELY UNCERTAIN RIGHT NOW (the contingency I'm honest about): __________
```

### Step 4 — Map the genre's gravity, then write against it (Pattern 18, Q7)

Your format has a pull. Name it, because the pull is *toward not answering* — toward the safe survey, the hedge, the catalogue of open questions that lets you look rigorous without risking a position. The big-questions frame is the escape, but only if you name what you're escaping.

| Format | The genre's gravity pulls toward… | How the big-questions frame escapes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade / annual letter** | the airport book — broad, unserious, written to be agreeable to everyone | stake answers a serious reader could *disagree* with; agreeableness is the tell of the airport book |
| **Academic / analyst report** | tenure-padding — defensive hedging, "further research needed," no position | the staked answer *is* the position the report refuses to take; that's why it's read |
| **Substack / Twitter / LinkedIn** | ever-shorter, ever-more-frequent, ever-hotter takes (Draft 1 forever) | radical infrequency (Pattern 20) — one considered piece that answers, not ten reactive ones that survey |
| **Strategy memo / internal** | covering-your-ass survey of options with no recommendation | name the call; the memo exists to make a decision, not to list the decision's inputs |

Write one line: *the lazy version of this piece in my format would ___, and I am writing against that by ___.* If you can't name the lazy default, you'll drift into it.

### Step 5 — Write the confidence-honesty note (the Wang signature)

Wang has been "embarrassed three times a day before breakfast" — his phrase for the honest analyst's relationship to his own confidence. A staked answer without a confidence note is bravado; a confidence note without a staked answer is cowardice. You need both: commit to the position *and* label how sure you are, so the reader can weight it and you stay honest about contingency (Pattern 19).

For each staked answer, attach a confidence label and a one-line *why*:

| Confidence | Means | The honesty it preserves |
|---|---|---|
| **High conviction** | I'd bet on this; the ground truth I've observed is unambiguous | earns the reader's trust where you're actually sure |
| **Considered lean** | I lean this way but a smart person could take the other side; here's the swing factor | the honest middle — most "draft 1.5" answers live here |
| **Staked but unsure** | I'm committing to a position *because* surveying would be cowardice, but I expect to be partly wrong | the "embarrassed before breakfast" register — the honesty that makes the conviction credible |

The note is not a hedge. "Staked but unsure" still *stakes* — it just tells the reader the weather. The failure mode is using the confidence note to retreat into "it depends." Commit, then calibrate; never use the calibration to avoid committing.

### Step 6 — Final gate: read it as your smartest reader (Q1 + Q7)

Read the questions and staked answers cold, as the hedge-fund-client equivalent for your field — the person with stakes who already knows the basics and will judge whether you actually thought.

- **The "I already knew that" test:** would your smartest reader learn a *position* they didn't have, or just see their own open questions restated? If it's restatement, you surveyed; go back to Step 3 and stake harder.
- **The "I could disagree with this" test:** can a credible reader push back on each answer? If no one could disagree, you didn't stake anything — you wrote consensus.
- **The conversational-reality test (Step 2):** is *you* in it (your lens) *and* is it answering *their* question (the live tension)? Joyless or delusional means one half is missing.
- **The genre-escape test (Q7):** does it read as the lazy default your format produces, or as the piece that escaped the pull? If it slots neatly into the airport-book / survey-report / hot-take bin, rebuild against the gravity.

## Content Type Adaptations

| Format | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Annual letter / cornerstone essay** | Native habitat. Carry all 2-3 questions; each gets a full staked answer with contingency and confidence note. This *is* the spine of the piece — the questions become its sections, the staked answers its argument. Radical infrequency (Pattern 20): this is the one piece a year that earns the wait *because* it answers what the feed only surveyed. |
| **Substack / newsletter essay** | Usually 1-2 questions (the format can't carry three with full weight). Lead with the most contested one; stake the answer in the first third so the reader knows you're not surveying. The confidence note can be a single honest aside, not a section. |
| **Strategy memo / analyst note** | The staked answer *is* the recommendation. One or two questions max — a memo with three open questions and no call is the tenure-padding failure (Pattern 18). The confidence label maps directly to "how hard should we act on this." |
| **LinkedIn / social post** | One question, one staked answer, one confidence note — compressed. The frame is the hook: name the live tension everyone's circling, then stake the position they're afraid to. The genre's gravity (the hot take) is escaped by *answering* where everyone else is reacting. One device only; at this length the survey-vs-stake distinction is the entire post. |
| **Ghostwriting (operator's letter)** | The questions are the *operator's* market's live tensions; the staked answers are *their* convictions, in their voice. Your job is to surface the questions they're actually being asked and force the stake — operators default to safe surveys in public; the value is making them commit, honestly labeled. |

### Worked example A — Annual letter, AI-labs analyst (mid-2026)

**Field:** the frontier-AI-lab competitive landscape · **Audience:** institutional allocators and founders deciding where to build, capital on the line · **Period:** the year since reasoning-models became table-stakes.

> **Q1: Is the moat capability or distribution?**
> *Who argues the other side:* the capability camp (the best model wins) vs. the distribution camp (the best model is a commodity in 18 months). *Whose decision turns on it:* every allocator choosing between a lab bet and an application bet.
> *What only I would say:* I've watched three labs ship a clearly-better model and lose the quarter anyway — the ground truth is that capability leads are now measured in weeks, and weeks don't amortize a moat.
> ▸ **STAKED ANSWER:** Distribution is the moat; capability is rent. The lab that owns the workflow, not the benchmark, compounds.
> *What would make me wrong:* a capability discontinuity large enough that no application layer can close it (a genuine step-change, not a benchmark bump). *Genuinely uncertain:* whether the next architecture is incremental or discontinuous — nobody knows, and anyone who says they do is selling. *Confidence:* **Considered lean** — because the last four model releases each compressed to commodity faster than the one before, but four data points in a discontinuous field is thin.

*Why this is Wang, not a survey:* it names a live, contested, tractable question allocators are actually arguing; stakes a position you could disagree with (distribution > capability); grounds it in observed ground truth ("I've watched three labs…") rather than consensus; admits exactly what would falsify it; and labels the confidence honestly. It is draft 1.5 — not a hot take ("X lab is winning!"), not a settled history ("the moat was always distribution"), but a committed read while the dust is still in the air.

### Worked example B — LinkedIn post, S&C-coaching-for-brands operator (the user's lane)

**Field:** wellness/fitness brand content strategy · **Audience:** founders of performance brands deciding whether to hire creators or build in-house · **Period:** this quarter, post the creator-rate inflation.

> **Q (one, compressed):** Is hiring fitness creators still worth it now that their rates doubled and their reach halved?
> *What only I would say:* I've run the numbers on both sides — the brands winning right now aren't buying reach, they're buying *credibility transfer*, and that's a different math.
> ▸ **STAKED ANSWER:** Stop buying creators for reach; buy the two or three whose audience would *defend* your product, and ignore the follower count entirely. *What would make me wrong:* if your category is pure impulse-buy where reach genuinely is the lever. *Genuinely uncertain:* where the line falls between "credibility category" and "impulse category." *Confidence:* **Staked but unsure** — committing because "it depends" is the coward's post, but I've only tested this in supplements and apparel.

*Why this is Wang, not a hot take:* the genre's gravity (LinkedIn) pulls toward the reactive both-sides take ("creators: worth it or not? Here's my framework!"). This escapes it by *answering* — staking a contrarian position (ignore follower count) the audience is afraid to commit to — grounding it in observed ground truth, and labeling confidence honestly rather than performing certainty. One question, fully staked, beats five surveyed.

## Quality Gate

> **🛡️ Anti-Pattern Check**: review output against `genius.md § Dan Wang Would Never... (Anti-Patterns)` — especially **AN-4 (Single-Expert / Insight-Porn Formula)** and **AN-7 (Just-So Smoothness / Hindsight Bias)** — and § Decision Framework Q1 and Q7. Flag and fix before delivering.

- **Questions are live, not evergreen (Q1):** each question is time-stamped to *this* period and would have read differently three years ago. An evergreen question ("is X rising?") is a topic in disguise — recut.
- **Each question is genuinely contested:** you can name a credible person who holds the opposite view. If no one disagrees, there's no answer worth staking — drop the question or find the real tension under it.
- **Every question has a STAKED answer, not a survey:** there is a falsifiable position you'd defend, with a named "what would make me wrong." A "it depends" / both-sides paragraph is the failure this entire workflow exists to prevent. If the answer admits no way of being wrong, it isn't staked.
- **Draft 1.5 altitude, not Draft 1 or Draft 2:** the answers are synthesized and contingent — past the hot take, short of the false-certainty retrospective. No hindsight-bias smoothness (AN-7): the genuine uncertainty of the moment is preserved, not erased.
- **The conversational-reality test passes (Step 2):** your distinctive lens *and* the audience's live question are both present. Joyless (no you) or delusional (no live question) means rebuild — name which half is missing.
- **The genre's gravity is named and escaped (Q7, Pattern 18):** you stated the lazy default your format produces and how you wrote against it. If the output slots neatly into the airport-book / survey-report / hot-take bin, the gravity won.
- **Confidence is labeled, honestly (the "embarrassed before breakfast" gate):** each staked answer carries a confidence label that calibrates without retreating. "Staked but unsure" still stakes; it never collapses into "it depends." Bravado (stake, no calibration) and cowardice (calibration used to avoid staking) both fail.
- **Grounded in real material, not consensus (AN-4):** the answers come from *your* notes / observation / ground truth, not from restating what the field already agrees on. If the staked answer is the consensus answer, you have insight-porn, not insight — find the friction.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Surveying the questions instead of answering them.** Listing the open questions with care and nuance, feeling rigorous, and never staking a position — the single most common failure, because surveying feels safe and looks smart. **Recovery:** re-run Step 3 for every question. Force a falsifiable answer and a "what would make me wrong." If you genuinely can't stake one, the question is unanswerable (drop it for a tractable one) or you don't have the material yet (go back to your notes). Wang: "let's *also* try to have an answer" — the "also" is non-negotiable.

- **Picking evergreen questions because they're safer to answer.** "Is China rising?" / "Is AI overhyped?" — questions so broad that any answer is defensible and therefore worthless. They dodge the risk of being specifically wrong. **Recovery:** Step 1's table — time-stamp every question to *this period* and name who's arguing the other side *right now*. If it would've read identically three years ago, it's a topic; find the live tension the period actually produced.

- **Confidence note as escape hatch.** Using "staked but unsure" or a confidence label to walk back the position into a hedge — "I think X, but really it could be anything." That's cowardice wearing honesty's clothes. **Recovery:** Step 5 — the label calibrates *how sure*, it never reduces *whether you committed*. Read the answer with the confidence note attached: does it still say a thing a person could disagree with? If the note dissolved the stake, you've retreated; re-stake, then re-label.

- **Your lens with no live question (the delusional failure).** Writing the brilliant contrarian take on something no one with stakes is actually asking about — self-indulgent, no reason to exist (the travel-writing failure transposed to analysis). **Recovery:** Step 2's conversational-reality table — tether every lens to a question someone with a decision on the line is genuinely arguing. If you can't name whose decision turns on it (Step 1), the question isn't live; the lens has nowhere to land.
