## Role & Activation

This is a cross-expert stack. Anne Lamott captures raw material — the rag bag of scraps, threads, and silk — teaching the writer to notice widely. Wright Thompson teaches how to make one detail do the work of fifty, deploying with surgical selectivity. This stack sources wide (Lamott) then deploys narrow (Wright Thompson): the result is writing dense with observation but never cluttered. You can't deploy details you don't have — Lamott fills the well, Wright Thompson draws from it with precision.

## Input Required

1. **[PROJECT]** — what's being built toward
2. **[SUBJECT/SETTING]** — the person, place, or situation at the center
3. **[ACCESS LEVEL]** — observing firsthand, from memory, or from research

**Pre-Flight Gate**: the writer needs at least 48 hours between Phase 1 (Lamott sourcing) and Phase 2 (Wright deployment). Observation must settle before selection — do not rush this gap.

## Execution Protocol

### Phase 1 — Lamott Sourcing (Wide Net)
Run the full Observation Bank Pipeline:
1. Observation walk: minimum 10 minutes in or near the subject's world. If writing about a person, visit their neighborhood; if an event, visit the venue empty.
2. Eavesdropping station: if people are involved, listen to how they talk — capture syntax, vocabulary, rhythm.
3. Memory trawl: what's already known about this subject from the writer's own life? Use "There was a tree" as a prompt if needed.
4. Bank target: 25+ observations, tagged and categorized. Be greedy — capture everything. Only ~10% will end up used.

### Phase 2 — Wright Thompson Selection (Narrow Focus)
Switch experts. Apply Wright's detail economy.
1. Detail audit: spread all 25+ observations out. For each, ask Wright's question — does this detail do the work of fifty, or does it just describe?
2. Sort into three categories:
   - **Load-Bearing**: reveals character, sets mood, AND advances the story simultaneously — these stay.
   - **Atmospheric**: sets mood without additional work — keep 1-2, cut the rest.
   - **Decorative**: exists because the writer liked it — cut all.
3. Wright test for each load-bearing candidate: if this detail is removed, does the scene lose something the reader can't get anywhere else? If yes, essential. If the reader could infer it from context, cut it.
4. Detail stacking: place the selected detail at a structural pressure point — details hit harder at transitions, revelations, and endings than in descriptive passages.

### Phase 3 — Integration
Write or rewrite the piece deploying the selected details.
1. Density rule: maximum 2 load-bearing details per page — more creates clutter. A detail's power is inversely proportional to how many surround it.
2. Placement: details at scene openings set mood; at climax points they amplify emotion; at endings they create residue.
3. Subtext: the best details say one thing on the surface and mean something else underneath — the reader should feel the weight without being told what to feel.
4. Polaroid check: reread after placing the details. Do new associations surface that weren't consciously intended? If yes, consider incorporating them — the subconscious is still working.

## Output Contract

1. **Full Observation Bank** — the 25+ Lamott-sourced observations, preserved for future use
2. **Selected Details** — the 5-8 load-bearing details chosen for this piece
3. **Deployed Draft** — the piece with details placed at structural pressure points
4. **Detail Map** — where each selected detail lives in the piece and what work it does

## Quality Gate

1. Were at least 25 raw observations captured (Lamott standard)?
2. Did no more than 30% of observations make it into the draft (Wright standard of ruthless selection)?
3. Does each load-bearing detail carry character + mood + story simultaneously?
4. Is density discipline held — max 2 load-bearing details per page?
5. Does at least one image from the piece remain visible in the mind hours after reading (residue test)?

## Creative Latitude

The 48-hour gap between sourcing and selection is not a formality — genuine distance changes which observations still feel charged, and that distance is where taste operates. When selecting, favor the detail that surprises even the writer over the one that most obviously "fits" the theme; obvious fit is often decorative. The subtext layer in Phase 3 is where the real craft lives: a detail should be choosable BECAUSE it means something the writer hasn't stated outright, not despite it. If the piece starts to feel like a catalog of beautiful observations, Wright was underdeployed — cut harder. If it feels sparse and cold, Lamott's warmth got edited out — restore at least one detail purely for feeling, not function.

## Deploy When

- Building a long-form piece that needs both richness and economy
- A draft has good structure but feels thin, needing sensory weight
- The writer wants the kind of detail that unlocks memory in a reader, not just describes a scene
