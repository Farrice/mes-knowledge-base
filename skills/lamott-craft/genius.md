# Anne Lamott — Genius Context

## How to Use This Skill (Model Calibration)

The patterns below are intuition primitives, not a checklist. Absorb them, then write originally. If the output mechanically applies every pattern in order, you have failed. The test: would Anne Lamott recognize this as a relational practice — or as someone going through writing motions? If unsure, the answer is the second — rewrite.

Specifically:
- Do NOT enumerate which patterns you applied unless asked.
- Do NOT label sections by pattern name ("here's the rag bag move," "here's the Draft 2 pass").
- Do NOT perform vulnerability — be it. A narrator who announces "I'm being vulnerable now" has already failed her own test.
- Lamott's craft is the inverse of mechanical: presence, attention, conversation. Apply mechanically and you produce its opposite — the over-controlled, "showoffy" prose she spent 45 years teaching writers out of.
- Her texture is spoken, self-deprecating, funny about serious things ("carbonated holiness," "poopy first draft," "Nana, that was terrible") — never academic, never MFA-workshop language. If your output could double as a craft lecture, it's wrong.
- Polish is the tell. The moment prose sounds accomplished, ostentatious, or literary, it has failed her own test: "if it's literary, it isn't." Nickel words, not 25-cent words. A confused reader is an antagonistic reader.

Why this is here: capable models follow instructions more literally than the craft they're modeling can tolerate. Lamott's entire methodology resists technique-as-checklist — checklist execution produces exactly the over-controlled writing she teaches against.

## Core Genius
Writing is a relational practice, not a technical one. Your relationship with your inner critic, your material, your attention, and your reader determines output quality more than any technique. Lamott treats every stage of writing as a *conversation* — with the work, with yourself, with the world showing itself to you.

## What Makes Her Different
Every other writing expert in the roster operates from technique (Connelly = surgical detail, Wright = journalistic precision, Roth = cinematic erosion, Pressfield = warrior discipline). Lamott is the only one who operates from *tenderness*. She doesn't fight the creative process — she befriends it. This isn't softness — it's a harder, more sustainable method. She's been publishing for 45+ years. The warriors burn out. The tender ones endure.

## Genius Patterns

### 1. The Rag Bag Guy (Continuous Material Sourcing)
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Treats her subconscious as a character — a little Dr. Seuss-like figure who collects scraps of fabric, thread, dental floss, tinsel, silk. The writer's job is to *feed* the rag bag guy by paying attention. When you're ready, the rag bag guy lifts the assembled material to you.
- **Executable Behavior**: Stop trying to "have experiences." Start noticing — a color, a phrase overheard in the express line, the slightly greenish yellow inside an orchid's center. Write it down (phone notes, index card). You may use it, you may not. Writing it makes it almost indelible.
- **Deployment Context**: Any time you feel "empty" or blocked. The problem is never a lack of experiences — it's a lack of *attention*.
- **Success Metric**: You have 20+ captured observations before you sit down to write anything.

### 2. The Inner Critic as Character (Dialogic Protocol)
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Gives the inner critic a face, a voice, a history. Hers is a specific personality from NYC publishing who esteemed only a certain kind of elitist white male writing. By *seeing* the critic, she can *talk to* it.
- **Executable Behavior**: (1) Notice where the critic lives in your body (head, torso). (2) Bring it forward into your hand. (3) Ask it: "Who hired you?" "When did I first hire you?" "What are you afraid of?" (4) Thank it for keeping you alive as a child. (5) Say: "I'd like to take over. Go to the library. I'll come get you if I need an ethical consultation."
- **Deployment Context**: Before any writing session. When self-doubt flares. When perfectionism prevents starting.
- **Success Metric**: You can *see* the critic's face and speak to it without being consumed by it.

### 3. The Three-Draft Personality System
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Gives each draft a distinct personality and emotional relationship:
  - **Draft 1 — The Child's Draft** (poopy first draft): Write everything. All terrible. Way too long. No rules. Permission to be awful.
  - **Draft 2 — The Adult Draft**: "Firm but friendly" (Dr. Spock's advice for two-year-olds). "I like this description, but we're going to use it somewhere else" = we're cutting it. Find stronger verbs. Remove the boring stuff. Remove the parts that make you look good.
  - **Draft 3 — The Dental Draft**: Go tooth by tooth. Wiggle and jiggle. Floss. Some teeth need attention. Some are fine. Move on.
- **Executable Behavior**: Never edit while drafting. Never draft while editing. Each pass has its own *personality* and its own rules.
- **Deployment Context**: Any writing project — essays, chapters, scripts, posts.
- **Success Metric**: Your first draft is embarrassingly long and bad. Your final draft is 1/3 the length and every word earns its place.

### 4. The Anti-Literary Filter
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Immediate pattern recognition for "trying to sound literary." If it's literary, it isn't. If you're using 25-cent words instead of nickel words, you lack confidence. A confused reader is an antagonistic reader. If I have to look up one of your words, I'm on to you.
- **Executable Behavior**: Read every sentence and ask: "Am I trying to impress someone with this?" If yes, kill it. Use 5-cent words. Write the way people actually think and talk. The test: would Shirley Jackson call this reader-friendly?
- **Deployment Context**: Draft 2 and Draft 3 editing. Any time prose feels "elevated" or "accomplished."
- **Success Metric**: Zero moments where the reader notices the writing instead of the story.

### 5. Writer's Block as Misdiagnosis
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Reframes every instance of "block" as a different problem: emptiness, pressure, fear, perfectionism, or simply not knowing what happens next. Then applies the matching solution, not a generic "push through."
- **Executable Behavior**: Three dissolution tools:
  - **Skip Ahead**: Jump to page 50 where you DO know something — even if it's just "they've gotten off the elevator and their faces have changed." Work backward from there.
  - **Introduce Someone New**: Shake the snow globe. A character who knows both people. A stranger who changes the dynamics. Something that wasn't there at the point of blockage.
  - **"I Don't Know"**: Give yourself permission to not know. "How do these two people who've hated each other for 30 years end up with small smiles? I don't know. But I can pick it up there."
- **Deployment Context**: Any time forward progress stops. Do NOT fight through — dissolve.
- **Success Metric**: You're writing again within 15 minutes of deploying a dissolution tool.

### 6. The Polaroid Development Method
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Treats early drafts like a Polaroid photograph that hasn't fully developed yet. You think you're taking a picture of one thing, but as it develops, you notice other things — books under the couch, a coffee table book your dead father had, a Thelonious Monk song, his wife calling him "Melodious Thunk." The material reveals itself over time.
- **Executable Behavior**: Write what you *think* the scene is about. Then wait. Reread. Notice what else showed up. The real material is often the thing you didn't consciously put there.
- **Deployment Context**: After completing any first draft or scene. During revision when a piece feels "thin."
- **Success Metric**: You've discovered at least one element in your draft that you didn't consciously intend.

### 7. The Bookstore Audition (Opening Pages Diagnostic)
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Gives any piece exactly three pages. Looks for: (1) Pleasing sentences that aren't ostentatious. (2) A voice that's human, not literary. (3) A character who feels real — meaning they have something wrong with them. (4) Something at stake — sense of meaning, connection to others, survivable loss. (5) The desire to turn the page.
- **Executable Behavior**: Read your own first three pages as if you found the book in a store. Would you buy it? Where do you stop reading? That's where the problem is.
- **Deployment Context**: After completing any piece before sharing. During revision of opening pages.
- **Success Metric**: A reader genuinely wants to get to the bottom of the page AND turn it.

### 8. ABDCE Story Architecture
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Structures all narrative using Alice Adams' formula — not as a rigid template but as lily pads across a pond:
  - **A — Action**: Something happens early. We're together. Why? Are we interesting?
  - **B — Background**: Here's why we're here. What went before. Context through dialogue or flashback.
  - **D — Development**: A knock on the door. Footsteps that don't sound like yours. Things shift.
  - **C — Climax**: Where all the minor chords crash into each other. The cymbal. A killing — of a person, an illusion, a prejudice, a way of life.
  - **E — Ending**: Taking the reader out of the pond together. Not necessarily happy — but inevitable. "That's exactly right. That's the only thing that could have happened."
- **Deployment Context**: Structuring any prose piece — essay, chapter, short story, newsletter, memoir section.

### 9. The "Said Only" Dialogue Rule
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Rejects any attribution beyond "said." No "chuckled," "enthused," "proclaimed." Identifies character by rhythm and vocabulary, not attribution tags. Prefers patches of dialogue with NO tags at all — just quotation marks. Tests all dialogue by reading it aloud.
- **Executable Behavior**: Write dialogue. Read it aloud on voice memo. Cringe at what rings false. Cut everything that sounds like someone who had time to rewrite their sentences. Use what people's hands and mouths do physically instead of adverb-laden attribution.
- **Deployment Context**: Any dialogue scene in any format.
- **Success Metric**: Every line of dialogue could only have been said by that specific character.

### 10. Nickel Words Over Quarter Words
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Immediate word-economy assessment — if a word costs 25 cents (requires looking up), replace with the 5-cent version. Uses the thesaurus not for fancy words but for the *middle category* — words you know but don't currently use. "Wept" isn't fancy. It's precise.
- **Executable Behavior**: Three word categories in the thesaurus: (1) Know and use — your working vocabulary. (2) Don't know, don't use — these are showing off, avoid them. (3) Know but don't use — the gold. These are the subterranean words the thesaurus surfaces for you.
- **Deployment Context**: Verb strengthening in Draft 2. Any time prose feels flat OR too ornate.

### 11. "Everything Is Grist for the Mill"
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Converts every painful, embarrassing, or cruel experience into usable material. Her father taught her: when you decide to be a writer, everything can be used. The revenge principle — "You need no other reason to write than that the kids who teased you in seventh grade should eat it."
- **Executable Behavior**: Nothing is wasted. Change hair color and height for legal protection. Use direct quotes. The cruel things said to you are recognizable enemies to everyone. Pain is fuel, not paralysis.
- **Deployment Context**: Memoir, personal essays, character development in fiction, confessional content.

### 12. The Reverence Attention Practice
- **What She Does Unconsciously**: Treats attention as a spiritual practice. "Everything true and beautiful can be discovered on any 10-minute walk." Plays "I spy" — notice 5 things you've never noticed about a familiar block. "Laughter is carbonated holiness." Decides to be *available for wow*.
- **Executable Behavior**: Take a 10-minute walk. Notice 5 new things. Write them down. Ask: "How alive am I willing to be?" Take off armor. Become porous. Let the world show itself to you.
- **Deployment Context**: Before writing sessions. When feeling disconnected from material. When content feels mechanical.

## Anti-Patterns (Sourced)

- **Never substitute for "said."** Her own rule, verbatim from the source interview: "you can only say said. You can't say um Andrea chuckled" — and further, "if you start saying he chuckled, he enthused, he he proclaimed," you've traded the character's rhythm for author intrusion (`extractions/anne-lamott/transcript.txt`).
- **Never let prose "sound literary."** Verbatim from the transcript: "there's a rule in good writing about if it's literary, it's you can't use it... if you're trying to sound literary, take it out" — restated later in the same interview as "If it's literary, I'm not going to be interested in it" (`extractions/anne-lamott/transcript.txt`).
- **Never reach for the 25-cent word.** Her father's rule, quoted verbatim: "you used... five cent words, nickel words instead of 25 cent words" — a word the reader has to look up costs you their trust, per the Shirley Jackson line she cites in the same breath: "a confused reader is an antagonistic reader" (`extractions/anne-lamott/transcript.txt`).
- **Never write a sentence that's "showoffy."** Verbatim: "the sentences are pleasing. They're not ostentatious. They're not showoffy" — she names this "the Neil's rule," stated in the same breath as "If it's literary, it isn't" (`extractions/anne-lamott/transcript.txt`).
- **Never let a character talk to impress.** Verbatim: "somebody at a cocktail party who's just trying to impress you with their overeducation, then it is tiresome" — clipped, "snappy" dialogue only works when the character is warm and in a hurry, never when performing intellect (`extractions/anne-lamott/transcript.txt`).
- **Never let cleverness survive Draft 2 unexamined.** Her own scar: the 1980 *Hard Laughter* reviews, quoted exactly as the transcript's audio-to-text rendering has it, "whatever meager charms the book possessed were harmed by the writer show coffee overkill" (the source audio is degraded on this phrase — see `references/source-ledger.md` for why this is labeled LIKELY rather than VERIFIED on exact wording, though "show-offy overkill" is the evident intended phrase) — she reports she "no longer... was doing show was doing show off they overkill of trying to be funny" and started editing herself differently after that review (`extractions/anne-lamott/transcript.txt`, reviews dated 1980).

## Hidden Knowledge

### 1. The Emptiness Distinction
Writer's block isn't about the door — it's about what's behind it. When Isabelle Allende confided she had writer's block, Lamott told her she wasn't blocked — she was empty. "All the sand has escaped from the burlap sack." The solution isn't to try harder. It's to stop, get the pressure off, and fill up through *attention*, not experiences.

### 2. The Characters Know Who They Are
In fiction, the characters know who they are and what would happen naturally in their lives. The writer's job is to get out of the way so they can tug on your sleeve and say "I wouldn't do that." This is the opposite of plotting — it's *listening*.

### 3. The Show-Off Overkill Lesson
Lamott's first reviews (Hard Laughter, ~1980) said "whatever meager charms the book possessed were harmed by the writer's show-offy overkill." That criticism permanently changed her editing — she never again tried to be funny for its own sake. The scar became a quality filter.

### 4. The Sandwich Critique Method
When editing someone's work: positive → honest diagnosis → positive. "I love this. It's going to be great. I don't think your lead works. But at the bottom of page one when that paragraph starts — that's where you've hooked me." Endings are always "way before the ending you've presented." Essays: a page and a half earlier. Books: 13 pages earlier.

### 5. Clipped Dialogue as Red Flag
"Snappy" dialogue at a cocktail party is pelting with tiny ping-pong balls (Updike's image). Clipped dialogue works only when the character is warm, doing good, and in a hurry. When it's someone trying to impress you with their overeducation — tiresome.

### 6. The Copy Editor's Sin
The copy editor who changed "I'm going to have to pay you like in total dimes" to "I'm going to pay you totally in dimes" destroyed rhythm and blues in the name of grammar. The writer is the final arbiter. Trust your ear over the style manual.

### 7. Endings Live Earlier Than You Think
Good endings make sense in retrospect — "I might have known. I should have intuitively seen this coming." But there's also surprise where you smile and go "Yep, that's exactly right. That's the only thing that could have happened." The ending is a contract fulfilled.

### 8. The "There Was a Tree" Master Prompt
The single most generative writing prompt: "There was a tree." Period. Is it from childhood? The cherry tree outside your window? The tree you fell out of in second grade? The tree where you carved initials? The tree where you buried grandfather's ashes? You could write for days. The tree IS every single thing about life.

## Hall of Fame Exemplars

### Exemplar 1: The Irish Bartender Story
- **Context**: Lamott describing observation-in-action during a morning at an Irish tavern
- **The Example**: "We decided to stop into an Irish tavern to get the Irish breakfast... The bartender came over, very genial Irishman of about 60 who we could barely understand. We thought he was punking us. And when I saw that, I started laughing again... I asked for the Wi-Fi and it happened to be 147 Guinness. He started spelling Guinness for me. Two ends to it. And I said, 'You know, I'm a sober alcoholic. I've been sober for decades.' And then I really remember how to spell Guinness, believe me. And he laughs. Well, then Neil got up during our breakfast to go to the bathroom and the guy came over and he said kind of sotto voce, 'How long have you been sober?' And I said, 'I'm coming up on 40 years.' And he said, 'Wow, the third great prayer.' And then he reached in his pocket and he pulled out a 24-hour chip and a six-month chip."
- **What makes this excellent**: This IS the observation pipeline working. She noticed the incomprehensible accent, the Wi-Fi password, the sotto voce moment — and used the entire anecdote in the piece she was writing that morning. The "Track B" — the new pair of glasses — activated by paying attention to one genial bartender.

### Exemplar 2: The "Like in Total Dimes" Line
- **Context**: A scene from her novel Joe Jones, set in a dive cafe on the Petaluma River
- **The Example**: Two teenagers at the cash register: "I'm going to have to pay you like in total dimes."
- **What makes this excellent**: This is rhythm and blues. The syntax is "wrong" by copy-editor standards but *perfect* by ear. It's a captured observation — she heard it, said "wait one second," and wrote it down. The copy editor tried to "fix" it to "I'm going to pay you totally in dimes" — which kills the music. This exemplar teaches both observation capture AND the anti-literary filter in one line.

### Exemplar 3: The Middlemarch "Oh No" Moment
- **Context**: Lamott describing the reader-stopping power of George Eliot's Middlemarch
- **The Example**: "George who is in love with Mary... The father has accepted the proposal. George has already lost everything. He's young, full of life, keeps betting on the horses. The father has lent him money to get started. George has a good idea — he's going to take the money, say it's £1,000, go to the racetrack and double it. And you put down the book and you say, 'Don't go to the racetrack.' And you don't almost want to go on, but maybe it's going to work out this time."
- **What makes this excellent**: This demonstrates what "make me care" actually means — not through explanation but through the reader's visceral "Oh no." The character is recognizably human (we all have our racetrack). The stakes are clear. The reader becomes an active participant.

### Anti-Exemplar: The Literary Writer
- **What mediocre looks like**: A writer using 25-cent words, overly complex sentences, clipped witty dialogue, and showing-off education. The cocktail party writer who pelts you with tiny ping-pong balls. The writer whose first three pages make you think "this person is trying very hard."
- **Why it fails**: Violates the core Lamott principle — if it's literary, it isn't. A confused reader is an antagonistic reader. If I have to look up your words, I'm on to you that you don't have confidence.

## Signature Moves

- **"Stop Not Writing"**: The first instruction to any workshop. You don't start writing — you stop *not* writing. You stop waiting for conditions to improve. → **Deploy when**: Procrastination, perfectionism, condition-setting ("I'll write when...")

- **The Cold Water Plunge**: Getting into writing is getting into cold water — once you're in, you might as well paddle around. You warm up. → **Deploy when**: Opening any writing session. First 5 minutes of resistance.

- **"Kill Your Darlings in Draft 2"**: You write the brilliant, ironic, funny lines that make you look good — in Draft 1. You kill them in Draft 2. Not Draft 1. You need them to exist first so you can sacrifice them. → **Deploy when**: Draft 2 editing. Anytime you're in love with your own cleverness.

- **The Squint**: Close your eyes. There's a movie screen behind them. See the scene. Open your eyes. Squint to bring it back into focus. Bear down to capture it more precisely. → **Deploy when**: Starting any scene. Recapturing a fleeting image. Teaching anyone to visualize.

- **"Nana, That Was Terrible"**: Her grandson's verdict after her first kindergarten writing workshop — "You told us you could help us write a book, but you only taught us how to write one page." Her response: "Honey, that's all I got." → **Deploy when**: Anyone overwhelmed by the scale of a project. The answer is always: one page.

## Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Score 4 (Acceptable) | Score 7 (Good) | Score 10 (Savant) |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Sentence Quality** | Grammatically correct, clear | Pleasing — you want to get to the bottom of the paragraph | You turn the page involuntarily. MFK Fisher standard: one clean, fresh sentence with a period at the end |
| **Voice Authenticity** | Doesn't sound forced | Sounds like a real person | You forget you're reading. Could only be this writer. "Not ostentatious, not show-offy" |
| **Emotional Truth** | Some vulnerability | Real flaws in characters/narrator. "If you don't have anything wrong with you, I'm not interested" | The reader says "Oh no" and puts the book down — then picks it back up |
| **Word Economy** | No jargon | Nickel words. Strong verbs. No actually, no very | "Like in total dimes" — rhythm and blues in every sentence |
| **Dialogue** | Attributed correctly | Read-aloud test passed. No "chuckled," no "enthused" | You know who's speaking without tags. Character-specific rhythm and vocabulary |
| **Observation Density** | Some sensory details | Specific, earned details (the greenish yellow inside the orchid's center) | Details that unlock memory — Thelonious Monk, Melodious Thunk, Lulu's Back in Town |
| **Anti-Pretension** | No 25-cent words | No show-off moments. Nothing making you look good | The reader never notices the writing. They only notice the story |
