# Technical Craft — The 36 Rules Module

The "really real" north star is human contact, not virtuoso performance. This module holds the *sentence-level machinery* from the source that lets a writer earn that contact without showing off. It is the technical half of the package — Neal's side, the part that starts at the sentence — gap-diffed against `lamott-craft` and the rest of this skill so it adds only what was missing.

Everything below is anchored to the source video (`https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sfK6XVV0M74`, local extraction `extractions/anne-lamott-neal-allen-really-real/`). Quotes are paraphrase-safe excerpts from auto-derived captions — keep the timestamp, check exact wording against the audio before any formal publication.

**A note on the rules as rules.** Neal frames his 36 as rhetoric and persuasion, never right/wrong (00:08:53). They are not grammar police. Each one is a way of asking *will the reader want the next sentence, and do they feel accompanied while they read it.* Cite a numbered rule only when you mean it — the numbering is a memory aid, not an authority.

## Mechanic 1 — Economize to 25–30%

**The rule.** Cut roughly a quarter to a third out of a finished draft before anyone else sees it. Not "tighten" as a vibe — an actual percentage you can aim at.

**Source.** Anne, reading her own book back, used the rules on a too-long text and "probably took 30% out and I thought this is awesome" (00:24:59). Neal, on his own revision: "the second draft I write, I've cut out a quarter of the first draft" (00:28:09). The principle the cut serves is named explicitly: economize so you "show respect for the audience's time and desire for novelty" (00:24:21). Neal's gloss when asked what economizing is: "I've economized. I answered your question. It's time and respect for novelty" (00:26:06).

**Why it works.** The principle of restraint already lives in this skill (the Anti-Overproof Cut, "depth is often a compression problem"). What the percentage adds is a *measurable dial* so restraint stops being a feeling and becomes a pass. Most of the quarter you cut is the writer proving what the reader already understood — defensive explanation, the second version of a point, throat-clearing. Cutting it doesn't remove depth; it lets the reader participate, and it returns their time.

**How to apply it.**
- Mark the draft's word count. State the target: minus 25–30%.
- First pass removes whole sentences and beats — the over-proving, the second explanation, the apology, the wind-up before the real first line.
- Second pass removes "the little words" (see Mechanic 4 / read-aloud): *thing*, *just*, *really*, *very*, *actually*, *somewhat*, hedges, *in order to*, *the fact that*.
- If the cut feels like it's removing meaning, you cut too late — clarify the draft first, then omit (silence requires prior clarity).
- The test: after the cut, the surviving sentences should feel *closer*, not thinner.

**Failure mode.** Cutting to the number when the draft was vague to begin with. Then you've made fog shorter. Clarify, then economize.

## Mechanic 2 — Humor as the Acceptable Form of Complaint

**The rule.** Humor is how you lodge a hard truth or a complaint the reader will actually accept. Take the thing that, said straight, makes you a whiner, and turn it so the reader laughs in recognition instead of bracing.

**Source.** Anne: "humor is always about the absurdity of the difficulty of life" (00:44:36). The mechanism, almost verbatim: "if you just complain as a straight complaint... it's unacceptable. You're a whiner. You're a complainer. But if you do it as humor, it's 100% acceptable" (00:45:06). Her example is Robin Williams "riffing wildly through all the complaints of civilized life and turning the complaint into humor, which... we can accept through humor and we don't accept... presented straight ahead" (00:45:21). And the stakes she puts on it: "laughter is carbonated holiness... when you're laughing with somebody, it's holy" (00:43:02); getting a reader to laugh out loud is "the jackpot" (00:42:34).

**Why it works.** This is the move neither skill taught as a *technique*. `lamott-craft` only warned against funny-for-its-own-sake. But humor is generative: it is the socially licensed channel for the true, difficult, complaining thing the reader would defend against if you said it plain. It also does the harmony work of the music model — laughing *with* someone is the deepest reader-relationship move available. It is contact, not performance.

**How to apply it.**
- Find the real complaint under the piece — the thing about how hard this is that, said straight, would read as whining.
- Don't cut the complaint. Find the angle where its absurdity is visible. The humor lives in the gap between how it feels and how ridiculous the situation actually is.
- The truth still has to be true. Humor that isn't carrying a real complaint is just decoration — and that decoration is exactly what Anne kills: "I put in a lot of stuff that is attempting to be funny and I take it out. I kill my little darlings" (00:43:18). Keep only the funny that is "really genuine... a funny way of looking at this that you might not have thought about before" (00:43:34).
- Distinguish the two: humor that *carries* the hard thing = keep. Humor that *covers* for the absence of a hard thing = cut.

**Failure mode.** Funny as a weapon or a shield — funny to look clever, funny to avoid the real complaint. That reads as performance and the reader feels it. The funny has to be the complaint, told sideways.

**The bridge.** Humor-as-complaint is the gentlest entrance to the Hard-Stuff Gate (Rule 33). The complaint under the joke is often the hard thing the piece is avoiding. Make it funny, then ask whether the straight version belongs too.

## Mechanic 3 — Mastery Is the Effort Going Invisible (The River Guide)

**The rule.** The end state of craft is not virtuosity. It is the work becoming effortless enough that the technique disappears and you can spend your attention on the world and the reader instead of on your own paddling.

**Source.** Anne, in the opening and again at 00:22:33: "the point of mastery isn't virtuosity. The point of mastery is there comes a time when you don't have to try as hard anymore... you can just sort of sit back and ease into it and let it flow on its own" — and then use the mastery "as a groove in which you can center your view of the outside world." The metaphor: "a good river guide knows the river so well that you barely see him or her working the paddle... they're not even bothering to watch the currents anymore... What are they watching? The otter playing on the banks. Nothing to do with their mastery" (00:23:05). The freedom this buys: once she accepted her own voice, "then I got to use writing to write about anything I wanted to" (00:23:49).

**Why it works.** This skill already had the reader-side of effortlessness — "the reader can move sentence to sentence without forced effort," "you forget you're reading." What was missing is the *writer-craft thesis*: true mastery reads as effortless flow because the writer is no longer fighting the craft, and that freed attention is what they spend on contact. A draft that still shows the writer working — the visible virtuosity, the strain to impress — is, by this definition, *less* mastered, not more. Effort that shows is effort that hasn't finished.

**How to apply it.**
- Diagnostic question for any draft: *where can you see the writer working the paddle?* The visible effort — the showpiece sentence, the strained metaphor, the over-engineered transition — marks where mastery hasn't arrived.
- Effortlessness is an outcome of the other mechanics, not a separate trick: economize the strain out, replace the laboring verb, let the question stand, kill the darling that announces itself. What remains is flow.
- The point of getting good is not getting better at being good. It is being free to look at the otter — to put the attention on the subject and the reader. If the draft is still about the writer's skill, the paddle is still showing.

**Failure mode.** Confusing effortless with careless, or effortless with showing off ease. The river guide is working *hard*; the work is just invisible. Flow is earned by the cut and the practice, never by writing fast and skipping the revision.

## Mechanic 4 — The Numbered Rules (Verbatim-Sourced)

The source's named rules. Use these citations when a numbered reference earns its keep; otherwise teach the principle plainly.

### Rule 6 — Prefer Anglo-Saxon words / the two-stream model

**The rule.** Favor shorter, punchier Anglo-Saxon words over fancy abstract Latinate words.

**Source.** "Certainly rule six is prefer Anglo-Saxon words... favor shorter, punchier Anglo-Saxon words over fancy abstract Latinate words" (00:35:43). The model underneath: English uniquely has *two streams* — a northern stream of Scandinavian/Germanic (Anglo-Saxon) words, and a southern stream of Italian/Spanish/French cognates (00:36:17). "A whole lot of the multisyllabic boring words are coming up from the south, and a whole lot of the crisp, brutal, get-you-right-into-the-scene words are coming in from the north" (00:37:04). Neal traces his whole instinct to "my love of the Anglo-Saxon directness" (00:37:31). The first rule he ever picked up, from an editor, was adjacent: "write with your verbs... if you use trudged instead of walking, you pull the reader in better" (00:03:03).

**Why it works / how to apply it.** Plain-force language already exists in this skill as a repair path. What's additive is the *named two-stream model* — a fast diagnostic the writer can run on their own draft. Circle the multisyllabic words; ask which stream each came from. The southern (Latinate) ones are usually the abstractions that floated the meaning away from the body. Swap to the northern (Anglo-Saxon) word and the sentence gets you "right into the scene." This is the same axis as `lamott-craft`'s nickel-vs-quarter words, named one level deeper.

### Rule 33 — Write the hard stuff

**The rule.** "Write the hard stuff. Don't shy from the big mysteries of life" (00:46:22).

**Source & note.** This is the named rule behind the Hard-Stuff Gate already taught in `genius.md`. Worth keeping the citation because the *editing demonstration* around it is itself a lesson: Neal points out that Anne's own phrasing — "don't shy *away* from the big mysteries of life" — carried a dead word: "you knew the word *away* was completely unnecessary and you dropped it. Economized" (00:46:36). The rule about writing the hard thing and the rule about economizing meet in a single edit. The full method lives in `genius.md` ("The Hard Stuff Is Where The Piece Starts To Matter") and the `/really-real-oh-no` workflow.

### Rule 36 — Worship talented editors (the sandwich, the rubric)

**The rule.** "Worship talented editors. Writing is collaborative and editors save your skin" (00:52:37). The final rule in the book.

**Source.** Two operational pieces sit under it, both verbatim-recoverable:

*The trust rubric.* How you tell a talented editor from a bad one: "I developed a rubric that editors are going to insert things... three improvements for every mistake" (00:54:07). A trustworthy editor's changes net out as improvements at roughly that ratio; if they don't, the editor isn't yours.

*The sandwich.* "You still have to do the sandwich... if you're going to criticize something, you've got to start with something that's praise" (00:56:39). The exact shape Neal hands Anne: "You've really got it. You've really captured it. The structure is perfect. I just have a few quibbles about some things. And you'll notice it looks all marked up, but it's all minor stuff" — then close with "the world needs this," usually the word *brilliant* (00:56:51 → 00:57:12). Praise → honest diagnosis → reassurance/praise. And the closing truth under the whole rule: "you don't have to do it alone... you need a fresh set of eyes on it... someone you trust who respects your work" (00:57:38).

**Why it works / how to apply it.** The Blind-Spot Edit (Pattern 12) and `lamott-craft`'s sandwich method already carry the content. What's additive here is the *number*, the *3:1 improvement rubric* as a way to vet a reviewer, and the verbatim sandwich script. Use the rubric to decide whose notes to take. Use the sandwich whenever you deliver notes — including in client work and council critique. The deep frame: the editor rule is really a trust rule. A good outside eye improves the work, reveals what you can't see from inside it, and shows care for the piece's existence — without taking ownership away from the writer.

## Voice as Music — The Beatles Model

*(`/depth-voice` consumes this section directly. The diagnostic form — melody / rhythm / harmony — lives in `genius.md` (Music: Melody, Rhythm, Harmony section) and `references/genius-patterns.md` Pattern 7; this section adds the Beatles typology so a writer can name their own dominant center.)*

**The frame.** "Writing relies on the integration of melody, rhythm, and harmony, and... we're adept at all three but usually one is [dominant]" (00:14:00). The three map to three centers (00:14:44):

- **Melody = the head.** Curiosity, the bright stuff, metaphor, "leaps of interest." The fresh leap that makes a reader lean in.
- **Rhythm = the body.** "Wanting to dance." Breath, pace — and crucially "not the rhythms of iambic pentameter, these are the rhythms of conversation" (00:18:56). Conversational motion.
- **Harmony = the heart.** "Being relational and adding voices" (00:15:08) — connecting with the reader, the relationship, warmth. The reader feeling accompanied.

**Find your own Beatle (00:16:07 → 00:18:31).** The point is to notice your strength and build on it instead of straining to be a writer you aren't.

- **Paul McCartney — the pure melodist.** "No better melodist in our generation... a pure melodist leaping intervals." Invents melody so fast a song is complete in 18 minutes. The writer who drops metaphor after metaphor. (Neal names Anne as this: "an incredible melodist... Annie can just drop one metaphor after another after another," 00:19:06.)
- **John Lennon — the rhythmist.** A narrow vocal range, "doesn't think in melody, John thinks in rhythm." Ringo calls him "the greatest rhythm guitarist who ever lived." The group follows *his* rhythm; he stretches and bends the beat. The conversationally rhythmic writer. (Neal names himself this: "I'm a fundamentally rhythmic writer... I don't have to read my sentences aloud because... I can be conversationally rhythmic," 00:18:37.)
- **Ringo Starr — the rhythmic melodist.** A drummer whose trick is that he's "never following the bass player" — in jazz everyone follows the bass; Ringo follows *the voice*, "the melody all the time," listening to whoever's singing (00:17:39). The writer whose rhythm is shaped by, and serves, the meaning being sung — rhythm in service of melody.
- **George Harrison — the harmonist (harmonic-rhythmic).** "An okay melodist... but he's thinking first of harmony because that's who he is, this spiritual harmonist" (00:18:14). The writer whose primary gift is the relationship and the warmth — the voices added underneath.

**Why knowing your type matters.** Neal, asked directly: "so you don't get hung up on trying to be something you aren't. John was okay with not being as good a melodist as Paul. It didn't stop him from producing music we all listen to just as much" (00:19:34). Anne: "it's very important to know what you're good at and what you love to do with writing" (00:19:50).

**How `/depth-voice` should use it.**
1. **Diagnose the writer's dominant center.** Read a sample. Where is the gift — the leaps (melodist), the conversational motion (rhythmist), the meaning-led cadence (rhythmic melodist), or the warmth and relationship (harmonist)?
2. **Build on the strength, don't homogenize.** A melodist shouldn't be flattened into a metronomic rhythmist. Strengthen what's already strong.
3. **Add only the missing layer.** Most drafts are strong in one register and weak in another — a clever (melody) draft with no warmth (harmony); a sincere (harmony) draft with no leap. Find the single missing center and add just enough for the piece to come alive, without forcing all three equally.
4. **A self-aware writer can skip checks others need.** Lennon (the rhythmist) doesn't read aloud to fix rhythm because rhythm is what he's mastered. Know your type, then know which revision passes you can trust your ear on and which you have to run by hand.

This is the more humane rubric: a writer diagnoses by naming the missing musical layer, not by global self-criticism about whether the writing is "good."
